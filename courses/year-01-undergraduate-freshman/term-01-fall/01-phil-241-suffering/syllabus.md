## Course description

Examines the philosophical and theological problem of suffering and evil through
major Christian and philosophical treatments of the problem of pain.

## Independent curriculum purpose

The course equips a beginning undergraduate student to distinguish forms of the
problem of evil, reconstruct major arguments, compare important Christian
responses, interpret representative biblical material, and form a reasoned
confessional synthesis. It joins philosophical rigor to pastoral restraint:
arguments must be evaluated carefully, but suffering persons must not be reduced
to examples or pressured to suppress lament.

Because the reconciled curriculum places PHIL 101 concurrently rather than as a
prerequisite, the first part of PHIL 241 explicitly scaffolds vocabulary,
premise-conclusion reconstruction, validity, soundness, and charitable objection.

## Learning outcomes

On successful completion, the student will be able to:

1. **LO-01:** Distinguish and correctly use the central concepts, argument forms,
   and methodological categories in philosophical and theological treatments of
   suffering and evil.
2. **LO-02:** Reconstruct and evaluate major logical and evidential arguments from
   evil in premise-conclusion form without caricaturing their proponents.
3. **LO-03:** Compare leading Christian defenses and theodicies, including their
   claims, limits, and principal objections.
4. **LO-04:** Interpret representative biblical texts and canonical themes
   concerning suffering, evil, lament, providence, judgment, and hope using the
   ESV and NASB 2020 responsibly.
5. **LO-05:** Present major non-Christian, skeptical, and intra-Christian
   perspectives accurately and charitably before offering confessional evaluation.
6. **LO-06:** Formulate and defend a coherent broadly evangelical Protestant
   response to the problem of evil that integrates philosophical reasoning,
   Scripture, and historical theology while acknowledging unresolved objections.
7. **LO-07:** Differentiate philosophical explanation from pastoral response and
   apply trauma-aware, non-coercive, and appropriately bounded judgment to a case
   involving suffering.

## Instructional pattern

Weeks 1–14 normally contain two substantial written lectures, four hours of
required source study, guided argument or interpretive practice, and staged
assessment work. Week 15 contains no new instruction and is reserved for final
synthesis and the cumulative final examination. Videos supplement the written
course; they do not replace it.

The sequence advances from concepts and Scripture through historical treatments,
logical and evidential arguments, defenses and theodicies, comparative
perspectives, and confessional-pastoral synthesis. See
[schedule.md](schedule.md).

| A-06 | Oral defense dialogue | 10% | 5 hours |
| A-07 | Final integrative examination | 25% | 18 hours |
|  | **Total** | **100%** | **54 hours** |

Assessment modes intentionally include open-book writing, closed-book timed
retrieval and examination, take-home analysis, and recorded oral argument. The
final assessment is a cumulative, time-limited take-home examination. Its 18
hours include 6 hours of preparation during weeks 12–14 and 12 hours during week
15. No exam questions or assignment prompts are included at the design stage.
