# Week 3 Guided Discussion — A Historical Disputation

This is 60 minutes of ungraded oral practice. Its purpose is to represent
Augustine, Aquinas, Leibniz, and the speakers in Hume's dialogue accurately
before evaluating them. No consensus or personal disclosure is required.

If no group is available, record each timed role privately, listen once as the
source auditor, and complete the same revision. The recording is not submitted.

## Time plan

| Stage | Time |
|---|---:|
| Source preparation | 0.17 h |
| Ontology and causation exchange | 0.25 h |
| Worlds and evidence exchange | 0.25 h |
| Canonical audit and revision | 0.33 h |
| **Total** | **1.00 h** |

## 1. Source preparation — 10 minutes

Assign four functions. In groups of fewer than four, combine adjacent roles.

- **Augustine–Aquinas advocate:** prepares privation, created goodness,
  deficient will, accidental causation, fault, and penalty.
- **Leibniz advocate:** prepares possible worlds, compossibility, whole-world
  value, creaturely contingency, and permission.
- **Hume dialogue analyst:** prepares Philo's four circumstances and the
  distinction between compatibility and evidential support, while tracking
  Cleanthes's reply.
- **Source and canonical auditor:** checks attribution, identifies whether a
  statement is ontological, causal, or evidential, and compares it with Week 2
  Scripture notes.

Each role writes one claim, one reason, one serious objection, and one source
location. The auditor prepares a correction signal: “That may be defensible,
but the assigned source does not yet establish it.”

## 2. Ontology and causation exchange — 15 minutes

Consider a fictional case in which a public official freely diverts emergency
funds, leaving a town less prepared for a severe storm. Discuss the argument,
not the experience of any real victim.

1. The Augustine–Aquinas advocate explains why the wrong action and its damage
   are real even if evil is not an independent substance.
2. The Hume dialogue analyst asks whether describing evil as privation answers
   the moral question of divine permission.
3. The advocate distinguishes the official's direct intention, harms that
   follow, the defect in the voluntary act, and any claim about providence.
4. The Leibniz advocate asks whether the account explains only the creaturely
   cause or also the creator's permission.
5. The auditor pauses the exchange whenever ontology, causal attribution, and
   moral justification are conflated.

Before moving on, each advocate must state the strongest version of an
opponent's objection and receive that role's confirmation that it is fair.

## 3. Worlds and evidence exchange — 15 minutes

Use these two questions:

> If God wisely chooses an unsurpassed complete world, what follows about the
> relation between a local evil and the value of the whole?

> If our evidence is a world containing both goods and severe evils, what may
> an observer infer from that evidence about the cause's perfect goodness?

The Leibniz advocate gives a three-minute answer. The Hume dialogue analyst
then gives a three-minute response in Philo's voice and a one-minute
Cleanthes-style caution against claims that outrun observed effects. The
Augustine–Aquinas advocate identifies any assumption about evil's ontological
status or creaturely causation. The auditor labels each move as a claim about
**compatibility**, **actual explanation**, or **evidential support**.

Reverse the order for a second round. No one may dismiss Hume as merely hostile
to faith or treat Leibniz as saying that every evil is good when considered by
itself.

## 4. Canonical audit and revision — 20 minutes

The auditor asks the group to test three proposed conclusions:

1. “Because Genesis calls creation good, every existing arrangement must be
   the best possible arrangement.”
2. “Because Romans 8 places suffering within providence and hope, Christians
   know why each particular evil was permitted.”
3. “Because biblical lament protests evil before God, Christian faith need not
   pretend that an ontological analysis removes grief or moral outrage.”

For each, identify whether the Week 2 sources support, qualify, or fail to
establish it. Cite the passage rather than appealing to a general biblical
impression.

End by drafting a joint four-sentence report:

1. the strongest Augustine–Aquinas contribution;
2. the strongest Leibnizian contribution;
3. the strongest Humean pressure;
4. one question a broadly evangelical Protestant evaluation must still answer.

Each participant then privately revises one sentence to remove a
misattribution, overclaim, or false dilemma noticed during the exchange.
