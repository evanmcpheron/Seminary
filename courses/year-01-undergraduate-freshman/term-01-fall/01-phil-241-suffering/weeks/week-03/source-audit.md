## Augustine claim audit

- The anthology's chapter 13 begins with *City of God* XI.17 and presents evil
  as vice contrary to a good created nature, with the origin of wickedness in
  will rather than the Creator.
- Public primary-text cross-checking in *City of God* XI.17 and XII.1, 3
  confirms the lecture's claims that good and bad rational creatures share a
  created nature, vice damages a good subject, and voluntary departure rather
  than an independently evil substance accounts for moral wickedness.
- The lecture states the privation account as an ontology, not a complete
  explanation of divine permission, natural suffering, distribution, or a
  particular loss.
- The denial-of-genuine-evil objection is presented in a strong form. The reply
  says deprivation can be devastating because the absent good is real and due;
  it does not claim the objection settles all further theodicy questions.

## Aquinas claim audit

- The anthology's chapter 4 and *Summa Theologiae* I, q. 49 agree on the three
  article questions: whether good causes evil, whether God causes evil, and
  whether a supreme evil causes all evils.
- The lecture accurately distinguishes causation by accident, positive action,
  a defect in a secondary cause, fault, penalty, and the absence of an
  independently causal supreme evil.
- The lecture does not present an opening objection in the disputed-question
  form as Aquinas's conclusion.
- Aquinas's attribution of penalty and natural corruption is reported rather
  than silently endorsed. The lecture explicitly preserves the moral question
  about divine permission and uses Job to block case-specific punishment
  diagnoses.
- Aquinas's use of the book of Wisdom is identified as historical evidence; the
  curriculum's 66-book Protestant canon remains controlling.

## Lewis chapters 4–5 claim audit

- Chapter 4 supports the lecture's account of concrete guilt, critique of
  reducing virtue to effortless kindness, warning against evasion through
  corporate guilt, denial of total depravity, and distinction between
  intellectual recognition and cultivated gloom.
- The lecture treats potentially harmful uses of shame as its own critical and
  pastoral evaluation rather than attributing them to Lewis.
- Chapter 5 supports Lewis's stated use of the fall against monism and dualism,
  his appeal to abused creaturely freedom, his rejection of a simple
  remote-ancestor punishment account, and his explicit presentation of the
  human-origins picture as conjectural.
- No speculative anthropology is represented as established science, biblical
  exegesis, or required confessional detail.

## Leibniz claim audit

- The anthology's chapter 5 record verifies the objection, Leibniz's denial of
  its minor premise, and the prosyllogism about avoidable evil and failure to
  choose the best.
- The public *Theodicy*, especially §§7–10, 29–31, and 52–54, confirms the
  lecture's use of divine perfection, possible worlds, whole-world connection,
  best-world choice, the laden-boat illustration, positive being and privation,
  and contingency under divine knowledge and decree.
- The lecture does not attribute to Leibniz the later vocabulary of a
  “local-improvement fallacy” or the lecture's person-centered objections.
- The view is not caricatured as denying evil or claiming that each evil is
  visibly beneficial. Its disputed assumptions about a maximal world,
  comparability, compossibility, and total-value reasoning are clearly marked.

## Hume claim audit

- The anthology's chapter 6 title, authorship, chapter location, and relation to
  *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* are confirmed by the JSTOR record.
- Parts 10–11 of the public primary text confirm the distinct roles of Demea,
  Cleanthes, and Philo; Philo's four circumstances; the pressure on an inference
  to unlimited moral attributes; and the distinction between possible
  compatibility and empirical support.
- The lectures attribute arguments to dialogue characters and mark the relation
  between Philo and Hume as contested rather than treating every line as direct
  authorial assertion.
- Philo's claim about mixed phenomena is presented as an evidential argument,
  not a deductive demonstration of atheism. Relevant Leibnizian and
  revealed-theology replies are stated before confessional evaluation.

## Claim-label, Scripture, and theological audit

- Direct reports are marked as primary-source claims; later classifications,
  objections, and pedagogical formulations are labeled as contested evaluation,
  confessional conclusion, or curriculum synthesis where confusion is likely.
- The lectures distinguish ontology, causation, responsibility, compatibility,
  evidence, and theodicy rather than making one historical claim answer every
  question.
- Genesis 1–3, Job, the lament psalms, Romans 8, and Revelation 21–22 are used as
  the already approved canonical framework. No new mandatory Scripture reading
  or workload is added.
- The confessional evaluation follows charitable exposition and identifies
  itself as independent curriculum judgment, not official Wheaton material.

## Pastoral, student-safety, and copyright audit

- No personal disclosure is required and no private case is introduced.
- The lectures reject victim-blaming, case-specific hidden-purpose claims,
  coerced shame, passive acceptance of preventable harm, and the use of
  providence to erase creaturely accountability.
- The lectures contain original synthesis, short argument reconstructions,
  source locations, and paraphrase. They reproduce no extended passage from a
  copyrighted Bible edition, Peterson anthology, or Lewis edition.
- The public-domain primary texts were used for content cross-checking; no full
  text or substantial excerpt is stored in the repository.
