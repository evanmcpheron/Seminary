# Augustine and Aquinas on the Nature and Cause of Evil

## Learning objectives

After completing this lecture, the student should be able to:

- distinguish questions about the nature, cause, and evidential force of evil;
- explain Augustine's privation account in its anti-dualist setting;
- reconstruct Aquinas's distinctions among direct cause, accidental cause,
  deficient secondary cause, fault, and penalty;
- compare both positions with Lewis's account of wickedness and the fall; and
- test the historical accounts against Scripture without turning them into an
  explanation of a particular tragedy.

## Key terms

**Nature:** a thing considered according to what it is and the capacities proper
to it. **Privation:** the lack of a good that ought to be present in a subject
capable of that good. **Corruption:** the damaging or loss of a due good in an
existing nature. **Vice:** a defective disposition or act contrary to a nature's
proper good. **Dualism:** here, the view that good and evil arise from opposed,
independent ultimate principles. **Accidental cause:** a cause of an effect it
does not intend as such but that follows from what it does intend. **Secondary
cause:** a created agent or process through which an effect occurs. **Fault:**
evil involving defective voluntary action and culpability. **Penalty:** a loss
or suffering considered under an order of judgment. **Theodicy:** an account
that proposes God's actual justifying reasons for permitting evil.

Read Augustine, “A Good Creation's Capacity for Evil,” and Aquinas, “No Evil

## Lecture

### 1. Do not ask one theory to answer four questions

Historical study can become a museum tour of names. It can also become a search
for one sentence that supposedly solves suffering. Both approaches miss the
arguments. Augustine and Aquinas help because they slow the inquiry down.
Before asking whether evil disproves God, they ask what evil is and how a good
reality can be involved in causing it.

At least four questions must remain distinct:

1. **Ontology:** What kind of reality is evil? Is it a substance, force,
   quality, relation, absence, or disorder?
2. **Causation:** Through what agents or processes does an evil occur? What is
   intended, permitted, defective, or merely consequent?
3. **Moral responsibility:** Which agents are culpable, and why?
4. **Evidence and justification:** What does the occurrence and distribution of
   evil count for or against, and could a perfectly good God have sufficient
   reason to permit it?

An ontological answer is not automatically a theodicy. Saying what blindness
*is* does not explain why this person is blind, who is responsible for a
preventable injury, or why God permits it. Likewise, identifying a human
perpetrator does not by itself settle the divine-permission question. The
distinctions do not make the problem disappear; they show which work remains.

**Curriculum synthesis:** Good argument assigns each claim to the question it
actually answers. Week 3 therefore studies Augustine and Aquinas neither as
automatic victors nor as obsolete curiosities. Their conceptual grammar will be
reconstructed first, then exposed to its strongest relevant pressures.

### 2. Augustine: good creation against dualism

Augustine writes with a live alternative in view. Manichaean teaching located
good and evil within opposed ultimate principles. Augustine's Christian
account rejects that cosmic dualism. God alone is the uncreated source of all
that exists; therefore created natures, insofar as they exist, are good. Even a
wicked spiritual creature is not an evil substance created by an evil god. Its
nature is good as creature; its will is vicious in departing from the good to
which it ought to adhere.[^1]

**Primary-source claim:** In the assigned selection, Augustine reasons from
vice to prior goodness. A defect can injure only something that has a good to
lose. Blindness is harmful because sight belongs to the good of an eye; a
wicked will is harmful because rational nature was fitted to adhere to God. If
a being lost every good, it would no longer remain a being available for
corruption.[^2]

That argument can be set out as follows:

1. Every created nature is good insofar as it has being and the goods proper to
   its kind.
2. Evil damages, disorders, or removes some good that ought to be present.
3. Such damage presupposes a good subject capable of being damaged.
4. Therefore evil is not a rival nature or substance; it is a privation or
   corruption within a good created nature.

The conclusion is metaphysical. It denies that evil has independent creative
power. It does not deny that a corrupted will acts, that a wound hurts, or that
the loss of sight matters. The agent, tissue, desire, intelligence, or social
institution that carries the defect remains real; the disorder in it is
parasitic upon goods it bends or destroys.

This is why *privation* is more precise than mere *absence*. A stone lacks sight,
but the stone is not blind, because sight is not due to a stone. An eye or
sighted animal can be blind because it is the kind of subject in which sight is
a proper good. Moral evil is similarly not the bare nonoccurrence of every
possible kindness. It concerns a will's failure to love, choose, or act under
the good order appropriate to a rational creature.

### 3. Corrupt will and the origin of moral evil

Augustine's privation analysis joins an account of will. Good and bad rational
creatures do not require different creators or different basic natures. The
difference arises when a will turns from the common and higher good toward a
private good as though the creature were its own final measure. The good sought
may be real—power, pleasure, security, recognition—but the mode and order of
seeking become defective.

**Primary-source claim:** Augustine locates wickedness not in the created nature
as created, but in voluntary departure from God. The departure injures the
creature rather than God. Divine rule can order a wicked will within justice
without converting the wickedness itself into a good.[^2]

This claim guards two convictions at once. First, God is not the maker of an
evil substance. Second, creaturely agency is not erased. A thief cannot excuse
theft by saying that the powers of movement, intelligence, and desire involved
are gifts from God. Those powers are goods; their defective ordering belongs to
the creature's act.

The distinction also clarifies the relation between evil and good outcomes.
Augustine can hold that God draws good from an evil will without saying the
will's defect becomes good. A judge may restrain a wrongdoer, a community may
repair an injustice, and a victim may display courage; none of those goods
changes the original cruelty into a good deed. An ordered response is not a
retrospective renaming of the wrong.

**Confessional conclusion:** Genesis 1–3 supports the architecture of created
goodness, creaturely limit, disobedience, and disorder. It does not give a
complete philosophical statement of privation, so the Augustinian account
should be identified as theological reasoning from Scripture rather than the
verbatim teaching of one verse. Job further prevents the account from becoming
a diagnostic rule: moral evil is real, but severe suffering does not prove a
severe hidden sin in the sufferer.

### 4. The denial-of-genuine-evil objection

A serious critic may hear “evil is a privation” as “evil is nothing,” then hear
that as “nothing bad happened.” That would be both a logical mistake and a
pastoral disaster. The theory says that evil has no independent substance. It
does not say that the deprived good is unimportant or the damage slight.
Starvation is a lack of nourishment in a living body, betrayal is a lack of
fidelity in a relationship, and injustice is a defect in the treatment owed to
persons. Each can devastate precisely because the absent or damaged good is so
valuable.

Yet correcting that misunderstanding does not remove every objection. The
critic can still ask:

- Why would a good creator permit natures vulnerable to catastrophic
  deprivation?
- Does placing moral evil in created wills address disease, predation,
  earthquake, or involuntary mental anguish?
- If God can order evil to good, why is there this amount and distribution of
  evil rather than less?
- Does talk of a total order risk sacrificing the good of particular persons to
  an aesthetically satisfying whole?

These questions do not refute privation as an ontology. They show that an
ontology is not yet a morally sufficient explanation. Augustine's claims about
divine permission, justice, and the ordering of evil extend beyond the
privation definition and must bear their own argumentative burden.

**Contested evaluation:** The privation view can preserve the radical wrongness
of evil by describing it as destruction of a due good. Critics can nevertheless
argue that it does too little to explain divine permission or the experience of
apparently pointless suffering. A fair evaluation should grant both points.

### 5. Aquinas asks how good can cause evil

The assigned Aquinas selection begins not with a continuous essay but with the
form of a disputed question. Aquinas states objections, presents a contrary
authority, gives his answer, and replies to objections. The reader must not
mistake an objection at the beginning of an article for Aquinas's conclusion.
The selection asks three questions: whether good can cause evil; whether God,
the supreme good, causes evil; and whether there is a supreme evil that causes
all evils.[^3]

Aquinas inherits the privation framework. Because every active cause acts
insofar as it has actuality and some good, evil cannot function as an
independent positive source. Yet he refuses the simplistic conclusion that good
can never be involved in an evil effect. Causal description needs more
precision.

**Primary-source claim:** Aquinas says that good can cause evil *by accident*.
An agent may intend a form or good whose production entails the loss of another
form; or an action may fail because a secondary cause is deficient. The evil is
not intended under the description of evil, but it occurs through the action
and its defective reception or execution.[^4]

Consider a crafted example. A surgeon may intentionally remove damaged tissue
to preserve a life. The tissue's destruction is real, and the surgeon directly
causes it physically, but the loss is intended under the good of healing rather
than for its own sake. That example concerns a deliberate lesser harm. Another
kind of case involves defect: a worker attempts a good construction, but a
damaged tool produces a crooked cut. The productive power explains the cut;
the tool's defect explains the crookedness. Neither example proves that any
actual suffering is justified. They display different structures of causation.

### 6. God, secondary causes, fault, and penalty

Aquinas then applies the distinctions to divine causation. God causes and
sustains whatever being and actuality a creature has. When a created agent
performs a sinful act, the act has positive features—power, movement,
intelligence, effects—that depend on God as first cause. The culpable defect,
however, belongs to the disordered secondary cause. Aquinas's image is a limp:
the power of movement explains that walking occurs, while the damaged limb
explains its irregularity.[^4]

**Primary-source claim:** Aquinas therefore denies that the defect of a sinful
act is reduced to God as its cause. He also distinguishes the evil of *fault*
from the evil of *penalty*. He attributes the defect of fault to the deficient
created agent, while locating penalty under divine justice and treating natural
corruption as consequent upon God's willing the order of created reality rather
than corruption for its own sake.[^5]

This is a strong causal proposal, but it raises immediate questions. If God
creates, sustains, knows, and permits the secondary cause, does locating the
defect in that cause adequately address divine responsibility? When a human
authority knowingly permits preventable harm, “I did not directly intend it”
may be morally insufficient. The theist must explain why divine permission is
wise and good, not merely why creaturely fault is not a positive object God
creates.

Aquinas's distinction between fault and penalty also demands restraint. A
general doctrine of judgment cannot be used to identify a particular person's
pain as divine punishment. Job's friends offer exactly that sort of inference,
and the book's narrative controls reject it. Moreover, harms done by human
agents remain subject to human justice and prevention. Divine providence does
not turn a perpetrator into a passive instrument or remove obligations to
protect a neighbor.

**Historical-theological note:** Aquinas cites biblical texts through the
scriptural and translational conventions available to him and also cites the
book of Wisdom. This curriculum studies that use as historical evidence while
retaining its commitment to the 66-book Protestant canon. Historical accuracy
does not require treating every cited authority as canonical within this
course.

### 7. Why there can be no supreme evil

The third Aquinas article opposes a supreme evil principle. A cause acts only
insofar as it has being, power, and actuality—all goods under his metaphysics.
A supposedly unqualified evil would lack every good and therefore lack the
being and power required to cause anything. Actual evils instead exist as
defects in good subjects and actions.[^4]

This conclusion reinforces Augustine's anti-dualism. Evil can be pervasive and
powerful without being an equal creator. A corrupt institution possesses real
people, offices, skills, money, language, and coordination; its injustice
misdirects those goods. The analysis may even sharpen moral criticism by
showing how many goods are damaged or commandeered.

Still, the conclusion does not yield a scale for comparing worlds or show why
God permits all actual evils. It defeats one proposed ontology—a supreme evil
substance or source—rather than every argument from evil. Hume's later
challenge, studied in Lecture 2, will ask what the mixed character of the world
allows an observer to infer about its cause.

### 8. Lewis on wickedness and the fall

Lewis translates parts of this tradition into a modern apologetic setting.
Chapter 4 argues that Christianity's promise of forgiveness and healing makes
sense only when persons recognize concrete moral guilt. He criticizes the
reduction of goodness to effortless niceness, warns against using corporate
guilt to evade personal wrongdoing, and tries to expose the distance between
public respectability and inward motive.[^6]

**Primary-source claim:** Lewis explicitly denies that he is reinstating total
depravity. He appeals to observable goods in human nature and says his intended
effect is intellectual recognition, not the cultivation of continuous sadness.
He links repentance to concrete amendment and assistance rather than vague
self-condemnation.[^6]

That qualification matters. Language about shame can expose self-deception, but
it can also be used coercively against people already burdened by abuse,
scrupulosity, depression, or false accusation. The chapter's claim should be
limited to accountable recognition of one's own wrongs. It gives no license to
assign guilt to a sufferer, demand disclosure, or treat emotional distress as
evidence of rebellion.

Chapter 5 states what Lewis considers the doctrine of the fall's central
function: it guards the goodness of God against monism, which makes God the
undifferentiated source of good and evil, and dualism, which posits an
independent evil power. God makes good creatures, including rational freedom;
creatures misuse that good and become corrupt.[^7]

**Primary-source claim:** Lewis rejects the claim that the doctrine simply
proves it just to punish individuals for a remote ancestor's fault. He also
distinguishes the doctrinal claim from an imaginative picture of how human
origins and a first self-centered choice might relate to evolutionary history.
He labels that picture conjectural and declines to present it as demonstrated
science or complete exegesis.[^7]

Lewis's candor supplies a reading rule: do not promote admitted speculation to
dogma. His durable philosophical point is that freedom capable of rational love
also includes the possibility of self-centering refusal, and that stable
consequences allow creaturely choices to matter. Whether his particular model
accounts for natural history, inherited corruption, or the scale of suffering
requires arguments beyond the chapter.

### 9. Canonical and confessional synthesis

Week 2 supplies six controls for this historical material.

1. **Good creation:** Genesis 1–2 fits Augustine's and Aquinas's denial that
   created being or embodiment is evil as such.
2. **Creaturely rebellion:** Genesis 3 supports locating moral disorder in
   creaturely mistrust and disobedience, while not spelling out the full
   metaphysics of privation.
3. **Lament:** Psalms 22, 73, and 88 prevent “God orders evil” from becoming a
   ban on protest or a demand for immediate resolution.
4. **Limits on diagnosis:** Job blocks the inference from severe suffering to
   severe personal guilt.
5. **Providence and hope:** Romans 8 situates groaning within divine action and
   Christ-shaped hope without calling every event good.
6. **Judgment and renewal:** Revelation 21–22 depicts evil judged and overcome,
   not merely redescribed as a necessary feature of an aesthetically pleasing
   whole.[^8]

**Confessional conclusion:** This curriculum affirms one good Creator and
rejects an independent evil principle. It receives evil as a real corruption of
created good, affirms responsible creaturely agency, and confesses divine
providence and judgment. Augustine and Aquinas offer valuable metaphysical and
causal distinctions consistent with those commitments.

**Curriculum synthesis:** Those distinctions do not reveal God's reason for a
particular loss, prove that every instance of pain is punishment, or remove the
evidential problem. The Christian response must retain lament, protection of
the vulnerable, accountability for perpetrators, and hope for evil's defeat.
What God can bring from evil must never be confused with calling the evil good.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Augustinian privation:** evil is corruption of a due good in a good created
  subject, not a coequal substance. The view addresses ontology and moral
  disorder but requires further account of permission and natural suffering.
- **Thomistic causal analysis:** good can cause evil accidentally; positive
  action and being depend on good causes, while culpable defect belongs to a
  deficient secondary cause. Critics press the responsibility of a creator who
  knowingly permits defective causes.
- **Lewis's apologetic appropriation:** personal guilt and a creaturely fall
  explain why corrective good is needed. Lewis rejects total depravity and
  labels his anthropological picture speculative.
- **Denial-of-genuine-evil objection:** critics charge that privation minimizes
  harm. Defenders reply that a deprivation can be devastating because the lost
  good is real and due, while conceding that definition alone is not a
  theodicy.

## Confessional analysis

The curriculum receives the good-creation and creaturely-rebellion framework as
consistent with Scripture and historic Christian confession. It evaluates
Augustine, Aquinas, and Lewis appreciatively but does not treat any one of them
as an infallible interpreter. The course rejects dualism, creaturely innocence
about actual wrongdoing, victim-blaming, and the conversion of admitted
speculation into doctrine. These are independent curriculum judgments, not
official Wheaton College teaching.

## Summary

Augustine rejects cosmic dualism by arguing that every created nature is good
insofar as it exists and that evil corrupts a good it does not create. Moral
evil arises through a defective will, while divine ordering of evil does not
make the defect good. Aquinas refines the causal analysis: good may cause evil
by accident, positive action depends on good causes, and the defect of fault
belongs to a deficient secondary cause. His distinctions preserve creaturely
responsibility but leave a live question about divine permission. Lewis adapts
the tradition through concrete guilt and the fall, while denying total
depravity, rejecting inherited punishment as a simple explanation, and marking
his human-origins picture as speculation. Scripture supports good creation and
creaturely rebellion while requiring lament, epistemic restraint, judgment,
and hope.
