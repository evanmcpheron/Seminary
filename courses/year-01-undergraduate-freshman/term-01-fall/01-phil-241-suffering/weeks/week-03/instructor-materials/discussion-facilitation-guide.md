# Instructor Facilitation Guide — Week 3 Historical Disputation

## Purpose and preparation

The exercise evaluates practice, not confessional assent. Require source
attribution before evaluation. Have the four assigned Peterson selections,
Lewis chapters 4–5, Week 2 canonical notes, and the lecture source audit
available.

## Facilitation sequence

1. Confirm that each role has a claim, reason, objection, and location.
2. During the first exchange, write three headings where all can see them:
   **what evil is**, **how an evil effect arises**, and **why permission might be
   justified**. Move each claim under the question it answers.
3. During the second exchange, use separate headings for **logical
   compatibility**, **proposed actual reason**, and **empirical support**.
4. Ask an advocate to restate an opponent's objection before replying. The
   opponent may correct the restatement once.
5. Reserve the final 20 minutes for canonical control and individual revision.

## Source anchors

- Augustine's privation account rejects an independent evil substance while
  affirming that a good nature can be severely corrupted.
- Aquinas distinguishes the positive being of an act from its defect and uses
  direct/accidental causal distinctions. This does not make divine permission
  morally trivial.
- Leibniz denies that imagining a local evil absent is sufficient to establish
  a better complete possible world. Students may challenge the availability of
  a best world, whole-world comparison, or our warrant for applying it.
- Philo's four circumstances and his compatibility/evidence distinction press
  Cleanthes's empirical inference. Dialogue form requires qualified authorial
  attribution.
- The Week 2 biblical material supports good creation, real rebellion and
  lament, providence, judgment, and hope; it does not reveal the reason for a
  particular tragedy or explicitly teach every later philosophical category.

## Corrections to make promptly

- “Privation means evil is unreal.” Ask: unreal as an independent substance, or
  unreal as a loss suffered by a real creature?
- “Aquinas says God has no causal relation to an evil event.” Ask the student to
  distinguish the act's being, the created defect, permission, and judgment.
- “Leibniz says every event is good.” Ask whether his claim concerns isolated
  events or a complete world's order and value.
- “Hume proves God does not exist.” Ask what Philo's actual target is in the
  assigned passage and whether his conclusion concerns contradiction or
  empirical inference.
- “The Bible says this is the best possible world.” Require a passage and
  distinguish textual claim from later theological inference.

## Pastoral and discussion boundaries

The fictional case illustrates causal distinctions; it is not an invitation to
diagnose a real disaster or disclose trauma. Do not require autobiographical
application. Interrupt ridicule of a skeptical or Christian position. If a
real disclosure concerns danger, abuse, or risk of harm, stop the exercise and
follow the course safety and referral policy.

## Completion evidence

A completed session includes mutual confirmation of one charitable
restatement, explicit use of the two sets of distinctions, a four-sentence
joint report, and one private sentence-level revision per participant. Do not
collect the private revision unless course delivery procedures later require
formative documentation.
