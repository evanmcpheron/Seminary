# Instructor Key — Week 3 Retrieval Practice

This key supports ungraded self-correction. Accept substantively equivalent
wording grounded in the assigned sources. Do not distribute before the student
has completed both retrieval and source-check phases.

## Part I

1. **Privation** is the absence of a good that ought to be present in a nature
   capable of that good. The damaged nature and loss are real: blindness, for
   example, is a real deprivation in a real subject even though it is not a
   rival substance.
2. A direct cause produces what it intends as such. An accidental cause
   produces an effect that follows from what it directly causes without
   intending that effect under that description.
3. The act has positive being and powers in operation; the moral defect is its
   failure to conform to the act's due order. In Aquinas's account, God causes
   created action insofar as it has being, while the deficient created will is
   the source of the defect.
4. **Fault** is moral evil in a deficient voluntary act and carries
   culpability. **Penalty** is a deprivation suffered under an order of
   justice. This distinction does not authorize identifying a particular
   sufferer's pain as a divine penalty.
5. A possible world is a complete possible way reality could be. Compossibility
   concerns whether possible beings, goods, choices, and states of affairs can
   obtain together in one complete world.
6. A proposed local alteration may exclude goods or conditions connected to
   the whole. Leibniz therefore evaluates complete compossible orders rather
   than assuming that subtracting one evil while holding everything else fixed
   describes a possible, better world.
7. Demea, Cleanthes, and Philo.
8. Compatibility asks whether the propositions can both be true without
   contradiction. Evidential inference asks whether the observed world gives
   positive reason to attribute perfect goodness to its cause.

## Part II

9. A sound reconstruction should include: every nature, insofar as it exists,
   is good; corruption damages or removes a good proper to such a nature; evil
   therefore depends on good as its subject; thus evil is corruption or
   privation rather than an independent substance. The map should not infer
   that harms are slight.
10. A sound map should include the claim that perfect wisdom and goodness choose
    the best available complete order; God is perfectly wise, good, and
    powerful; therefore God's chosen world is unsurpassed among possible
    worlds. The objection treats actual evil as proof that a better complete
    world was available. Leibniz denies that inference: removing an isolated
    evil does not by itself describe a compossible world better in the whole.
11. Philo's four circumstances are: the use of pain as a motive alongside
    pleasure; government through general laws rather than continual special
    adjustment; a sparse or narrowly sufficient distribution of creaturely
    powers and resources; and the apparent imprecision or poor calibration of
    nature's mechanisms. He argues that mixed effects do not furnish an
    empirical inference to unlimited goodness, even if a prior belief in a good
    deity could seek a compatible explanation.
12. (a) Lewis; (b) Leibniz; (c) Philo, in engagement with Cleanthes's empirical
    method; (d) Augustine/Aquinas. For (d), either is acceptable if the student
    accurately locates and explains the chosen source.

## Diagnostic use

Frequent errors worth revisiting are: treating privation as nonexistence of
harm; making permission identical to direct causation; treating “possible
world” as a second planet; attributing every speech to Hume without
qualification; and confusing a compatibility response with positive
evidential support.
