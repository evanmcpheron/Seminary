# Week 3 Study Guide — Nature, Cause, Worlds, and Evidence

Complete this ungraded guide after the readings and lectures. Work from your
annotations rather than memory alone, and identify the speaker whenever using
Hume's dialogue. Concise phrases are sufficient.

## 1. Source-attribution control — 10 minutes

For each statement, name the source or dialogue character most directly
associated with it. Then record a location in the assigned reading. Some
statements may be accepted by more than one thinker, but choose the most direct
source and note any overlap.

1. Evil is not an independent substance but the corruption of a good nature.
2. Good may cause an evil accidentally while the defect of a voluntary act
   arises from a deficient created cause.
3. A wise and good creator chooses a complete possible world by reference to
   the whole, not by maximizing each local part in isolation.
4. Even if perfect goodness can be reconciled with mixed phenomena, it does not
   follow that the phenomena give empirical support to perfect goodness.
5. Human beings resist the diagnosis of guilt, yet Christian redemption becomes
   unintelligible if guilt is reduced to social embarrassment alone.
6. The fall is doctrinally used to attribute the human predicament to created
   rebellion, while a detailed picture of prehistoric human consciousness is
   offered speculatively.

Add one sentence explaining why “Hume says” can be an inadequate attribution
when reporting a speech by Philo, Cleanthes, or Demea.

## 2. Question-type sort — 10 minutes

Label each question **ontology**, **causation**, **compatibility**, **evidence**,
or **pastoral application**. A question may receive two labels only if you
explain why.

1. Is evil a substance, power, privation, relation, or something else?
2. How can a defective choice arise within a creation called good?
3. Could a perfectly good and powerful God coexist with actual evils?
4. What do actual patterns of pleasure and pain support an observer in
   believing about the cause of the world?
5. What should be said first to a person whose loss is recent?
6. Does describing blindness as a privation of sight explain why this person
   became blind?

For item 6, state what the privation account answers and what it leaves open.

## 4. Leibniz–Hume argument exchange — 15 minutes

Write each position as numbered claims rather than a paragraph.

### Leibniz

1. State the objection concerning the world God would choose.
2. State the prosyllogism that moves from divine wisdom and goodness to the
   chosen world.
3. Identify the premise Leibniz denies.
4. Explain why compossibility and whole-world comparison matter.
5. State the strongest objection to inferring local justification from an
   unsurpassed whole.

### Philo in Hume's dialogue

1. List the four circumstances Philo says produce much misery.
2. State what alteration of those circumstances is meant to show.
3. Distinguish “a good deity might have reasons” from “the observed world
   supports the inference to perfect goodness.”
4. State the strongest Cleanthes-style concern about moving beyond observed
   effects.

Finish with one sentence in which Leibniz answers Philo and one sentence in
which Philo presses Leibniz. Make each sentence recognizable to its position.
