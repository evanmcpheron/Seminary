# Leibniz and Hume on Worlds, Evidence, and Divine Goodness

## Learning objectives

After completing this lecture, the student should be able to:

- reconstruct the objection, prosyllogism, and denied premise in Leibniz's
  best-world argument;
- explain possible worlds, compossibility, whole-world value, and contingency;
- identify Demea's, Cleanthes's, and Philo's roles in Hume's dialogue;
- distinguish showing compatibility from establishing an empirical inference;
- compare Leibnizian and Humean reasoning without caricature; and
- offer a provisional Christian evaluation that acknowledges unresolved
  evidential and moral questions.

## Key terms

**Possible world:** a complete way reality might have been, considered as a
maximal possibility rather than another physical planet. **Compossibility:**
the capacity of possible things or states of affairs to exist together within
one complete world. **Best possible world:** a possible world unsurpassed in
overall value. **Sufficient reason:** an explanation of why something is so
rather than otherwise. **Contingency:** the status of what is actual but not
necessary in itself. **Natural theology:** reasoning about God from creation or
general human experience rather than from special revelation. **A priori:**
known or argued independently of the particular empirical evidence under
discussion. **A posteriori:** inferred from experience or observed effects.
**Mixed phenomena:** effects displaying both goods and evils. **Compatibility:**
the absence of contradiction between claims. **Evidential support:** a relation
in which evidence raises or lowers the reasonableness or probability of a
hypothesis.

## Lecture

### 1. From “Where did evil come from?” to “What does this world show?”

Augustine and Aquinas begin with created goodness, corruption, will, and
causation. Leibniz and Hume shift the center of gravity. Leibniz asks how a
perfect God could rationally choose a world containing evil. Hume asks what an
observer may infer about a world's cause when the observed effects include both
order and misery.

The distinction is important. Leibniz reasons from a prior conception of God as
perfect in power, wisdom, and goodness toward a conclusion about God's choice.
Hume's character Philo asks whether experience of the world can establish that
prior conception in the first place. The two projects therefore meet at an
epistemic boundary:

- If perfect goodness is already well grounded, perhaps unknown reasons can
  reconcile it with suffering.
- If perfect goodness must be inferred from worldly effects alone, unexplained
  suffering may weaken or block the inference.

Neither side should be reduced to “everything is fine” or “evil deductively
disproves God.” Leibniz acknowledges real disorder and argues about the best
available whole. Philo repeatedly allows possible compatibility while denying
that compatibility supplies the empirical proof Cleanthes needs.

### 2. Leibniz's objection and reply

The assigned Leibniz selection begins in explicit argument form. The objection
says that anyone who fails to choose the best lacks power, knowledge, or
goodness; God did not choose the best in creating this world; therefore God
lacks at least one perfection. A supporting argument claims that a maker has
not chosen the best when the product contains avoidable evil or when making it
could have been omitted.[^1]

Leibniz denies the objection's second premise. He does not deny that the world
contains evil. He denies the move from “this world contains an evil that we can
describe as absent” to “there is a better complete world God could have
actualized.” His answer depends on how complete possibilities relate.

The basic reasoning can be reconstructed as follows:

1. God is perfect in power, wisdom, and goodness.
2. A perfectly wise and good creator acts for sufficient reason and does not
   choose a worse complete option when a better one is available.
3. God surveys the complete possible worlds and actualizes one.
4. The actual world is the world God chose.
5. Therefore the actual world is the best possible complete world, even though
   it contains local evils.

This is not a neutral proof beginning only from suffering. Premise 1 supplies a
theological starting point, and premises 2–3 supply substantive metaphysical
claims. The conclusion is powerful only if those premises and the comparison of
complete worlds can be defended.

### 3. Possible worlds and compossibility

A *possible world* is not a distant cosmos alongside ours. It is a complete way
reality might have been. “The injured traveler is unharmed” describes part of a
possibility, not yet a complete world. A complete world must include every
connected difference: what agents choose, what histories result, which persons
exist, what laws operate, and what goods or losses follow.

Leibniz emphasizes that a world is an interconnected whole. Removing one evil
while holding everything else fixed may not identify a genuinely available
world. The desired states might not be *compossible*. For example, a proposal
might combine undetermined creaturely choices with a guarantee that every
choice is good, or stable natural regularity with constant interventions that
prevent every harmful collision. Whether those combinations are possible
cannot be assumed merely because each description sounds desirable in
isolation.[^2]

**Primary-source claim:** Leibniz argues that supreme wisdom chooses the best
among possible worlds and that the parts of a world are connected. He therefore
denies that subtracting a described evil, with no attention to the entire
world, proves that a better total possibility was available.[^2]

This is the strongest form of the view. It does not say this world is pleasant
at every point, that each pain benefits its sufferer, or that humans can see how
every evil contributes to the whole. It says that the relevant object of divine
choice is a complete world and that comparison must be global rather than a
simple subtraction of an unwanted event.

### 4. Permission, positive being, and creaturely contingency

Leibniz also inherits the privation tradition. God causes what is positive in
created being and action; creaturely limitation and defective will account for
the privative feature of evil. His illustration of differently laden boats
carried by the same current aims to separate the positive source of motion from
the limitation that produces unequal speed. The current supplies movement, but
the load explains the slowing.[^3]

**Primary-source claim:** On this account God does not will moral evil for its
own sake. God permits evils within the choice of a complete order whose total
good supplies the reason for creation. Creaturely acts remain contingent in
themselves even though God knows them and chooses a world whose complete history
includes them.[^2]

This claim tries to preserve both providence and agency. Certainty is not the
same as necessity: from “God certainly knows that an agent chooses A” it does
not follow, without further argument, that the agent is coerced or that choosing
B is logically contradictory. Leibniz treats the action as flowing from the
creature within the chosen order, not as a defect injected by God.

The distinction is relevant to Lewis. Lewis argues that rational freedom
includes the possibility of creaturely self-centering and that a stable world
allows choices to have consequences. He too distinguishes God's good creation
from the creature's abuse of a good power. But Lewis does not offer Leibniz's
full claim that the actual world is the best possible world. His admitted
speculation about human origins should not be silently used to establish
Leibniz's modal premises.[^4]

### 5. What best-world reasoning accomplishes

Leibniz's framework offers at least three important insights.

First, it exposes the **local-improvement fallacy**. Improving one feature while
stipulating that every connected feature remains unchanged may not describe a
possible complete world. Moral choices, personal histories, natural laws, and
goods can be interdependent.

Second, it distinguishes **permission from approval**. An agent may permit a
bad feature only because preventing it would exclude a sufficiently important
good or require an equally bad or worse whole. This does not make the bad
feature good under its own description.

Third, it imposes **epistemic humility**. Finite observers do not survey all
complete possibilities or all long-range consequences. The absence of a reason
visible to us is not deductive proof that no reason exists.

These points can block an overconfident inference. They do not yet prove that
the actual world is best or that any particular evil is necessary for it. The
move from “we do not see all possibilities” to “this evil is required” would be
invalid. Ignorance can limit a conclusion; it cannot by itself establish a
specific hidden explanation.

### 6. Major objections to Leibniz

A fair critic can press at least four questions.

**First, does a best possible world exist?** For any finite collection of goods,
perhaps a slightly better world is possible. If value has no maximum, premise 3
cannot be satisfied in the way Leibniz proposes. Leibniz argues that perfect
wisdom would not create without a best option, but the critic may see that as a
reason to revise the premise rather than proof of a maximum.

**Second, how are worlds compared?** Goods differ in kind, persons are not
interchangeable units, and some evils seem resistant to aggregation. A greater
total of beauty or happiness elsewhere does not obviously justify a person's
torture. Whole-world value needs a moral account of what may and may not be
traded.

**Third, are the modal connections established?** Saying every detail is
connected does not show that each actual evil is tied to a greater good or the
avoidance of a worse evil. The specific compossibility claim needs support.

**Fourth, what practical posture follows?** If people infer that actual evil is
necessary because the actual world is best, they may excuse injustice or avoid
prevention. That conclusion would be self-defeating within a providential
framework: efforts to resist evil are themselves parts of the actual order.
More fundamentally, the theory gives finite agents no warrant to identify a
hidden necessity in a case.

**Contested evaluation:** Leibniz supplies a sophisticated answer to simplistic
world-editing objections, but his strongest conclusion depends on disputed
metaphysical and axiological premises. The view deserves more than parody, and
the suffering of persons deserves more than aggregation.

### 7. Hume writes a dialogue, not a textbook thesis

The Hume selection comes from *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*. Its
speakers have distinct strategies. Demea stresses human misery and divine
incomprehensibility. Cleanthes defends an empirical, analogical argument from
the ordered world to a designing intelligence. Philo is skeptical of the reach
of that inference and uses the world's mixture of good and evil to pressure
claims about the designer's moral attributes.[^5]

**Primary-source claim:** The assigned passage gives arguments to characters.
It is therefore safest to write “Philo argues” or “Cleanthes replies” rather
than treating every sentence as direct authorial declaration. Interpreters
debate how the dialogue's reversals and closing language bear on Hume's own
position. The philosophical argument can be reconstructed without pretending
that this interpretive question is simple.

Demea and Philo initially agree that human life contains extensive misery, but
their purposes differ. Demea uses dependence and suffering to motivate
religious recourse and mystery. Philo uses the same evidence to challenge
Cleanthes: if divine goodness is inferred by analogy from effects, the painful
effects cannot be ignored.

This exposes a tension in natural theology. Cleanthes needs human analogy to
move from ordered effects to intelligence. If he abandons analogy whenever the
effects raise questions about goodness, the method becomes selective. If he
retains the analogy, mixed effects complicate the inference to unlimited
benevolence.

### 8. Four circumstances of suffering

Philo asks readers to consider alternative designs that appear, from a human
perspective, to involve less suffering. He organizes much natural evil under
four circumstances.[^6]

1. **Pain as a motive:** living creatures are moved by pain as well as pleasure.
   Philo asks why degrees of pleasure could not guide action without severe
   pain.
2. **General laws:** regular causes permit planning, but their collisions also
   cause harm. Philo asks why selective providential adjustments could not
   prevent more misery without destroying regularity.
3. **Narrow endowments:** creatures often have barely sufficient resources and
   capacities, leaving little margin for accident, illness, scarcity, or error.
4. **Imprecise adjustment:** useful systems—weather, bodily processes,
   passions—can exceed or fall short of beneficial ranges and ruin individual
   lives.

The force of the examples is comparative. Philo is not required to design a
complete world in exhaustive detail before questioning the inference from this
world to perfect goodness. He asks why apparently modest improvements are not
present and why confident appeals to unknown necessity should count as
evidence.

A Leibnizian reply will object that these alternatives are partial
descriptions. Removing pain, increasing intervention, enlarging every capacity,
or narrowing every natural range may exclude connected goods or produce a
worse total world. Philo anticipates part of that reply: hidden reasons may
establish possible compatibility. His sharper question is whether an unknown
reason supplies positive empirical support.

### 9. Compatibility is not evidential support

This is the central distinction of the lecture.

Suppose:

- **G:** a perfectly powerful, wise, and good God created the world.
- **E:** the world displays its actual mixture and distribution of order,
  pleasure, pain, moral virtue, and moral evil.

A defense may show that `G` and `E` are possibly compatible. That defeats a
claim of strict contradiction. But Cleanthes's natural theology needs a further
claim: `E` should support an inference to `G`, or at least fit `G` better than
relevant alternatives.

**Primary-source claim:** Philo concedes that unknown reasons may reconcile bad
appearances with perfect attributes if those attributes are established on
other grounds. He denies that merely possible unknown reasons allow an observer
to infer perfect goodness from the mixed phenomena themselves.[^7]

The logic can be displayed without assigning numerical probabilities:

1. An empirical argument from an effect to a cause must proportion the inferred
   qualities of the cause to the observed effect.
2. The observed world contains mixed goods and evils rather than unmixed
   benevolence.
3. Unknown explanations can preserve compatibility between the mixed effect and
   a perfectly good cause, but they do not turn the mixed effect into evidence
   of unmixed goodness.
4. Therefore observation of this world alone does not establish a perfectly
   good cause by Cleanthes's analogical method.

This is not yet the later probabilistic evidential argument studied in Weeks
8–9. It is an important historical predecessor. It shifts the burden from bare
consistency to comparative explanation.

### 10. Mixed phenomena and rival hypotheses

Philo considers several broad hypotheses about the moral character of a first
cause: perfect goodness, perfect malice, opposed good and evil principles, or
indifference. Mixed phenomena do not directly display either perfect goodness
or perfect malice. The regular unity of nature counts against a simple dualist
conflict. Philo therefore argues that indifference fits the mixed evidence more
readily than the morally perfect designer Cleanthes wants to infer.[^7]

This conclusion must be handled carefully. “Fits more readily” is an
evidential judgment, not a demonstration that an indifferent cause exists. The
hypotheses may not exhaust every alternative, and the entire argument assumes
that natural-theological inference should be restricted to observed worldly
effects. A Christian may claim independent reasons from revelation, religious
experience, historical testimony, or other arguments. Philo's response is that
those would be additional evidence, not success for this particular design
inference.

The dialogue also complicates a quick appeal to mystery. Human cognitive limits
matter, but they cut both ways. If observers are too limited to judge that
suffering counts against perfect goodness, are they also too limited to infer
perfect goodness from order? A stable position must explain why the same
epistemic standard does not protect only favorable data.

### 11. Leibniz and Hume in direct conversation

The rival pressure can now be stated charitably.

**Leibniz to Philo:** You evaluate isolated changes without surveying complete,
compossible worlds. Perfect wisdom has access to relations finite observers do
not. The fact that you cannot see a sufficient reason does not show that none
exists.

**Philo to Leibniz:** Your unknown total plan may protect logical compatibility,
but the plan is not observed. If perfection is supposed to be inferred from the
world, actual misery is part of the evidence. Ignorance of alternatives cannot
serve as positive evidence that this complete world is best.

**Leibniz to Philo:** The argument for God and divine perfection need not rest
only on the narrow design analogy you criticize. Metaphysical reasoning about a
sufficient reason for contingent reality supplies an independent route.

**Philo to Leibniz:** Then the quality of that metaphysical reasoning must be
assessed independently. The world itself does not verify your ranking of all
possibilities, and moral evaluation cannot be suspended when the theory reaches
particular sufferers.

This exchange reveals two burdens. The critic must not treat inability to see a
reason as deductive proof that none exists. The theodicist must not treat the
possibility of an unknown reason as proof that an actual justifying reason
exists.

### 12. Biblical and confessional evaluation

Week 2's canonical grammar does not map neatly onto either system.

Genesis 1–2 supports a good creation and thereby agrees with Leibniz against a
world whose ultimate source is indifferent or malicious. Genesis 3 locates
moral rebellion in creatures rather than God. Yet Job denies that observers can
read a sufferer's guilt or God's particular reason directly from circumstances.
The lament psalms refuse to treat painful appearances as intellectually or
religiously irrelevant. Romans 8 names real groaning while locating hope in
God's action and conformity to Christ. Revelation 21–22 expects judgment and a
renewed creation in which death, pain, and curse are overcome.[^8]

**Confessional conclusion:** This curriculum affirms the perfectly good triune
God on the basis of Christian Scripture and confession, not because every
feature of the present world transparently displays an optimal arrangement. It
therefore does not accept Philo's restriction of relevant evidence to the
present mixed phenomena. It also accepts his demand that suffering be counted
honestly and that possible compatibility not be advertised as a complete
theodicy.

**Curriculum synthesis:** A responsible Christian response can say three things
together. First, no observed evil creates a formal contradiction in the mere
concept of a good Creator if a morally sufficient permission is possible.
Second, the amount, kinds, and distribution of suffering still generate serious
evidential and moral pressure. Third, Christian hope rests on God's revealed
character and promised defeat of evil, not on a finite observer's ability to
label each tragedy necessary for the best world.

That synthesis remains provisional. Later weeks will examine logical and
evidential arguments in sharper form. At this stage, intellectual faithfulness
requires neither premature surrender nor premature triumph.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Leibnizian best-world theodicy:** a perfect creator selects the best
  complete possible world; local subtraction does not establish a better
  compossible whole. Critics dispute the existence and comparability of a best
  world and the moral treatment of persons within total-value reasoning.
- **Humean empirical critique:** Philo argues that mixed effects cannot establish
  unmixed moral perfection by analogy. Unknown reasons may preserve
  compatibility but do not provide positive empirical support.
- **Mystery or skeptical restraint:** Demea appeals to divine
  incomprehensibility, while Philo uses human limits to restrict inference.
  The approaches share language of limitation but serve different conclusions.
- **Christian revealed theology:** the curriculum treats Scripture and historic
  confession as evidence beyond Cleanthes's design analogy, while allowing
  Hume's criticism to test overly confident natural-theological claims.

## Confessional analysis

The curriculum affirms God's perfect power, wisdom, and goodness, created
goodness, responsible creaturely agency, providence, judgment, resurrection,
and new creation. It does not claim that the present world transparently proves
each of those doctrines or that humans can rank all possible worlds. Hume's
challenge is presented in its strongest responsible form before evaluation.
Leibniz is neither caricatured as denying evil nor treated as having supplied a
case-specific explanation. These are independent curriculum judgments, not
official Wheaton College teaching.

## Summary

Leibniz reasons from divine perfection to the choice of the best complete
possible world. His reply stresses compossibility, interconnected wholes,
permission rather than approval, and contingent creaturely action. It blocks
simple local-edit arguments but depends on disputed claims about a best world,
world comparison, and moral aggregation. In Hume's dialogue, Philo challenges
Cleanthes's empirical inference from the world to a perfectly good designer.
The four circumstances of suffering make the data mixed; unknown reasons may
preserve compatibility, but they do not establish perfect goodness from those
data. Christian confession brings scriptural evidence and revealed hope beyond
the design analogy while retaining Hume's demand to count suffering honestly
and refusing to identify hidden reasons for particular evils.
