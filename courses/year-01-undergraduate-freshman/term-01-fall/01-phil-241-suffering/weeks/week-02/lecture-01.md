# Creation, Fall, and Lament

## Learning objectives

After completing this lecture, the student should be able to:

- trace the movement from good creation through rebellion, curse, and exile;
- distinguish claims made by Genesis 1–3 from later theological inferences;
- compare three scriptural patterns of lament and moral protest;
- reconstruct Lewis's account of divine goodness and perfecting love; and
- identify both a confessional use and a pastoral misuse of this biblical
  grammar.

## Key terms

**Creation:** God's act of bringing into being and sustaining a reality distinct
from God. **Goodness:** that which fittingly fulfills or participates in the good;
in Genesis 1 it is first God's evaluation of created order. **Image of God:** the
human identity and vocation named in Genesis 1:26–28. **Fall:** the theological
name for humanity's primal rebellion and its consequences narrated in Genesis
3. **Curse:** divine judgment on the serpent and the ground in Genesis 3; the
text must be read carefully before extending the term. **Lament:** truthful
address to God that voices distress, complaint, petition, remembrance, or hope.
**Retribution principle:** the expectation that righteous conduct ordinarily
receives blessing and wicked conduct punishment. **Analogy:** reasoning from a
similar relation while acknowledging relevant dissimilarity.

## Required preparation

Read Genesis 1–3 and Psalms 22, 73, and 88 in the ESV 2025 and NASB 2020. Read
C. S. Lewis, *The Problem of Pain*, chapter 3, “Divine Goodness.” Complete the

## Lecture

### 1. A grammar, not a shortcut

Week 1 separated several questions about suffering. Week 2 asks how Scripture
teaches Christians to speak before trying to solve every philosophical problem.
The word *grammar* is deliberate. Grammar supplies patterns by which truthful
sentences may be formed. It does not generate the same sentence for every case.
Genesis, Job, the Psalms, Romans, and Revelation give recurring categories—good
creation, creaturely rebellion, judgment, lament, providence, groaning, hope,
and new creation. They do not authorize a speaker to infer a hidden divine
reason for each tragedy.

**Curriculum synthesis:** A canonical framework should widen moral attention.
It should help the student say that creation is genuinely good, evil is
genuinely opposed to God's will, creatures are responsible agents, suffering is
not distributed by a simple desert formula, protest can be faithful prayer, and
Christian hope concerns restoration and judgment. If the framework instead
turns every sufferer into an illustration of a theory, it has been misused.

The distinction between *textual observation* and *theological inference* is
essential. A textual observation identifies what a passage depicts or asserts.
A theological inference relates that claim to other passages or doctrines. An
inference may be responsible, but it should not be smuggled into the wording of
the source. This discipline is especially important in Genesis 1–3, where a
compact narrative carries enormous theological weight.

### 2. Genesis 1–2: goodness before disorder

**Primary-source claim:** Genesis 1 depicts God creating by speech, ordering
realms, filling them with living creatures, and repeatedly calling the result
good. Humanity, male and female, bears God's image and receives a vocation to be
fruitful and exercise rule. The completed whole is declared very good. Genesis
2 presents the human as formed from the ground and animated by God's gift of
life, placed in a garden to work and keep it, given abundant provision and a
real prohibition, and made for interpersonal fellowship.[^1]

Several features matter for the problem of suffering. First, matter and bodily
life are not introduced as evil. Embodiment, work, relation, food, animal life,
and a differentiated world all belong to created goodness. Christian hope
therefore cannot mean escape from creation as though finitude were the basic
mistake. Second, humans are dependent creatures. They receive life, place,
vocation, and command; they are not self-originating measures of good and evil.
Third, creaturely limit is not itself punishment. The command in Genesis 2
places a moral limit within a good world before the transgression.

**Contested interpretation:** Christian readers differ over the genre and
chronology of Genesis 1–2, the relation between the two creation accounts, and
the precise historical and scientific claims the chapters make. Those disputes
matter, but this week's assigned text supports a narrower point shared across
many readings: the narrative identifies God as creator, creation as good,
humans as dependent image-bearers, and human vocation as morally answerable to
God. The lecture does not settle debates the assigned sources cannot settle.

These claims set a rule for later explanation. Evil is neither a coeternal
substance alongside God nor the proper name for material existence. Whatever
account Christians give of suffering, it must preserve the prior goodness of
God's creation and the goodness of the embodied life God intends to renew.

### 3. Genesis 3: mistrust, rebellion, and fractured life

Genesis 3 begins with contested speech about God. The serpent recasts the
command, questions the announced consequence, and portrays God as withholding a
good. The woman and the man eat; their new awareness produces shame and hiding.
When God questions them, the man blames the woman and implicitly the giver of
the woman, while the woman identifies the serpent's deception. The scene moves
from mistrust to transgression, from transgression to alienation, and from
alienation to blame.[^2]

**Primary-source claim:** The judgments name conflict with the serpent, pain in
childbearing, distorted human relation, hardship in cultivating the ground,
toil, mortality, and exclusion from the garden and the tree of life. The serpent
and the ground are explicitly cursed. The humans are judged and exiled, but the
wording should not be carelessly expanded beyond what the text says.[^2]

Later Christian theology calls this complex the *fall*. The term gathers the
narrative's movement from created fellowship to rebellion and estrangement. It
rightly directs attention to the moral agency of creatures and to the pervasive
disorder in human relation to God, neighbor, self, and earth. It also explains
why biblical hope is not merely pain control. Restoration must address guilt,
alienation, death, domination, and disordered desire.

But the framework has limits. Genesis 3 does not give a case-specific formula
by which a person's suffering can be traced to that person's sin. It does not
say that every pain is proportionate punishment, or that a sufferer has less
faith than a person who prospers. It also does not, by itself, answer every
question about animal suffering, geological history, disease, or the exact
relation between the primal transgression and natural processes.

**Confessional conclusion:** Within this curriculum's orthodox Protestant
framework, sin is a real creaturely revolt against the good Creator, and the
world humans inhabit is not now as it ought to be. That conclusion rejects both
the denial of guilt and the denial of creation's goodness. It does not grant a
student permission to pronounce God's particular reason for another person's
loss.

This distinction matters pastorally. “Suffering exists within a creation
disordered by sin” is a general theological claim. “Your child died because of
your sin” is a particular accusation. The second does not follow from the first.
Job will make that failure unmistakable in Lecture 2.

### 4. Lament as faithful speech

If Genesis supplies a grammar of origin, vocation, rebellion, and exile, the
Psalms supply a grammar for speaking from within distress. Lament is not the
opposite of faith. It is speech addressed to God when experience and confessed
truth seem painfully misaligned. A lament may name absence, accuse, remember,
ask, wait, praise, or end without visible resolution.

**Primary-source claim:** Psalm 22 opens with abandonment and unanswered crying.
The speaker alternates between present derision and memories of divine
faithfulness, describes bodily and social threat, petitions God to come near,
and later announces praise and a widening horizon in which the afflicted are
heard and the nations worship.[^3] The movement matters, but the opening must not
be treated as a disposable prelude. The praise is the praise of the one who has
actually voiced abandonment.

Christian readers also hear Psalm 22 through its use in the passion narratives.
That canonical relation intensifies rather than cancels lament: the crucified
Christ takes its opening cry upon his lips. One should not use the later praise
to demand that a suffering person move through grief on a schedule.

Psalm 73 addresses a different crisis. The speaker nearly loses moral footing
because the arrogant appear secure and prosperous while the faithful suffer.
The problem is not simply pain; it is the apparent failure of moral order. A
change occurs when the speaker enters God's presence and perceives the end of
the wicked. The resolution combines judgment with a deeper good: nearness to
God even when body and heart fail.[^4]

**Contested interpretation:** Readers may emphasize either the psalmist's new
understanding of the wicked's destiny or the reorientation of desire toward God.
Both are present. The psalm does not say that apparent injustice is imaginary.
It relocates the present within a larger moral horizon and names communion with
God as a good prosperity cannot measure.

Psalm 88 prevents us from making every lament follow the same arc. The speaker
addresses the God of salvation and continues to pray, but describes lifelong
affliction, abandonment, unanswered petition, and social isolation. Unlike many
laments, the psalm ends in darkness rather than narrated deliverance.[^5]

**Broad scholarly consensus:** The visible form of Psalm 88 gives canonical
room to unresolved lament. That observation does not require claiming that the
speaker has no faith; prayer continues even when consolation is not reported.
Nor should an interpreter append Psalm 22's turn to praise as though Psalm 88
were defective. Scripture preserves both forms.

Taken together, the three psalms resist two simplifications. Despair is not the
only honest speech, because remembrance, petition, judgment, and praise remain
possible. Yet rapid resolution is not the only faithful speech, because prayer
may end in darkness. The pastoral implication is permission before technique:
permit truthful address, do not coerce disclosure, and do not manufacture a
happy ending.

### 5. Lewis on divine goodness and perfecting love

Lewis's chapter 3 asks whether “God is good” has enough continuity with human
moral language to answer the problem of pain. If divine goodness meant the
reverse of everything creatures recognize as good, calling God good would offer
no assurance. Yet if perfect goodness must approve every finite desire exactly
as it stands, the word would merely project human preference onto God.[^6]

**Primary-source claim:** Lewis argues that divine morality exceeds human moral
understanding without simply contradicting it. He then distinguishes love from
mere kindness understood as the wish that another be content on whatever terms.
Love seeks the beloved's actual good and may oppose the beloved's destructive
wants. To develop this claim, Lewis uses relations between an artist and a work,
a human and an animal, a father and a son, and one lover and another. He warns
that creator and creature have a unique relation and that analogies illuminate
only aspects of it; he identifies danger in at least one of his own analogies.[^6]

The argument does real conceptual work. Perfect goodness need not be indulgence.
If persons can be mistaken about their own good, it is possible for love to
will transformation rather than immediate satisfaction. Lewis also presents
God's love as prior giving rather than need: creatures do not fill a lack in
God. Their good lies in receiving and responding to the One who gives them being.

**Contested evaluation:** The analogy does not by itself justify the permission
of any particular horror. An artist may alter a canvas because the canvas is not
a person; a pet owner or parent can act abusively while claiming to know the
dependent's good; human romantic desire is not a safe measure of divine action.
The greater the asymmetry, the more important Lewis's warning about analogy
becomes. A critic can reasonably ask what moral constraints distinguish
perfecting love from paternalistic domination and why less harmful means would
not secure the same good.

Lewis's proposal should therefore be reconstructed before it is evaluated. Its
conclusion is not “suffering proves God loves you” or “every pain improves its
victim.” It is the more limited claim that love can be good without being mere
kindness, and that divine love may seek a creature's fulfillment in God rather
than the preservation of every present preference. Whether that account can
address severe, involuntary, apparently destructive suffering remains a live
question.

### 6. Rules for confessional and pastoral use

**Confessional conclusion:** The canonical pattern read here affirms a good
Creator, responsible creaturely rebellion, a world marked by judgment and
alienation, faithful permission to lament, and a love not reducible to comfort.
It denies that evil belongs to God's good creation as evil and denies that
Christian faith requires pious silence about abandonment or injustice.

Four rules keep this grammar from becoming a weapon:

1. **Preserve the order.** Begin with created goodness; do not treat pain,
   finitude, or embodiment as the essence of reality.
2. **Distinguish levels of claim.** A doctrine about the fall does not reveal
   the cause or purpose of a particular person's suffering.
3. **Let each lament keep its shape.** Do not force Psalm 88 to end like Psalm
   22 or treat Psalm 73's judgment as permission for personal vengeance.
4. **Test analogies morally.** An analogy can clarify one relation while
   distorting another. Never use “perfecting love” to excuse abuse, prevent
   safety-seeking, or silence protest.

A student may confess Christian hope while admitting unresolved questions. The
next lecture will show that this restraint is not an embarrassment added from
outside Scripture. It is learned within Job itself.

## Primary-source engagement

The lecture closely reads Genesis 1–3 and Psalms 22, 73, and 88 in the assigned

Claims about the texts are marked as primary-source claims. The fall framework,
canonical synthesis, and pastoral rules are identified as theological inference
or curriculum synthesis rather than silently attributed to a single passage.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Creation-fall framework:** Christian interpreters commonly relate present
  evil to good creation and creaturely rebellion, while disputing the genre and
  chronology of Genesis and the fall's relation to natural history.
- **Lament as trust:** One reading emphasizes that complaint remains addressed
  to God and can therefore enact trust. Another emphasizes lament's protest
  against experienced divine absence and moral disorder. The assigned psalms
  support both address and protest.
- **Lewis's perfecting-love proposal:** Defenders see a needed distinction
  between love and indulgence. Critics question whether analogies of formation
  explain severe suffering or protect the agency of vulnerable persons.

This lecture maps these positions only at the level supported by the assigned
primary sources. It does not claim a secondary-scholarly consensus beyond the
observable literary forms of the texts.

## Confessional analysis

This curriculum receives Genesis 1–3 as truthful Christian Scripture and reads
the Psalms canonically as authorized forms of prayer. It affirms creation's
goodness, humanity's fall into sin, divine judgment, and the moral legitimacy of
lament. It evaluates Lewis appreciatively but not uncritically. These are the
curriculum's confessional judgments, not official material of Wheaton College or
any other institution.

## Discussion and retrieval questions

1. Name two features of good creation that remain necessary for a Christian
   account of suffering.
2. Give one direct claim of Genesis 3 and one theological inference often drawn
   from it. Why should they be distinguished?
3. Compare the endings of Psalms 22, 73, and 88. What does each ending permit a
   worshiper to say?
4. Reconstruct Lewis's difference between kindness and love without endorsing
   it. What premise connects that difference to permitted suffering?
5. How could “perfecting love” be misused in an unequal human relationship?
6. Write one sentence that uses the fall framework responsibly in a classroom
   and one sentence that would overclaim in a pastoral encounter.

## Summary

Genesis 1–2 locates creaturely life within God's good gift, vocation, relation,
and limit. Genesis 3 narrates mistrust, rebellion, blame, fractured relations,
toil, mortality, and exile, but does not connect every sufferer's pain to a
particular sin. Psalms 22, 73, and 88 authorize distinct forms of faithful
lament, including prayer that ends without visible resolution. Lewis argues
that divine love seeks true creaturely good rather than mere contentment, while
his analogies leave serious questions about severe suffering and pastoral use.
The resulting grammar is confessional but restrained: it names the world before
God without pretending to know God's hidden reason for each event.
