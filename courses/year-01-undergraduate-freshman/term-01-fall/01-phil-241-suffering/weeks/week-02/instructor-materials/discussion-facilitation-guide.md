# Instructor Facilitation Guide — Lament, Explanation, and Hope

## Purpose

Facilitate accurate representation of the synthetic objection, source-controlled
use of Scripture, and a bounded first pastoral response. This is not counseling,
spiritual direction, or a forum for required personal disclosure.

## Before discussion

- Reconfirm that the case is fictional and participants may pass on speaking.
- Keep the Week 2 lectures and source audit available for adjudicating textual
  claims, but require participants to make their attempt before consulting them.
- Remind the group that confessional disagreement is permitted and that no one
  should diagnose another participant's faith or suffering.
- Know the applicable local emergency, safeguarding, pastoral, and licensed
  professional referral pathway. If a real disclosure indicates danger, stop
  the exercise and follow that pathway.

## Listening anchors

### Charitable statement of the challenge

A strong representation observes that Sam is not merely requesting comfort.
Sam raises a moral and philosophical objection: permission of devastating harm
appears difficult to reconcile with wise and good government, and familiar
religious language may blame victims or rename evil. Do not allow a respondent
to reduce the concern to anger, ignorance, or lack of faith.

### Job and providence

Look for these controls:

- the reader knows Job's characterization and a heavenly scene unavailable to
  Job and the friends;
- the friends' severe-sin diagnosis is disallowed;
- the divine speeches locate Job within a creation beyond his governance but do
  not disclose the prologue or explain a modern tornado; and
- confession of providence is distinguished from a hidden-purpose claim.

Useful follow-up: “Which sentence comes from Job, and which is your theological
inference?”

### Romans 8 and Revelation 21–22

Look for the paragraph's sequence: present suffering; creation's, believers',
and the Spirit's groaning; unseen hope; Christ-shaped good; inseparable love.
The Revelation material should name judgment, presence, renewal, healing, and
the end of death and curse. Correct any claim that the passages call each event
good or prove its necessity.

### First response

A responsible four-sentence response ordinarily acknowledges the loss, reflects
the question without diagnosis, rejects blame or overclaim, and asks whether Sam
wants listening or further discussion. It may name hope if it does so as
confession rather than an explanation of the tornado.

Do not provide participants with a memorized “model answer.” Ask them to revise
their own sentences against these standards.

## Intervention triggers

Pause and redirect if a participant:

- attributes the deaths to victims' sin or insufficient faith;
- claims revelation of God's purpose in the fictional event;
- treats future restoration as making death good or persons replaceable;
- uses providence to erase creaturely agency or moral accountability;
- pressures another participant to disclose, forgive, reconcile, pray, or
  affirm a doctrine; or
- shifts from educational discussion into diagnosis or crisis management.

## Debrief evidence

The activity has met its purpose when participants can name:

1. the strongest form of Sam's challenge;
2. one general confession and one unwarranted case-specific inference;
3. one unresolved philosophical question;
4. one contextual rather than closure-producing use of Scripture; and
5. one concrete revision that increases accuracy, agency, or restraint.

No grade is assigned. Record only aggregate misconceptions for future teaching;
do not preserve private disclosures in course notes.
