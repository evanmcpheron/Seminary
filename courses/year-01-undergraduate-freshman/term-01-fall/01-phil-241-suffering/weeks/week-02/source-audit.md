## Scripture claim audit

### Genesis 1–3

- Lecture 1 accurately identifies repeated divine evaluation, human creation in
  God's image, vocation, provision, prohibition, transgression, shame, blame,
  pain, toil, mortality, and exile.
- The lecture distinguishes direct wording from the later theological category
  of the fall.
- It does not infer a particular sufferer's guilt, settle disputed scientific or
  chronological questions, or claim that Genesis 3 alone explains every natural
  process.

### Psalms 22, 73, and 88

- The comparison preserves the distinct movement of each psalm: complaint and
  praise in Psalm 22, moral perplexity and reorientation in Psalm 73, and
  unresolved prayer in Psalm 88.
- The lecture does not force every lament into a common resolution or turn
  canonical relation to Christ into a demand on a sufferer's emotional timing.

### Job 1–2; 38–42

- Lecture 2 preserves the prologue's characterization of Job and distinguishes
  the reader's knowledge from Job's and the friends' knowledge.
- It does not treat the divine speeches as disclosing the prologue to Job or as
  giving a transferable reason for every case of suffering.
- Competing emphases in the divine speeches and readings of Behemoth and
  Leviathan are presented as contested.
- The discussion of Job 42 preserves the assigned translations' visible
  difference without making an unverified Hebrew or lexical claim.
- Restoration is not presented as replacing or erasing the persons who died.

### Romans 8:18–39 and Revelation 21–22

- Romans 8 is read in its paragraph-level sequence: suffering, creation's
  groaning, believers' groaning, the Spirit's aid, unseen hope, Christ-shaped
  good, and inseparable love.
- The lecture does not call every event good or use Romans 8:28 to prohibit grief.
- Revelation's new-creation imagery is joined to judgment, divine presence,
  healed nations, and the end of death, pain, and curse.
- Eschatological hope is not presented as a case-specific explanation or as
  permission for passivity toward preventable harm.

## Lewis chapter 3 claim audit

- The lecture attributes to Lewis only the chapter's moral-language dilemma,
  difference between kindness and love, limited use of four analogies, account
  of divine giving, and proposal that love can seek transformation.
- It does not attribute the lecture's abuse-aware objections or pastoral rules
  to Lewis.
- It explicitly states that analogy does not establish a morally sufficient
  reason for any particular horror and that Lewis's proposal remains open to
  serious criticism.

## Claim-label and theological audit

- Direct textual observations are marked as primary-source claims.
- Interpretive alternatives are marked as contested; the lectures do not invent
  a consensus on questions the assigned sources do not settle.
- Canonical synthesis, pastoral rules, and course-specific philosophical
  conclusions are marked as curriculum synthesis or confessional conclusion.
- Important skeptical concerns—especially the gap between perfecting love and
  permitted horrors—are presented before confessional evaluation.
- Confessional conclusions accord with the repository's theological commitments
  while disavowing official institutional authorship.

## Pastoral and student-safety audit

- No personal disclosure is required.
- The lectures prohibit case-specific claims about hidden divine purposes,
  victim-blaming, spiritualized protection of abusers, coerced lament, and
  premature emotional resolution.
- Pastoral statements remain educational and subject to the course policies on
  safety, privacy, competence, safeguarding, referral, and emergency response.

## Copyright audit

The lectures contain original synthesis, chapter and passage identifiers,
paraphrase, and citations. They reproduce no extended passage from a copyrighted
Bible edition or from Lewis. The reading plan requires lawful access and stores
no scan, chapter transcription, proprietary answer key, or transcript.
