# Week 8 Study Guide — Evidence, Bridge, and Scope

This is **45 minutes of ungraded, open-source practice**. Complete it after the
required reading and both lectures. Attempt every section before consulting the
instructor key. Do not use a real tragedy as an example.

## Forty-five-minute plan

| Work | Time |

## 1. Three-level claim sort

Label each statement `appearance`, `factual premise`, `theological premise`,
`atheistic conclusion`, or `none/insufficient`. More than one label is allowed
only if you explain an ambiguity.

1. No qualifying justifying good is apparent after careful reflection on `E`.
2. `E` actually could have been prevented without losing a greater good or
   permitting an equal or worse evil.
3. A perfectly good and knowing being prevents any intense suffering unless a
   qualifying reason obtains.
4. Standard theism is probably false.
5. The observer feels unable to endure the thought of `E`.
6. A good event happened several years after `E`.

Explain why statements 1 and 2 are not equivalent and why statement 6 does not
yet satisfy Rowe's conditions.

## 2. Compact and expanded maps

Write Rowe's compact argument in three numbered lines. Then expand it into five
to seven lines so that the evidence for the factual premise is visible. Mark
every added bridge as `curriculum reconstruction` or `student reconstruction`.

For each map, answer:

- Is the displayed inference deductive or inductive?
- Which premise or bridge carries the greatest dispute?
- What is the strongest warranted conclusion?
- What stronger conclusion is not established?

## 3. Absence-inference controls

For each scenario, rank failure to find `x` as `strong`, `moderate`, `weak`, or
`no` evidence that `x` is absent. Give one sentence about detectability and
search competence.

1. A careful search finds no full-size chair in an empty, well-lit room.
2. A naked-eye inspection finds no bacteria on a kitchen surface.
3. A trained mechanic using the appropriate diagnostic finds no stored fault
   code in a vehicle.
4. A beginning chess player sees no good defense in a complex position.
5. A finite moral reasoner sees no complete divine justification for one
   apparently gratuitous evil.

For item 5, name one respect in which it resembles items 2 or 4 and one respect
in which Rowe might argue that it differs.

## 4. Response role and conclusion audit

Classify each response as `rebutting`, `undercutting`, `counterevidence`,
`target revision`, or `incomplete`. Identify its point of contact.

1. “A justifying reason would probably be beyond finite human recognition.”
2. “This specific good was necessarily connected to the suffering and was
   good for the sufferer.”
3. “Independent evidence for God is stronger than the evil evidence.”
4. “God cannot unilaterally prevent every natural event.”
5. “A reason is logically possible.”

Put each conclusion on Lecture 2's six-level scale:

- the fawn case is relevant to standard theism;
- the fawn case proves no God exists;
- evil supplies some prima facie evidence against standard theism;
- atheism is more reasonable on total evidence; and
- Rowe's factual premise remains uncertain after an undercutting objection.

Finish with two sentences explaining how a Christian can reject Rowe's final
conclusion while representing the argument and a rational atheist charitably.
