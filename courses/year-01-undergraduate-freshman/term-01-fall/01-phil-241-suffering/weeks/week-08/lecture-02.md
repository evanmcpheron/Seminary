# From Apparently Gratuitous Suffering to Evidential Judgment

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture, the student should be able to:

1. distinguish an appearance claim, a factual claim about gratuity, and an
   atheistic conclusion;
2. reconstruct the inductive bridge connecting failure to find a justifying
   good with the probable absence of such a good;
3. identify detectability, search competence, domain knowledge, and sample
   breadth as controls on an inference from absence;
4. distinguish rebutting a conclusion from undercutting its support;
5. compare five major reply families without collapsing their burdens;
6. separate the evidential force of evil considered alone from a total-evidence
   verdict; and
7. preserve both confessional conviction and ordinary moral duties under
   uncertainty about divine reasons.

## Key terms

**Appearance claim:** a report that no adequate justifying reason is apparent
after responsible consideration.

**Factual claim:** the stronger claim that at least one instance of suffering
actually lacks a qualifying justifying good or worse-evil relation.

**Inductive bridge:** the defeasible move by which an appearance is treated as
evidence for a claim about reality.

**Inference from absence:** reasoning from failure to detect something to its
probable nonexistence.

**Detectability:** how likely it is that an item would be found if it existed.

**Search competence:** the observer's relevant ability, opportunity, method,
and care in looking for an item.

**Undercutting response:** a response that weakens the connection between
evidence and conclusion without directly proving the conclusion false.

**Rebutting response:** a response that supplies reason to think the conclusion
false.

**Prima facie evidence:** evidence that counts in one direction unless
defeated or outweighed.

**Total evidence:** all relevant evidence available to an inquirer, including
evidence not isolated in the immediate argument.

**Moral asymmetry:** the difference between what God could be justified in
permitting and what a limited human agent is authorized or obligated to do.

## Lecture

### 1. “Apparently” is neither a surrender nor a proof

The phrase *apparently gratuitous suffering* can be used in two unhelpful ways.
One speaker may use “apparently” to dissolve the argument: things only *look*
gratuitous, so there is nothing to explain. Another may treat appearance as
certainty: no reason is visible to me, so no reason exists. Rowe's evidential
project occupies the contested space between those extremes.

An appearance can be evidence without being infallible. If a careful observer
finds an empty parking space in full daylight, the appearance of emptiness is
usually good evidence that no vehicle is present. If the same observer sees no
microbe with the unaided eye, the appearance supplies almost no evidence that
the surface is sterile. The difference lies in what would be detectable, the
observer's capacities, and the search conditions.

The divine-reason question is vastly more difficult. A complete justification
could involve goods, harms, agents, histories, counterfactuals, and future
relations beyond a human observer's view. Yet it does not follow merely from
complexity that our evidence has zero force. The dispute is precisely about
whether and how much force remains.

### 2. Separate three levels of claim

Trakakis's analysis of Rowe distinguishes a factual premise from a theological
premise and then examines the support for the factual premise.[^1] A clear
argument map should separate three levels:

1. **Appearance:** after responsible reflection, no good we know of appears to
   justify divine permission of certain instances of intense suffering.
2. **Gratuity:** probably no good at all justifies permission of at least some
   of those instances.
3. **Theism:** given the moral premise about perfect goodness, the existence of
   gratuitous suffering counts against or supports the denial of standard
   theism.

Confusing the levels produces predictable errors. “We do not know the reason”
states an epistemic limitation, not gratuity. “The suffering is gratuitous”
states a fact about the absence of a qualifying reason. “Therefore God does
not exist” requires the theological premise connecting perfect goodness with
prevention.

Rowe's later presentation, as summarized by Trakakis, sometimes uses `P` for
the claim that no good *we know of* justifies the relevant cases and `Q` for
the claim that no good *at all* does so. The move from `P` to `Q` is inductive,
not deductive.[^1] The 1979 compact argument places the factual premise inside
a valid form but supports that premise by the appearance and profusion of
suffering.[^2] These presentations are related, not textually identical.

### 3. Make the inductive bridge visible

Consider this curriculum reconstruction:

1. For suffering `E`, careful inquiry reveals no known good `G` such that an
   omnipotent being could obtain `G` only by permitting `E` or an equal or
   worse evil; nor does inquiry reveal an equal or worse evil preventable only
   by permitting `E`.
2. If a qualifying justification for `E` existed, responsible human inquiry
   would have some meaningful chance of recognizing the relevant kind of good
   or connection.
3. Therefore, the continued absence of an apparent justification is evidence
   that `E` lacks such a justification.
4. When this pattern persists across many diverse instances, the evidence that
   at least some suffering is gratuitous increases.

This is not offered as Rowe's own numbered text. It displays the bridge that
an evaluator must test. Premise 2 does not require certainty or perfect
detectability. Evidence can be weak, moderate, or strong. If an item would
almost certainly be detected, absence is strong evidence; if it would almost
never be detected, absence is weak evidence.

The bridge must also identify what is supposed to be detectable. It may be
unrealistic to expect finite persons to survey every cosmic consequence.
Rowe's defender can answer that no omniscient survey is needed: human beings
know some very great goods and moral relations, and the persistent inability
to connect any of them adequately to many terrible cases may still matter.
The question is not whether humans know everything but whether the goods we
can grasp are sufficiently representative for a responsible inference.

### 4. Four controls on an inference from absence

At least four controls determine the evidential force of not finding a reason.

**First, detectability.** If the sought item existed, how likely is it that an
observer would notice it? A large object in a bare room is more detectable than
a small object in a dense forest. Divine justifying reasons may include remote
relations or unfamiliar goods, lowering expected detectability.

**Second, search competence.** Did the inquirer have relevant concepts,
evidence, time, and method? A novice's failure to see a chess tactic says less
than an expert's analysis. The issue is not intelligence in general but
competence relative to the object sought.

**Third, domain coverage.** Has inquiry considered the kinds of relation Rowe's
premise permits: necessary greater goods, unavoidable equal alternatives, and
worse evils prevented? A search limited to immediate human character growth
does not cover animal goods, communal goods, eschatological goods, or complex
counterfactual relations. Expanding the domain improves coverage but can also
make a proposal too flexible to test.

**Fourth, sample breadth.** One baffling case may admit an unseen explanation.
A large, varied set of apparently gratuitous cases can strengthen an induction
that at least one is genuinely gratuitous. Quantity alone is not enough: the
cases must be relevant, accurately characterized, and not selectively chosen.

These controls reveal why both sides can make serious arguments. The critic
emphasizes persistent appearance across intense and diverse suffering. The
skeptical respondent emphasizes the distance between finite moral-cognitive
access and the hypothesized wisdom of God. Week 10 will examine whether that
response undercuts Rowe without producing wider skepticism.

### 5. Why Rowe's case has genuine evidential force

Rowe's case has several strengths that should be stated before objections.

1. **It is modest about logic.** It does not depend on proving contradiction.
2. **It is morally serious.** Intense suffering remains bad in itself even if
   its permission could be justified.
3. **It is particular.** A concrete natural case pressures vague appeals to
   free will or character formation.
4. **It is cumulative.** The variety and profusion of suffering matter, not
   only one hypothetical event.
5. **It admits fallibility.** Rowe recognizes possible unknown goods and the
   difference between rational belief and certainty.[^2]
6. **It targets standard theism precisely.** Power, knowledge, and goodness
   jointly generate the prevention expectation.

The argument also captures an ordinary epistemic practice. We often revise a
hypothesis when repeated observations are surprising on it, even if we cannot
prove the hypothesis false. A theory that can accommodate every outcome by an
unspecified hidden factor risks losing explanatory discipline. Rowe's critic
asks the theist to explain when appeal to unknown reasons is principled rather
than merely protective.

This demand should not be caricatured as hostility to mystery. Every worldview
contains unknowns. The issue is whether a specific appeal to limitation gives
an independent reason to reduce the evidence's force and whether it does so
without disabling ordinary moral reasoning.

### 6. Two opposite shortcuts fail

The first shortcut is:

> I see no justifying reason; therefore, no justifying reason exists.

This is invalid as a deductive inference. It needs a premise about expected
detectability. Rowe does not need it to be deductive, but the evidential weight
still depends on that premise.

The opposite shortcut is:

> A justifying reason is possible; therefore, apparent gratuity is no evidence
> at all.

This also fails. Bare possibility answers a necessity claim more readily than
an evidential one. If the possible reason is highly ad hoc, weakly connected,
or unlikely on the rest of the view, it may do little to change the evidence.
The relevant question is comparative: how much should the reason's possibility
reduce confidence in the inference?

A disciplined analysis therefore avoids both certainty and zero-weight
language. It can say, “This case supplies some evidence,” “This objection
undercuts part of that support,” or “The remaining evidence is insufficient
for the proposed conclusion.” These are more precise than “obviously
gratuitous” or “mere mystery.”

### 7. Five families of response

Trakakis surveys multiple replies in the full entry; Week 8 needs a taxonomy,
not a verdict on the later debates.[^1]

**1. Challenge the appearance report.** A respondent argues that a familiar
good or worse-evil relation is actually apparent. The response must state the
relation, not merely name freedom, growth, order, or heaven.

**2. Challenge the inductive bridge.** A respondent grants that no reason is
apparent but argues that a reason would probably remain beyond human detection.
This is the route later associated with skeptical theism. Its burden includes
explaining why the limitation is relevant and how ordinary moral knowledge
survives.

**3. Rebut the gratuity claim.** A theodicy proposes that the suffering is
actually connected to a justifying good or the prevention of a worse evil.
This is stronger than offering a merely possible defense and bears the
necessity, alternatives, beneficiary, amount, and distribution burdens.

**4. Appeal to counterevidence.** A respondent grants that evil counts against
theism but argues that independent evidence for God outweighs it. This may
support a Moore shift or a broader total-evidence judgment. It does not show
that evil had no negative evidential force in isolation.

**5. Revise or deny a connecting premise.** A respondent may alter the concept
of divine power, knowledge, goodness, risk, or providence, or may deny that the
standard moral prevention principle applies to God. Such a view must state
whether it still answers Rowe's narrow target or adopts a different theology.
It must also avoid defining goodness so differently that the original
confessional claim is lost.

The five replies can be combined, but their conclusions should remain visible.
An inference challenge does not identify an actual good. A theodicy does not
automatically establish theism. Counterevidence does not convert suffering
into a good. Revising divine attributes may reduce one problem while creating
another.

### 8. Undercutting is not rebutting

Suppose a witness identifies a vehicle as blue under unusual lighting. A
rebutting reason would be reliable evidence that the vehicle was red. An
undercutting reason would show that the lighting made color identification
unreliable. The undercutter weakens the path from experience to conclusion
without proving the conclusion false.

Applied to Rowe:

- a **rebutting response** gives reason to believe a qualifying divine reason
  exists or that the suffering is not gratuitous;
- an **undercutting response** gives reason to doubt that failure to see a
  reason is reliable evidence of its absence.

The distinction prevents overstatement. If cognitive limitation succeeds only
as an undercutter, the correct result may be suspension of judgment about the
factual premise, not proof that every evil is justified. Conversely, showing
that one proposed greater good fails does not prove gratuity if other relevant
possibilities remain.

The same precision applies to confessional reasoning. Scripture may provide
rebutting grounds for a believer by supporting the truth of God's goodness and
providence. It may also undercut confidence in exhaustive human judgment by
teaching creaturely limitation. The philosophical task is to explain those
roles and their strength, not to label the argument “unbiblical” and stop.

### 9. Local evidence and the total evidence

The assigned Peterson debate intentionally asks what evil does when grounds
for belief in God are set aside.[^3] This isolation helps identify evidential
direction. If theism leads us to expect less apparently gratuitous suffering
than a rival view does, the observation may count against theism relative to
that rival. Week 9 will examine explanatory comparison more carefully.

A total-evidence judgment asks a larger question. It can include cosmological,
moral, historical, experiential, scriptural, testimonial, or other evidence,
along with objections to those sources. A person might coherently say:

1. evil is prima facie evidence against theism;
2. the evidence has significant but limited force;
3. other evidence supports theism more strongly; and
4. therefore theism remains reasonable on balance.

Another inquirer may judge the evil evidence stronger or the alleged
counterevidence weaker. The point is not that every weighting is equally good.
It is that “counts against” and “settles the total case” are different claims.
A sound A-05 analysis must name which one it evaluates.

### 10. Probability without false precision

Evidential arguments use probabilistic language, but Week 8 does not require
assigning invented numbers. Words such as *likely*, *unlikely*, *strong
evidence*, and *reasonable* need comparison classes and stated assumptions.
If a student writes “there is a 70 percent chance the evil is gratuitous,” the
number needs a defensible model and data; intuition dressed as a decimal is not
greater rigor.

For now, use an ordinal conclusion scale:

1. the observation is relevant to the hypothesis;
2. it supplies some evidence against the hypothesis;
3. it supplies substantial evidence against the hypothesis;
4. it makes the denial more reasonable when other evidence is set aside;
5. it makes the denial more reasonable on the total evidence; or
6. it establishes the denial.

Each step is stronger. An analysis must show why it crosses one threshold and
not another. Week 9's study of Draper will add more explicit hypothesis
comparison; Week 8 should not pretend that work has already been completed.

### 11. Christian epistemic humility has two directions

A Christian response rightly emphasizes the gap between finite creatures and
God. Job 38–42 displays divine wisdom and governance beyond Job's survey.
Romans 11:33–36 praises the depth of God's wisdom and knowledge. These passages
can discipline the demand that every divine reason be transparent to human
reasoners.

But humility runs in both directions. If a Christian does not know God's
reason for a particular event, humility forbids confidently assigning one.
“God may have reasons beyond our understanding” does not entail “God did this
to teach you,” “your suffering was necessary for someone else's growth,” or
“seeking relief would resist providence.” Those additions claim precisely the
access the appeal to mystery denies.

**Confessional conclusion:** Christians can reject the claim that God's
goodness is disconfirmed beyond rational recovery while treating Rowe's
evidence as a serious pressure on shallow theodicies. Scripture's account of
creation, fall, providence, cross, resurrection, judgment, and new creation
offers a larger story. It also calls evil an enemy, authorizes lament, commands
love, and locates final explanation in God's wisdom rather than the analyst's
case-specific confidence.

**Contested interpretation:** whether these confessional grounds rebut Rowe,
undercut his inference, or contribute only when total evidence is considered
must be argued. The course's theological commitment determines its constructive
conclusion; it does not replace accurate exposition or evidential analysis.

### 12. Moral asymmetry and responsible action

Suppose a person accepts that God might have a reason for permitting an event.
It does not follow that a creature should permit the same event. God, as
hypothesized in the argument, knows the complete field and can secure final
justice and redemption. Human agents do not. Their decisions are governed by
the evidence and responsibilities actually available to them.

**Curriculum synthesis:** a possible hidden divine reason is not a human
authorization. When harm is preventable, the ordinary response is protection,
reporting, treatment, advocacy, and care. When abuse is alleged, secure safety
and follow legal and institutional duties. When medical or mental health risk
is present, seek qualified help. Philosophical uncertainty cannot be used to
discourage these actions.

This moral asymmetry also protects argument quality. It blocks the inference
that every human intervention would frustrate providence. If intervention
occurs, it may itself belong within providence. More importantly for the
limited human agent, the revealed and ordinary duty to love the neighbor is
clearer than an imagined token reason for nonintervention.

End every evidential analysis with four labels:

1. **evidence:** what observation or source claim is doing work;
2. **bridge:** what connects the evidence to the next claim;
3. **defeater:** what could rebut or undercut that connection; and
4. **scope:** the strongest conclusion actually supported.

This protocol does not predetermine a verdict. It makes the verdict auditable.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Rowean evidential argument:** careful inability to identify a qualifying
  reason across severe and diverse suffering supports probable gratuity and,
  with the moral premise, atheism.
- **Known-good theodicy:** a specific good or worse-evil relation rebuts the
  claim of gratuity if its necessity and moral adequacy can be defended.
- **Cognitive-limit response:** the human inability to see a reason is not
  reliable evidence of its absence because expected detectability is low.
- **Counterevidence response:** evil may count against theism but be outweighed
  by independent evidence for theism.
- **Attribute-revision response:** an alternative concept of divine power,
  knowledge, goodness, or risk changes the prediction; it must disclose that
  it has modified Rowe's target.
- **Confessional critical response:** affirms classical Christian theism,
  receives biblical reasons for trust and humility, tests philosophical
  inferences, and refuses unsupported token explanations.

## Confessional analysis

The course affirms the God Rowe's argument targets in central respects: the
Creator is powerful, knowing, and good. A confessional evaluation therefore
cannot evade the challenge by quietly adopting a finite or morally equivocal
deity. It may appeal to creaturely limits, providence, redemptive history, and
eschatological hope, but each appeal must state whether it rebuts gratuity,
undercuts the induction, or contributes to total evidence.

Job, the lament psalms, Mark 15, Romans 8, and Revelation 21–22 jointly prevent
a thin response. Christian Scripture includes protest, innocent suffering,
divine self-giving, creation-wide groaning, judgment, resurrection, and the
promised end of death and pain. It warrants trust and hope but does not provide
the analyst with God's undisclosed reason for a token event.

## Summary

The central Week 8 dispute concerns an inductive bridge. No apparent
justifying reason is not identical to no justifying reason at all, yet a
responsible failure to find something can sometimes be evidence of its
absence. The force of the inference depends on detectability, search
competence, domain coverage, and sample breadth. Rowe's case is powerful
because it is modest, concrete, cumulative, and morally serious. Replies may
challenge the appearance, undercut the bridge, rebut gratuity, offer
counterevidence, or revise the target. Every reply must state its burden and
conclusion scope. Christian conviction can coexist with honest evidential
pressure, two-directional humility, and decisive care for sufferers.
