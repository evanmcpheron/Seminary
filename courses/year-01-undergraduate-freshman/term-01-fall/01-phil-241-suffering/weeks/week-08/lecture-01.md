# Rowe's Evidential Argument from Suffering

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture, the student should be able to:

1. distinguish Rowe's evidential argument from a strict logical argument from
   evil;
2. reconstruct the factual premise, moral or theological premise, and
   conclusion without changing their modal force;
3. explain the greater-good and worse-evil conditions on justified divine
   permission;
4. state why the fawn case supplies inductive support but not deductive proof;
5. compare a direct attack on the factual premise with a G. E. Moore shift;
6. explain Rowe's friendly atheism as a position about rational disagreement;
   and
7. formulate a bounded Christian response that neither dismisses the evidence
   nor invents a reason for a particular sufferer's pain.

## Key terms

**Narrow theism:** in Rowe's usage, belief in an omnipotent, omniscient,
eternal, supremely good creator.

**Evidential argument from evil:** an argument that evil counts against the
truth of theism even if God and evil are logically compatible.

**Factual premise:** the claim that there actually are instances of intense
suffering meeting Rowe's gratuity conditions.

**Theological premise:** Trakakis's label for the moral claim about what an
omniscient, wholly good being would prevent if able.

**Apparently gratuitous suffering:** suffering for which no adequate
justifying good or worse-evil prevention is apparent after responsible inquiry.

**Gratuitous suffering:** suffering an omnipotent, omniscient being could have
prevented without losing a greater good or permitting an evil equally bad or
worse.

**Necessary condition:** a condition that must obtain if another state of
affairs obtains; it need not be enough by itself.

**Direct attack:** an attempt to reject Rowe's factual premise by criticizing
its support or identifying relevant justifying goods.

**G. E. Moore shift:** an indirect counterargument that begins with warranted
belief in God's existence, retains Rowe's theological premise, and infers the
denial of the factual premise.

**Friendly atheism:** Rowe's view that an atheist may regard some theists as
rationally justified even while regarding their belief as false.

## Lecture

### 1. Logical consistency does not end the problem

Weeks 4–5 examined a strict logical challenge. That challenge sought to show
that theism and evil could not both be true. A successful consistency defense
answers it if the defense supplies a genuinely possible circumstance in which
God has a morally sufficient reason to permit evil. The defense need not show
that the circumstance is actual or probable.

William L. Rowe begins after that result. His 1979 article says that the claim
of logical inconsistency has not been established and turns to a different
question: whether the variety and abundance of suffering provide rational
support for atheism.[^1] This concession is strategically important. Rowe does
not need to prove that God and evil cannot coexist. He can grant possible
compatibility and ask whether the world we actually observe is evidence
against the God of standard theism.

Compare two conclusions:

1. `It is impossible that God and evil both exist.`
2. `Given the evidence of evil, it is reasonable to believe that God does not
   exist.`

The first is modal and deductive. The second is epistemic and evidential. A
possible-world countermodel can defeat the first without answering the second.
Once this difference is clear, “Plantinga solved the problem of evil” is too
broad. A more accurate statement is that Plantinga's defense is widely taken
to answer a prominent logical formulation while leaving evidential questions
open.

### 2. State Rowe's target precisely

Rowe distinguishes narrow and broad uses of *theism* and *atheism*. His narrow
theism concerns an omnipotent, omniscient, eternal, supremely good creator. His
argument targets that proposition, not every possible religious reality or
every revisionary concept of God.[^1] A process theologian who denies classical
omnipotence, for example, changes the target rather than directly answering the
argument about narrow theism.

The evidential question in the assigned Peterson debate is further restricted:
set aside grounds for belief in God and ask whether the evils in our world make
atheism more reasonable than theism.[^2] This restriction isolates the force
of the evidence of evil. It does not yet answer what a person should believe on
*all* available evidence. An argument may show that one body of evidence counts
against a hypothesis even if other evidence eventually outweighs it.

That distinction will matter when evaluating the Moore shift. A student must
not alternate without notice between these questions:

- Does evil supply evidence against theism?
- Does evil make atheism more reasonable when theistic evidence is set aside?
- Is atheism more reasonable on the total evidence?
- Is a particular believer rationally justified?

They are connected, but no answer simply entails all the others.

### 3. Suffering remains evil even if permission is justified

Rowe begins with intense human and animal suffering as something bad in itself.
He expressly distinguishes the suffering from a good to which it might lead.
A painful event could remain evil in itself while its permission is morally
justified because of a necessary connection to some greater good or to the
prevention of an equal or worse evil.[^1]

This distinction converges with Week 6's value analysis. If rescue after an
injury produces courage, the courage does not make the injury pleasant or
erase the loss. If a physician permits short-term pain because a life-saving
procedure cannot otherwise occur, the pain remains bad considered by itself.
The morally relevant questions are whether the good is sufficiently weighty,
whether the connection is necessary, whether a less harmful route is
available, and whether the agent has authority to permit the cost.

Rowe's argument should therefore not be paraphrased as “nothing good ever
comes from suffering.” That is not its claim. It asks whether at least some
intense suffering lacks the kind of necessary justifying connection that a
perfectly good and powerful being would require.

### 4. Reconstruct the three-line argument

The 1979 argument can be stated in source-faithful form:

1. There are instances of intense suffering that an omnipotent, omniscient
   being could have prevented without thereby losing some greater good or
   permitting an evil equally bad or worse.
2. An omniscient, wholly good being would prevent any intense suffering it
   could, unless preventing it would lose some greater good or permit an evil
   equally bad or worse.
3. Therefore, no omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being exists.[^3]

The inference is deductively valid. If premises 1 and 2 are true, the
conclusion follows. But the argument remains *evidential* because Rowe's case
for premise 1 is inductive. The validity of the three-line form does not turn
the evidence for its factual premise into a proof.

The structure also explains why the principal dispute focuses on premise 1.
Many theists accept something close to premise 2 as an implication of perfect
goodness. A wholly good agent does not permit preventable intense suffering
for no adequate reason. Denying this indiscriminately risks emptying divine
goodness of recognizable moral content.

Some formulations separate the steps further:

- `A`: no justifying good is apparent for at least some intense suffering;
- `P1`: probably at least some such suffering is gratuitous;
- `P2`: a perfectly good, omniscient being prevents gratuitous suffering; and
- `C`: probably the God of standard theism does not exist.

This expanded form is a curriculum reconstruction, not Rowe's verbatim
numbering. Its value is diagnostic: it makes the inductive bridge visible.
Lecture 2 will examine that bridge.

### 5. The second premise states a necessary condition

Rowe clarifies his theological premise through three possibilities. If a
perfectly good, omniscient being permits preventable intense suffering, then
at least one of the following must obtain:

1. a greater good can be secured only if that suffering is permitted;
2. a greater good can be secured only if either that suffering or an equally
   bad or worse evil is permitted; or
3. preventing that suffering would require permitting an equally bad or worse
   evil.[^3]

These conditions distinguish losing a good from permitting another evil. They
also prevent a common simplification: “A greater good resulted, so permission
was justified.” The good must be connected to the permission in the relevant
way. If the same good could have been obtained without the suffering and
without an equal or worse alternative, simply naming the good does not meet
the premise's test.

Rowe treats these as a *necessary* condition for justified nonprevention, not a
complete *sufficient* condition. The fact that one of the relations exists may
still leave questions of authority, proportionality, distribution, intention,
and the sufferer's good. Premise 2 is therefore morally qualified, but it is
not a complete moral theory.

### 6. The fawn and the appearance of pointlessness

Rowe's famous example concerns a fawn trapped by a naturally caused forest
fire, suffering intensely for several days, and dying without any apparent
observer or human beneficiary.[^1] The case is hypothetical but meant to
represent a familiar kind of natural tragedy. Its design blocks several easy
responses. No human free choice starts the fire. No person observes the event
and grows because of it. The animal does not survive to develop character.
The duration appears preventable by an omnipotent being.

The case does not establish that no unknown good could justify permission.
Rowe acknowledges that an unfamiliar good might exist, or that a familiar good
might be connected to the suffering in a way human observers do not see. He
therefore refuses the claim of certainty.[^1] His question is whether our
experience nevertheless gives rational grounds for the factual premise.

This modesty strengthens the argument. A weak version says, “I cannot imagine
a reason; therefore there is none.” Rowe's version considers the limits of
human knowledge and still argues that inability to prove gratuity does not
eliminate rational support. Ordinary inquiry often proceeds without certainty.
We treat observed features as evidence while remaining open to correction.

The fawn also directs attention from a single case to a larger pattern. Rowe
appeals to the variety and profusion of apparently pointless human and animal
suffering. The evidential force is not merely an emotional reaction to one
story. The argument asks whether it is reasonable to suppose that *every*
apparently gratuitous instance is linked to a greater good or worse-evil
prevention in a way even omnipotence could not improve.[^1]

That cumulative move carries a burden too. A larger and more varied sample can
strengthen an induction, but only if the cases are accurately described and
relevant to the inference. No graded Week 8 task requires assembling tragedy
examples. The verified philosophical sources supply the argument; outside
case research would add distress and verification problems without improving
the assigned analysis.

### 7. Direct attacks: identify a good or challenge the inference

Rowe considers a direct attack on the factual premise: connect suffering to a
good such as moral or spiritual development, or to free choice. He judges this
strategy inadequate as a complete answer. Some suffering greatly exceeds what
appears needed for character formation, and natural suffering is not all the
result of human choice. More importantly, a general good does not show that
every intense instance is necessary for that good.[^4]

A responsible direct reply therefore has at least four tasks:

1. **relevance:** identify a good connected to the kind of suffering at issue;
2. **necessity or alternatives:** explain why a less harmful route would not
   secure the good or prevent the worse evil;
3. **scope:** show whether the account concerns some, many, or all of the cases
   used to support the factual premise; and
4. **moral fit:** address amount, distribution, beneficiary, and the standing
   of the permitting agent.

Possibility can be relevant without being decisive. If a theist gives a
coherent possible reason, that may show that the critic has not proved
gratuity. But Rowe already concedes the absence of proof. The live question is
whether the possible reason sufficiently undercuts the evidence for gratuity.
To answer, one must discuss how likely the reason is, whether it fits the case,
and what detection capacities human observers possess.

### 8. The G. E. Moore shift

Rowe calls an indirect response the theist's best procedure. In general, if
`P` and `Q` entail `R`, then `not-R` and `Q` entail `not-P`. The two arguments
share one premise; deciding between them requires comparing grounds for their
respective first premises.[^4]

Applied here:

1. An omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good God exists.
2. Such a being prevents intense suffering unless prevention would lose a
   greater good or permit an equally bad or worse evil.
3. Therefore, it is not the case that any intense suffering meets Rowe's
   gratuity conditions.

This counterargument is valid if Rowe's is valid. It does not expose a formal
error in Rowe. It relocates the disagreement to comparative warrant: are the
grounds for God's existence stronger than the grounds for accepting the
factual premise?

The shift is therefore not a license to assume the conclusion. A student who
writes “God exists because Christianity is true; therefore the evidence of
evil is irrelevant” has not compared evidence. A disciplined shift identifies
actual grounds for theism, explains their force, considers defeaters, and asks
whether they warrant denying or withholding the factual premise. Because the
assigned debate initially sets those grounds aside, the shift also answers a
broader total-evidence question than Rowe's isolated-evidence question.

### 9. Friendly atheism and rational disagreement

Rowe's 1979 paper ends by distinguishing unfriendly, indifferent, and friendly
atheism. The friendly atheist believes atheism true while allowing that some
theists may be rationally justified in believing theism. False belief and
irrational belief are not identical: people can responsibly reach a false
conclusion when their evidence or access differs.[^5]

This position is particularly useful for `LO-05`. Charitable representation is
not a concession that all positions are true. It requires separating at least
three judgments:

- whether a proposition is true;
- whether an argument supports it; and
- whether a particular person is rationally justified in believing it.

A Christian may similarly believe Rowe's atheistic conclusion false while
recognizing that a reflective atheist could be rationally moved by the
evidence of suffering. That acknowledgment is not relativism. It is a judgment
about the seriousness of evidence and the conditions of responsible belief.
It also protects discussion from motive attacks: neither grief nor faith
decides an argument merely by explaining why someone cares about it.

### 10. Confessional reception without evasion

**Primary-source claim:** Rowe maintains that the logical problem has not been
established, that apparently pointless suffering nevertheless supplies
rational support for atheism, and that a theist's strongest indirect response
appeals to grounds for God's existence.[^1]

**Broad scholarly consensus:** contemporary discussions distinguish logical
and evidential arguments and treat Rowe as a major evidential formulation.
Trakakis's reference entry documents that taxonomy and the extensive dispute
over Rowe's inference.[^3]

**Contested interpretation:** philosophers disagree about whether human failure
to find a justifying reason provides strong evidence that no such reason
exists, and about whether theism or a rival hypothesis predicts the observed
patterns better. Week 10 will examine the cognitive-limit dispute directly.

**Confessional conclusion:** the course affirms the triune God as omnipotent,
omniscient, and good. That confession supplies a reason to reject Rowe's final
conclusion, but it does not excuse careless treatment of the factual premise.
A Christian analysis must ask how creation, fall, providence, judgment,
redemption, and new creation bear on the evidence, while admitting where it
lacks a token explanation.

Job supplies an important boundary. The reader knows more about Job's testing
than Job does, yet the divine speeches do not give Job the case-specific causal
account known to the reader. The friends' confident moral diagnosis is
rejected. Romans 8 joins creation's groaning to hope for liberation, the
Spirit's aid, and inseparable divine love; it does not call suffering good in
itself or identify why each event occurred. These texts resist both despair
and explanatory presumption.

### 11. Moral action under uncertainty

The philosophical question is what a perfectly good God could be justified in
permitting. A human responder usually occupies a different role. The responder
does not possess omniscience, sovereign authority, or a mandate to preserve a
cosmic totality. On available evidence, the ordinary duty is to prevent or
relieve serious harm, report wrongdoing, secure safety, seek medical or mental
health care, and tell the truth.

**Curriculum synthesis:** uncertainty about God's reasons does not generate
permission for human passivity. Even if God could have a reason beyond our
view, it does not follow that *we* have a reason not to intervene. Nor does a
later good retroactively justify an abuser, negligent official, or indifferent
bystander. Divine-permission questions and creaturely-duty questions must be
argued separately.

This boundary is not external to the evidence. It preserves Rowe's recognition
that intense suffering is evil in itself, and it preserves the Christian claim
that love of neighbor seeks the neighbor's good. A conversation with a person
in crisis should begin with presence, safety, listening, and practical care,
not with a speculative identification of God's purpose.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Rowe's evidential atheist:** apparently gratuitous intense suffering and
  its profusion give rational support to the conclusion that the God of narrow
  theism does not exist.
- **Direct theodicist:** argues that known goods or worse-evil prevention make
  at least the relevant suffering non-gratuitous; this response must address
  necessity, alternatives, amount, distribution, and scope.
- **Inference critic:** grants that no reason is apparent but questions whether
  human cognitive access supports the move to probable gratuity.
- **Moorean theist:** accepts the moral premise, begins from independently
  warranted theism, and infers that the factual premise is false.
- **Total-evidence respondent:** may concede that evil is evidence against
  theism while maintaining that other evidence outweighs it.
- **Friendly atheist:** believes atheism true while allowing that some theists
  are rationally justified.

These positions may overlap. An atheist can criticize one Rowean inference; a
theist can concede evidential force; and either may practice intellectual
charity.

## Confessional analysis

The broadly evangelical Protestant framework receives God's power, knowledge,
and goodness as truths, not merely hypotheses adopted for classroom play. Yet
faithful confession does not make evidence irrelevant. The Psalms authorize
lament; Job rejects confident case-specific blame; the cross displays divine
participation in suffering and judgment on evil; Romans 8 describes groaning
and promised liberation; Revelation 21–22 directs hope to the end of death,
pain, and curse.

These claims form a Christian explanatory horizon, but a horizon is not a
verified reason for every event. A confessional response should therefore (1)
state Rowe's argument at full strength, (2) identify where Christian evidence
or doctrine bears on it, (3) distinguish possible from probable and general
from token claims, and (4) preserve lament and practical love under unresolved
questions.

## Summary

Rowe accepts that theism and evil may be logically compatible and asks whether
intense, apparently gratuitous suffering is evidence against the God of
standard theism. His compact argument is valid, while the case for its factual
premise is inductive. The fawn case and the profusion of suffering are meant to
make that premise rational, not certain. Direct replies must do more than name
a generic good, and the Moore shift depends on independently warranted theism
rather than a formal flaw in Rowe. A Christian may reject the conclusion while
acknowledging the argument's force, preserving rational charity, and refusing
to invent a hidden purpose for a sufferer.
