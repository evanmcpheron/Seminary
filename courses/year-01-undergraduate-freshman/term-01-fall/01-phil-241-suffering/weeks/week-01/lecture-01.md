# Concepts and Distinctions

## Learning objectives

After completing this lecture, the student should be able to:

- distinguish pain, suffering, and evil;
- classify moral, natural, and mixed cases while separating causal explanation
  from moral responsibility;
- distinguish logical and evidential problems of evil and defenses and
  theodicies;
- reconstruct the main opening moves in Lewis and Peterson charitably; and
- state both a serious skeptical challenge and a bounded Christian response.

## Key terms

**Pain:** an unpleasant sensory or emotional experience. **Suffering:** a broader
disruption or distress that can involve the body, emotions, relationships,
agency, identity, or perceived meaning. **Evil:** a normatively grave wrong,
privation, harm, or disorder; accounts of what makes something evil are
contested. **Moral evil:** evil for which a moral agent is blameworthy.
**Natural evil:** harmful states or events arising through natural processes
where no human agent is morally responsible for their occurrence. **Logical
problem of evil:** an alleged inconsistency between claims about God and the
existence of evil. **Evidential problem of evil:** an argument that the kinds,
amounts, or distribution of evil count against the probability or
reasonableness of theism. **Defense:** a possible account intended to defeat an
inconsistency or inference without claiming to disclose God's actual reasons.
**Theodicy:** an account that proposes God's actual morally sufficient reasons
for permitting evil. **Lament:** truthful grief or protest addressed to God,
often while relationship with God continues.

## Lecture

### 1. Begin with the reality, then refine the words

The problem of evil is sometimes introduced as a tidy triangle: God is powerful,
God is good, and evil exists. The triangle is useful, but suffering never occurs
as a diagram. A person endures pain, loses someone, suffers betrayal, fears what
will happen next, or sees an animal suffer. Good analysis must therefore do two
things at once. It must use distinctions precise enough to test arguments, and
it must remember that its subject includes realities that can overwhelm people.

**Curriculum synthesis:** Conceptual precision is a form of moral discipline.
Calling every discomfort “evil” can trivialize atrocity; calling severe harm
merely “pain” can conceal its moral and relational dimensions. Yet definitions
can also become evasions. The aim is neither maximum abstraction nor maximum
emotional intensity. The aim is truthful attention.

We will use three related terms. *Pain* names an unpleasant experience, often
bodily but also emotional. *Suffering* is wider: pain can become suffering when
it threatens agency, attachment, identity, hopes, or a person's sense of the
world. Suffering can also occur without acute bodily pain—for example, through
humiliation, isolation, dread, or grief. *Evil* is normative. It says more than
“this hurts” or “I dislike this.” It judges something to be seriously wrong,
disordered, harmful, vicious, or contrary to a good that ought to be present.

These are working distinctions, not complete theories. Some painful acts are
good, such as a medically necessary procedure performed with consent. Some
pleasures are bound up with evil, as when a cruel person enjoys humiliation.
Some suffering follows an innocent accident and is terrible without being a
wrong done by the sufferer. These examples show why pain, suffering, and evil
cannot simply be substituted for one another.

### 2. Moral evil, natural evil, and causal complexity

Philosophers commonly distinguish moral evil from natural evil. Moral evil
results from culpable agency: assault, exploitation, deception, or culpable
neglect. Natural evil names suffering or harm associated with processes for
which no human agent is blameworthy: an earthquake, a congenital disease, or
predation among nonhuman animals are standard examples.[^1]

**Broad scholarly consensus:** The distinction is a standard way to organize
arguments, especially because an appeal to creaturely freedom addresses some
wrong actions more directly than it addresses earthquakes, disease, or animal
suffering. The classification helps a writer identify what a proposed response
actually explains.

**Contested interpretation:** The boundary is not always clean. A storm may be a
natural event, while negligent construction, corrupt emergency planning, and
unequal access to aid greatly increase its harms. Disease can arise naturally
while human choices shape exposure and treatment. Such a case has a mixed
causal history. “Natural” does not mean morally acceptable, and “caused by a
human act” does not by itself establish blame; intention, knowledge, freedom,
duty, and reasonable alternatives matter. Classification should open inquiry,
not end it.

It is also important to distinguish the evil suffered from the sufferer's moral
character. From the fact that someone suffers, it does not follow that the
person caused, deserved, or was spiritually deficient because of the suffering.
Jesus rejects a simple inference from disability to a particular person's sin
in John 9:1–3, and in Luke 13:1–5 he refuses the claim that victims of violence
or disaster were worse offenders than others. Those texts do not answer every
question about providence. They do rule out a common and damaging shortcut.

### 3. What beliefs generate the philosophical problem?

Peterson frames the philosophical problem as the challenge of reconciling evil
with belief in a God who is supremely powerful, intelligent, and good.[^2] In
more usual course vocabulary, *standard theism* affirms that God is omnipotent,
omniscient, and perfectly good, and that God creates and governs the world. The
problem is not created by the bare sentence “suffering exists.” It arises from a
relationship among claims:

1. God is perfectly good.
2. God knows every evil and every relevant way of preventing it.
3. God has power over creation.
4. Severe evils and suffering occur.

A responsible critic asks whether these claims can all be true and, if they can,
whether the actual pattern of suffering is what one should expect if they are.
This is not a cheap objection. It presses on central Christian claims about God
and on realities that include apparently pointless agony.

**Primary-source claim:** Lewis begins chapter 1 not by assuming Christian
belief, but by imagining an atheistic picture of an immense, dangerous universe
in which conscious life appears precarious. He then traces several strands in
religion: experience of the numinous, awareness of moral obligation, the
identification of the numinous power with the source of morality, and, finally,
Christianity's historical claim about Jesus.[^3] The details of Lewis's
historical sketch are not a proof of Christianity. His important structural
point is that a painful world is not yet an intellectual *problem for a good
God* unless one also has reason to believe ultimate reality is righteous.
Christianity therefore sharpens the problem rather than making it disappear.

That is a striking reversal. The problem of pain is an objection to Christian
theism, but one must understand theism before evaluating the objection. Lewis
does not get to evade the problem by pointing out that Christianity made it
possible to formulate. He still owes an account of how the beliefs fit together.
The critic, meanwhile, should criticize the strongest recognizable form of the
belief rather than a deity with attributes Christians do not affirm.

### 4. Logical and evidential questions

The *logical* problem alleges contradiction. In simplified form:

1. If an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good God exists, then no evil exists.
2. Evil exists.
3. Therefore, such a God does not exist.

The conclusion follows if the premises are true and the first premise is stated
strongly enough. The theist may deny premise 1 by arguing that a perfectly good
God could have a morally sufficient reason to permit some evil. At this stage,
the theist need not identify the actual reason for every evil. The logical task
is to show that divine perfection and evil are possibly consistent.

The *evidential* problem is more modest in form and potentially more powerful in
application. It grants, for argument's sake, that God and evil might coexist,
then asks whether the amount, intensity, apparent pointlessness, and distribution
of suffering make theism less likely than a rival account. One can defeat a
strict contradiction without showing that the world is probable on theism.
Conversely, one can think evil is serious evidence against God without claiming
that theism is formally inconsistent.

Week 1 introduces this distinction; later weeks will reconstruct both arguments
with greater care. For now, notice the different burdens. A logical objection
needs an inconsistency. An evidential objection needs a disciplined comparison
of how evidence bears on competing views. Emotional force does not substitute
for either burden—but emotional force may be an appropriate response to the
evil being discussed.

### 5. Defense, theodicy, and restraint

A *defense* offers a possible reason or scenario sufficient to block an alleged
contradiction or inference. A *theodicy* is more ambitious: it proposes to say
what God's actual justifying reasons are. The distinction matters. “It is
possible that God has a good reason” is not equivalent to “I know why God
permitted this death.”

Both projects face objections. A defense can be so unspecified that it protects
any belief from evidence. A theodicy can treat persons as instruments in a neat
story or claim access to divine purposes it does not possess. The critic can
also overreach: showing that a particular theodicy fails does not by itself show
that God does not exist, while showing that a defense is possible does not make
the sufferer's loss good.

The term *apparently gratuitous evil* will recur. It names an evil for which we
cannot identify a morally sufficient justifying reason. Whether “apparently”
supports “actually” is a major dispute. We should neither assume that limited
human knowledge settles the question nor use mystery as a verbal shield against
evidence.

### 6. What omnipotence does—and does not—mean

**Primary-source claim:** In chapter 2, Lewis argues that omnipotence is power to
do all that is intrinsically possible. A contradiction does not name a task
that becomes achievable with more power; it fails to describe a coherent state
of affairs.[^4] A “square circle” is not a very hard circle for God to make.
The words cancel the conditions by which the object would count as either.

This distinction blocks one careless question: “Could God make a creature free
in the relevant sense and at the same time causally determine every one of its
choices?” Depending on the account of freedom, the request may be incoherent.
But one must argue for that conclusion; it cannot simply be announced whenever
a difficulty appears. Disagreement about freedom, possible worlds, natural law,
and divine action remains.

Lewis also connects creaturely life with a shared, relatively stable world.
Distinct agents need an environment in which actions have effects and in which
one person's projects can conflict with another's. A stable order creates the
conditions for meaningful action, but also for collision, frustration, and
harm. This begins an account of why some risk may accompany embodied creaturely
life.

**Contested interpretation:** Lewis's chapter clarifies a limit internal to
coherent action; it does not yet explain why this world contains these evils, or
why God does not prevent a particular horror without contradiction. Nor does it
show that every familiar claim about creaturely freedom is coherent. Treat the
chapter as an opening conceptual proposal, not a completed theodicy.

### 7. Holding argument and lament together

Christian Scripture supplies no permission to rename evil as good. Genesis
1:31 calls God's completed creation very good; Genesis 3 presents rebellion,
alienation, pain, and death as enemies of created flourishing. The Psalms allow
the faithful to ask “How long?” (Ps 13) and even to end in darkness (Ps 88).
Romans 8:18–39 describes creation as groaning and Christian hope as patient
waiting, not denial. Revelation 21:1–5 hopes for the removal of death, mourning,
crying, and pain.

**Confessional conclusion:** Within this curriculum, the triune God is good,
wise, and sovereign; creation is good but fallen; evil is genuinely opposed to
God's good purposes; and the Christian answer culminates in Christ's cross,
bodily resurrection, judgment, and new creation. These commitments frame the
inquiry. They do not entitle the student to invent God's reason for a particular
loss or to silence a critic.

**Curriculum synthesis:** A mature first posture is double refusal: refuse a
vague skepticism that never states an argument, and refuse a vague appeal to
mystery that never faces the evidence. Learn the distinctions. Represent the
critic fairly. Test every proposed answer. Preserve the sufferer's dignity.
Week 1 establishes that grammar; it does not pretend to finish the course.

## Major scholarly positions

- **The skeptical challenge:** The actual kinds and distribution of suffering
  are difficult to reconcile with an all-knowing, all-powerful, perfectly good
  creator; possible compatibility may be too weak a reply.
- **Lewis's opening Christian proposal:** The problem arises within beliefs
  about moral and divine reality; omnipotence does not include contradictions,
  and coherent creaturely action occurs in an ordered world.
- **Analytic classification:** Logical and evidential formulations make
  different claims and impose different argumentative burdens.
- **Caution about classification:** Moral/natural and defense/theodicy
  distinctions clarify inquiry but can hide mixed causes or encourage
  overconfident explanation when applied mechanically.

## Confessional analysis

The course affirms classical Christian theism rather than treating it as a
disposable hypothesis. It also treats the existence of evil as evidence that
must be faced, not explained away by definition. Christian confession gives the
student reason to seek coherence and hope; Scripture gives the student language
for protest, grief, repentance, and waiting. A faithful response may say both
“God is good” and “I do not know why this was permitted.”

## Summary

Pain, suffering, and evil overlap but are not identical. Moral and natural evil
distinguish blameworthy agency from nonculpable natural process, while mixed
cases require causal care. Logical and evidential problems make different
claims; defenses and theodicies accept different burdens. Lewis traces the
beliefs that generate the Christian problem and argues that omnipotence does not
include contradictions. Peterson locates the issue within classical theism and
maps its major forms. These distinctions discipline inquiry but do not settle
why any particular evil occurs.
