# Instructor Key — Week 1 Retrieval Practice

This key supports self-correction or facilitation. The practice is ungraded.
Equivalent accurate wording should be accepted.

1. **Pain, suffering, evil:** Pain is an unpleasant experience; suffering is a
   wider disruption involving such dimensions as agency, relationship,
   identity, or meaning; evil is a normative judgment of grave wrong, harm, or
   disorder. The categories overlap but are not identical.
2. **Wildfire:** The ignition is natural; disabling warnings is blameworthy
   agency if the stated knowledge and duties are established; the resulting
   suffering has a mixed causal history. Do not infer every harm was intended.
3. **Natural is not acceptable:** “Natural” classifies a nonhuman causal process
   in the standard distinction. It neither praises the outcome nor prohibits a
   duty to prevent or relieve harm.
4. **Logical/evidential:** The logical argument seeks incompatibility; the
   evidential argument claims that actual evil lowers the probability or
   reasonableness of theism without requiring contradiction.
5. **Defense/theodicy:** A defense offers a possible account sufficient to block
   an inference; a theodicy claims, more ambitiously, to identify actual divine
   reasons.
6. **Apparently gratuitous:** An evil for which the inquirer cannot identify a
   morally sufficient justifying reason. “Apparently” does not settle whether
   no such reason exists.
7. **Lewis chapter 1:** Numinous awareness; moral awareness; identification of
   the numinous power with the moral source; the distinctively Christian
   historical claim concerning Jesus. Reasonable wording from the student's
   edition is acceptable.
8. **Omnipotence:** God can do all that is intrinsically or coherently possible;
   contradictions do not name tasks that power could perform.
9. **No:** The account supplies a logical constraint. One must still show that
   preventing the particular evil would require a contradiction or sacrifice a
   relevant good.
10. **Peterson's framing:** A supremely powerful, intelligent/knowing, and good
    God who creates or governs a world containing evil.
11. **Sample structure:** (1) If standard theism is true, vast apparently
    pointless suffering is comparatively unexpected. (2) Such suffering is
    observed. (3) Evidence less expected on a hypothesis than on a rival counts
    against that hypothesis, other things equal. (4) Therefore the suffering
    counts against standard theism. Students may separate the comparative
    premise differently but must not turn the argument into a strict
    contradiction.
12. **Lens:** Philosophical inquiry should lead because argument is explicitly
    requested. A useful question is, “Which version of the challenge do you
    regard as strongest, and what standard should count as a rational reply?”
13. **First response:** Acknowledge the loss and respect the request, perhaps
    offering presence or practical help. Avoid an unsolicited theodicy,
    victim-blaming, forced prayer, or debate.
14. **Safety/referral:** Any two well-explained examples: credible imminent harm,
    abuse or safeguarding concern, threat of self-harm or harm to another,
    medical emergency, or a need beyond the responder's competence. Follow
    applicable local procedures; this key does not supply jurisdiction-specific
    legal advice.
15. **General/particular:** A general claim of possible redemption does not
    identify the causal history or God's specific intention in an event.
    Moving to the particular requires evidence not supplied by the general
    doctrine and can become harmful overreach.
