# Guided Discussion — What Kind of Question Is This?

This is ungraded practice. Use the partner/seminar track when another willing
participant is available or the solo track when working independently. Do not
use a real person's private suffering as course material, and do not ask anyone
to disclose trauma or counseling history.

## Goals

- represent a skeptical challenge before evaluating it;
- identify whether philosophical, theological, existential, or pastoral inquiry
  should lead;
- practice disagreement without motive attribution; and
- choose a safe, non-coercive, bounded response.

## Time plan

| Activity | Time |
|---|---:|
| Preparation and role selection | 0.25 h |
| Round 1: argument and charitable restatement | 0.35 h |
| Round 2: cases and response sequencing | 0.55 h |
| Debrief and written synthesis | 0.35 h |
| **Total** | **1.50 h** |

## Preparation and ground rules — 15 minutes

Review the four-question framework in Lecture 2. Agree to the following:

1. synthetic cases only;
2. a participant may pause without explanation;
3. critique claims and inferences, not a person's faith, intelligence, or grief;
4. no diagnosis, forced prayer, or promise of confidentiality beyond one's
   actual role; and
5. urgent safety or abuse concerns leave the exercise and go to appropriate
   local help.

## Round 1: steelman before evaluation — 21 minutes

Speaker A has three minutes to present this claim as strongly as possible:

> The scale and apparently pointless distribution of suffering are more
> surprising if an omniscient, omnipotent, perfectly good God governs the world
> than if no such God exists.

Speaker B may ask clarification questions but may not rebut. Speaker B then
restates the case until Speaker A judges it recognizable and fair. Reverse roles:
Speaker B presents the introductory Lewisian response that omnipotence excludes
contradictions and that meaningful creaturely action occurs within an ordered
world. Speaker A restates it fairly.

Discuss:

- What did each position establish?
- What did neither establish?
- Where would an opponent press next?

## Round 2: response sequencing — 33 minutes

For each synthetic case, decide which lens should lead, which may follow, and
which proposed first response should be rejected.

### Case A: requested debate

Alex says after class, “I am not seeking comfort. I think premise 1 in your
argument is false, and I want to test it.”

- Options: ask for Alex's formulation; offer silent presence only; accuse Alex
  of evading grief; begin a doctrinal monologue.

### Case B: acute loss

Jordan says at a memorial, “If God is good, why did this happen? I cannot discuss
an argument right now.”

- Options: acknowledge the loss and ask what support is welcome; explain
  possible worlds; identify God's hidden purpose; pressure Jordan to pray.

### Case C: possible danger

Riley says, “Someone at home threatened me. I do not feel safe going back
tonight, and I need help.”

- Options: check immediate safety and connect Riley with appropriate local
  emergency or safeguarding support; promise absolute secrecy; investigate the
  allegation yourself; debate divine providence.

For each case, answer:

1. What speech act is occurring?
2. What would count as success in the first five minutes?
3. What information or authority do you lack?
4. What response preserves agency and appropriate boundaries?

## Solo track

Record or write both sides of Round 1, then replay or reread them and revise each
side until it could be affirmed as recognizable by a responsible proponent.
For Round 2, state a first response, a later possible response, and one rejected
response for each case. The solo track uses the same 90-minute budget.

## Debrief and synthesis — 21 minutes

Write 150–200 ungraded words:

- one way philosophical rigor can serve a suffering person;
- one way a correct claim can become pastorally harmful through timing or
  overreach; and
- one sentence you can use to ask what kind of response a speaker wants.

Check the discussion process with the separate instructor/facilitator guide.
The exercise is complete when both the skeptical argument and the Christian
response have been fairly restated and all three cases have a bounded first
response.
