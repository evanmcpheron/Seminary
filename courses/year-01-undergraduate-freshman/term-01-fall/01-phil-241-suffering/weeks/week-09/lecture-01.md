# Draper on the Distribution of Pain and Pleasure

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture, the student should be able to:

1. distinguish Draper's argument from Rowe's argument and from a logical
   contradiction claim;
2. define Draper's theism, Hypothesis of Indifference, and observation report
   O without broadening or narrowing them;
3. explain explanatory advantage as a difference in how surprising O would be
   if each rival hypothesis were true;
4. distinguish antecedent epistemic probability from temporal priority and
   from statistical frequency;
5. reconstruct Draper's division of O into O1, O2, and O3;
6. explain the roles of biological usefulness, pathological experience, and
   biological appropriateness;
7. state why Draper thinks the distribution of both pain and pleasure favors
   HI over theism;
8. identify what the likelihood conclusion establishes and what it leaves for
   a later total-evidence judgment; and
9. evaluate the argument confessionally without minimizing suffering or
   inventing a divine purpose for a particular event.

## Key terms

**Theism:** Draper's statement that an omnipotent, omniscient, morally perfect
person created the universe.

**Hypothesis of Indifference (HI):** the hypothesis that neither the nature nor
the condition of earthly sentient beings results from benevolent or malevolent
actions by nonhuman persons. HI is inconsistent with Draper's theism but does
not by itself assert naturalism or deny every supernatural being.

**Observation report O:** a statement collecting one's observations of human
and animal pain and pleasure together with encountered testimony reporting
others' observations.

**Explanatory power:** in the comparison relevant here, the degree to which an
observation is expected rather than surprising on a hypothesis.

**Antecedent epistemic probability:** an observation's rational probability on
a hypothesis when assessed independently of the observation and testimony
reported by that observation. Antecedent means evidentially independent here,
not earlier in calendar time.

**Biological usefulness:** Draper's category for a feature that contributes
nonaccidentally to a goal of an organic system, such as survival or
reproduction.

**Biologically gratuitous pain or pleasure:** pain or pleasure that does not
serve a biological goal in Draper's sense. This is not identical with Rowe's
moral category of gratuitous suffering.

**Pathological pain or pleasure:** pain or pleasure arising from an organic
system's failure to function properly.

**Biologically appropriate pain or pleasure:** an experience occurring in a
type of situation in which having that response is biologically useful, even
if the particular experience does not help the individual.

**Prima facie reason:** a reason with genuine rational force unless it is
defeated or outweighed by other reasons.

## Lecture

### 1. A new evidential strategy

Week 8 studied William Rowe's movement from apparently gratuitous suffering to
the probable existence of at least one genuinely gratuitous evil. Paul Draper
develops a different evidential strategy. He does not begin by asking whether
an observer can identify God's morally sufficient reason for a particular
case. He asks which of two rival hypotheses makes a broad body of observations
about pain and pleasure less surprising.[^1]

The shift matters. Rowe focuses on an inference from appearance to actuality:
no justifying good is apparent, so perhaps no justifying good exists. Draper
focuses on distribution and function: much pain and pleasure occurs in patterns
connected to the biological goals of organisms, while much other pain and
pleasure is pathological or biologically gratuitous. Would that mix be more
expected if a perfectly good creator were directly responsible for sentient
life, or if the well-being of earthly sentient beings were not the object of
benevolent or malevolent nonhuman action?

Draper calls the problem evidential, not logical. His observation report is not
alleged to contradict theism. Theism can be supplemented with possible reasons
for allowing pain. The issue is comparative: accommodation is weaker than
prediction. Two hypotheses may both permit an observation while one gives us
more reason to expect it.

Consider an ordinary example. Both “a sprinkler ran” and “a water truck passed”
could accommodate wet grass. The location, shape, and timing of the water may
fit one explanation better. One does not ask only whether the evidence is
possible on each hypothesis. One asks how expected the evidence would be on
each, holding the evidence itself out of the background used to make the
assessment.

### 2. Keep both hypotheses exact

Draper's theism states that there exists an omnipotent, omniscient, morally
perfect person who created the universe.[^1] This is close to Rowe's narrow
theism and to central features of classical Christian belief. The argument
therefore cannot be answered by quietly replacing God with a finite creator or
an indifferent deity. Such a revision changes the target.

HI requires equal care. It states that neither the nature nor the condition of
sentient beings on earth results from benevolent or malevolent actions
performed by nonhuman persons.[^1] HI is consistent with naturalism, but it does
not entail naturalism. It also permits the existence of supernatural beings,
provided their actions motivated by concern or hostility do not account for
earthly sentient nature and condition.

Three errors are common:

1. “HI means there is no God.” That is too broad. HI entails the denial of
   Draper's theism, but its content is more specific.
2. “HI means nature is cruel.” That imports malevolence. HI says indifferent,
   not hostile.
3. “Theism means every pain has an obvious moral lesson.” That is too narrow.
   Draper's theism includes divine attributes and creation, not a complete
   theodicy.

Comparison depends on the actual contents of the hypotheses. If a student
silently substitutes naturalism for HI or a detailed Christian theology for
bare theism, the probabilities being compared have changed.

### 3. What observation O includes

Draper lets O report observations of humans and animals experiencing physical
or mental pain and pleasure, together with testimony one has encountered about
such observations.[^1] O is intentionally broad. It includes pleasure as well
as pain and nonhuman animals as well as moral agents. This protects the
argument from the simplistic claim that the world contains only misery.

O should not be misunderstood as an exhaustive scientific census. It is an
observation report relative to an epistemic situation. Different people can
possess different testimony and therefore differ in epistemic probability.
Draper explicitly classifies the probabilities as epistemic rather than
statistical, physical, or logical.[^1]

That relativity does not make the argument merely subjective. Evidence can be
better or worse, testimony can be more or less reliable, and an assessment can
be corrected. The point is that rational probability is indexed to what an
inquirer responsibly knows, not that any felt confidence is automatically
justified.

The content boundary also matters ethically. O reports patterns of sentient
experience. The course need not collect graphic cases, compare people's trauma,
or estimate a cosmic quantity of pain. The assigned source supplies the
philosophical structure. Our task is to analyze how the reported distribution
functions as evidence.

### 4. Explanation as relative expectedness

Draper formulates his central conclusion in probabilistic terms:

P(O given HI) is much greater than P(O given theism).[^1]

Read this statement as a likelihood comparison. It says that, independently
of the observations and testimony included in O, we have much more reason to
expect O if HI is true than if theism is true. Draper connects this sense of
explanation with comparative surprise: O is much less surprising on HI.

The qualification independently of O prevents circular reasoning. One cannot
use O to build a detailed hypothesis that exactly matches O and then announce
that O confirms the hypothesis. The assessment asks what each hypothesis and
the admissible background give us reason to expect before O does its
evidential work.

Antecedent does not mean earlier in time. A person may assess an old
observation today. What matters is evidential independence. Nor is P(O given H)
a frequency measured across repeated universes. Draper treats it as epistemic:
how reasonable O is to expect on H from the relevant information.

This conclusion is not yet the claim that HI is more probable overall. A
hypothesis can predict one body of evidence better while another body of
evidence favors its rival. Lecture 2 will distinguish likelihoods, priors, and
posterior judgments. For now, preserve the modest but important claim: if O is
much more expected on HI, O is evidence favoring HI over theism.

### 5. Biological usefulness and intrinsic value

Draper's central explanatory proposal concerns the biological role of pain and
pleasure. Organic systems are goal-directed in the limited biological sense
that their parts and responses contribute to such goals as survival and
reproduction. A feature is biologically useful when it makes such a
contribution nonaccidentally.[^1]

Pain can warn an organism away from damage. Pleasure can reinforce activities
that serve biological goals. Yet pain and pleasure differ from many other
organic features because they also possess intrinsic moral value: pain is bad
for the subject in itself, while pleasure is good in itself, other things being
equal. Biological function and moral value can therefore come apart.

That distinction drives the comparison. On HI, Draper argues, there is no
antecedent reason to expect the moral value of pain and pleasure to prevent
them from playing the same sort of biological role that other organic features
play. A biological pattern is just what one might expect if sentient nature is
not the result of nonhuman benevolence or malevolence.

On theism, however, a morally perfect creator needs morally sufficient reasons
to permit pain. Omnipotence also means that biological goals do not make the
present pain-pleasure system unavoidable merely because finite organisms now
depend on it. Consequently, the existence of biological function does not by
itself give as strong an antecedent reason to expect this system on theism.

This is a comparison of expectations, not the claim that biological functions
are unreal or worthless. A Christian can affirm that warning responses support
creaturely life while still asking whether a perfectly powerful and good God
would choose this distribution.

### 6. O1: biologically useful experience among moral agents

Draper divides O into three components. O1 concerns pain and pleasure
experienced by moral agents that we know to be biologically useful.[^1] Humans
are the central example. Given independent knowledge that humans are
goal-directed organisms, HI gives reason to expect pain and pleasure, if they
exist, to contribute systematically to biological goals.

Draper argues that P(O1 given HI) is much greater than P(O1 given theism). On
HI, intrinsic badness and goodness do not introduce a benevolent nonhuman
agent whose moral purposes might redirect the system. On theism, by contrast,
God could create goal-directed organisms without this precise use of pain and
pleasure and would permit pain only for morally sufficient reasons. Those
reasons might or might not align systematically with biological function.

The step is contestable. A theist can argue that embodied, socially dependent,
rational creatures are valuable and that fitting affective responses belong
to their flourishing. If that claim is not simply read off O, it could raise
the expectedness of biologically functional pain and pleasure on theism.
Draper's reply is that omnipotence leaves alternatives and that pleasure is
worth producing even when it lacks further biological utility, while a good
creator would seek valuable goals with as little pain as possible.[^1]

The disagreement is not settled by naming design. “Designed for survival” can
describe the observed function without explaining why a morally perfect
designer would select the particular experiential cost. Conversely, identifying
an alternative in imagination does not prove it feasible together with every
other divine purpose. Both sides bear a comparative burden.

### 7. O2: nonmoral sentient beings

O2 concerns biologically useful pain and pleasure among sentient beings that
are not moral agents, including young children and nonhuman animals.[^1]
Draper's point is conditional. Given O1 and biological similarity, HI leads us
to expect related sentient organisms to share biologically useful experience.

Theism plus O1 also provides some basis for expecting similarity, but Draper
argues that the expectation is weaker. A moral-purpose account available for
mature agents may not transfer to subjects incapable of moral decision.
Biological pain can serve survival while failing to advance the subject's moral
agency. Draper therefore rates O2 as somewhat more probable on HI with O1 than
on theism with O1.

This component connects with Week 6's animal-suffering pressure. Human free
choice may explain some moral evil, but it does not automatically explain why
nonmoral creatures undergo a pain system or why pre-moral children bear its
costs. The point is not that such suffering is certainly morally gratuitous.
It is that an explanation centered on the sufferer's responsible moral choice
has restricted reach.

A theist may appeal to creaturely solidarity, stable causal order, ecosystem
goods, goods of relationship, or an eschatological whole. Those proposals
deserve evaluation. They must still show how they alter the antecedent
expectation and why the proposed goods fit the amount, distribution, subjects,
and available alternatives. Possibility alone does less work in an evidential
comparison than it does against a strict contradiction claim.

### 8. O3: what is not known to be biologically useful

O3 concerns pain and pleasure not known to be biologically useful. This class
includes experiences known not to serve a biological goal and experiences
whose biological usefulness is unknown.[^1] Draper argues that O3 is much more
probable on HI together with O1 and O2 than on theism together with those
components.

The first reason concerns happiness and moral order. Given theism, Draper
thinks we have more antecedent reason to expect sentient beings, especially
nonhuman animals, to be happier than O reports. He also expects a clearer
connection between certain moral goods, such as virtue and justice, and
biologically gratuitous pain and pleasure. The observed distribution does not
display that connection clearly.

The second reason concerns the kind of irregularity O3 exhibits. Much pain or
pleasure not biologically useful to the individual is nevertheless
pathological or biologically appropriate. Pathological experience arises when
an organic system fails. Biologically appropriate experience occurs in a
kind of situation where the response is generally useful, even if the token
experience does not aid the subject.

Suppose a warning system produces pain during damage too severe to escape. The
token pain does not secure survival, yet the response is appropriate to a
class of situations in which warning often helps. Such spillover is intelligible
if pain and pleasure are fundamentally biological systems that sometimes
malfunction or operate without benefiting the present individual. Draper
argues that HI gives stronger reason than theism to expect precisely that
pattern.[^1]

Do not confuse biologically gratuitous with morally gratuitous. The first says
the experience fails to contribute nonaccidentally to an organic goal. A
theist might still claim a moral, relational, spiritual, or eschatological
reason. Draper's point is comparative: the prevalence and shape of experiences
without biological utility fit an indifferent biological system better unless
a theistic expansion significantly raises their expectedness.

### 9. The three components form one distributional case

Because O is equivalent to O1 together with O2 and O3, Draper compares their
conditional probabilities in sequence.[^1] In simplified form:

- O1 is, he argues, much more expected on HI than on theism;
- O2, given O1, is somewhat more expected on HI than on theism; and
- O3, given O1 and O2, is much more expected on HI than on theism.

The sequence avoids counting the same evidence as though the components were
independent. Each later probability is conditioned on the earlier components.
Lecture 2 will use this feature to discuss evidence dependence and double
counting.

The strongest feature of the argument is its attention to pattern. Pleasure
does not simply cancel pain. Useful pain does not simply answer harmful pain.
The question is why experiences with intrinsic value appear so systematically
tied to biological mechanisms, why similar mechanisms extend to nonmoral
subjects, and why their failures and excesses retain the shape of those
mechanisms.

A critic may dispute one or more comparative assessments. Perhaps a robust
theology of embodied creaturely goods makes O1 highly expected. Perhaps a
theology of common creaturely life raises O2. Perhaps fall, providence, and
eschatological redemption change the expectation of O3. The critic must show
how these proposals affect the likelihoods rather than merely show that O and
theism can coexist.

### 10. What the conclusion does and does not establish

**Primary-source claim:** Draper argues that the antecedent epistemic
probability of O on HI is much greater than its antecedent epistemic
probability on theism, assuming theodicies do not significantly raise the
latter.[^1]

From this, Draper infers that O supplies a prima facie epistemic reason to
prefer HI and to reject or refrain from believing theism unless countervailing
evidence offsets it.[^1] The second move introduces questions of prior
probability and total evidence that require care. Lecture 2 will make those
questions explicit.

The likelihood result does not establish:

- a logical contradiction between God and pain;
- that every pain is morally gratuitous;
- that HI is the only alternative to theism;
- that naturalism follows directly from O;
- that no evidence supports theism;
- that every theist is irrational; or
- that pain and pleasure possess no goods or biological functions.

It does establish a serious burden if its comparative judgments are correct.
A theist must identify a flaw in the evidence, the hypothesis definitions, the
conditional comparisons, or the significance assigned to the likelihood
advantage. Simply repeating that God might have a reason leaves the comparative
surprise largely untouched.

### 11. The strongest initial responses

**Contested interpretation:** philosophers dispute whether HI and theism are
specified at comparable levels of detail. HI makes a focused negative claim
about benevolent or malevolent nonhuman action, while theism asserts a
particular creator. One might argue that HI gains an unfair advantage by
remaining permissive about many underlying mechanisms. Draper answers in part
that HI is not obviously ad hoc and has few positive ontological commitments,
but the comparison-class question remains important.[^1]

A second response expands theism. A Christian does not confess bare theism
alone but a doctrine of good creation, human rebellion, common creaturely
order, divine providence, redemption through Christ, resurrection, judgment,
and new creation. If this larger account is independently supported and gives
reason to expect a groaning but redeemable creation, it may raise the
probability of O on the Christian hypothesis.

Yet detail can be cheap. An expansion invented after seeing O may fit O without
receiving independent support. It must be plausible given theism and must
actually predict the pattern better. Draper examines free-will and
infinite-intellect expansions precisely because adding a possible story is not
enough.[^1]

A third response appeals to human cognitive limits. If divine goods and their
connections vastly exceed human knowledge, perhaps O would remain hard to
explain even on theism. Draper replies that unknown divine knowledge could
include better routes that avoid pain as readily as unknown goods requiring
it. Ignorance alone does not point expectation toward O.[^1] Week 10 will give
the skeptical-theist response its own full hearing.

### 12. Confessional judgment and moral boundaries

**Confessional conclusion:** this curriculum affirms the triune Creator as
omnipotent, omniscient, and good, so it rejects HI as the final account of
sentient existence. That confession does not make Draper's evidence disappear.
His argument usefully exposes the weakness of explanations that point to any
biological function or later good without testing alternatives, distribution,
and the good of the sufferer.

Scripture gives a thicker account than bare theism. Genesis 1–3 distinguishes
good creation from human rebellion and curse. Romans 8:18–39 describes creation
as groaning, awaiting liberation, while locating Christian hope in God's
redemptive action and inseparable love. Mark 15 places unjust suffering within
God's saving action without calling the injustice good. Revelation 21–22
directs hope toward judgment, healed creation, and the end of death and pain.

These themes can contribute to a total-evidence Christian response. They do
not yield a numerical likelihood, prove why this distribution was necessary,
or disclose God's reason for a particular injury. A biblical story can make a
kind of world intelligible while leaving token questions unresolved.

**Curriculum synthesis:** the distributional argument concerns divine
permission; it does not authorize human indifference. The fact that pain can
serve a biological warning function is a reason to respond to danger, not to
leave it untreated. A possible divine purpose is never evidence that a limited
human should withhold safety, medical care, mental-health care, protection,
reporting, justice, or practical aid.

When speaking with a sufferer, do not begin with O1, O2, and O3. These are
classroom categories for evaluating hypotheses, not labels to impose on a
person's experience. Listen, secure immediate safety, acknowledge harm, and
respect professional and legal boundaries. Philosophical comparison may
continue without turning a person into a data point.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Draper's HI comparison:** the biologically patterned distribution of pain
  and pleasure is much more probable on HI than on theism and therefore
  supplies prima facie evidence against theism.
- **Theistic expansion response:** a sufficiently plausible and predictive
  account of divine aims may raise the expectedness of O on theism.
- **Cognitive-limit response:** large differences between divine and human
  knowledge may undercut human confidence about what theism predicts.
- **Comparison-class objection:** the evidential result may depend on whether
  HI and theism are specified at appropriately comparable levels.
- **Total-evidence response:** O may favor HI locally while independent
  evidence still favors theism overall.
- **Confessional critical response:** Christian doctrine supplies a thicker
  account than bare theism but must be independently grounded, evidentially
  disciplined, and pastorally bounded.

## Confessional analysis

Christian confession gives reason to expect both created goods and a history
in which evil is opposed, judged, and overcome. It therefore resists the
interpretation of nature as ultimately indifferent. At the same time, the
canonical themes of lament and groaning forbid a simple inference from
biological function to moral justification.

A faithful analysis should say which part of Draper's comparison it addresses.
Creation and embodiment may affect the likelihood of O1. Creaturely solidarity
and common natural order may affect O2. Fall and eschatological redemption may
affect O3. None of these labels does evidential work until its connection,
independent support, alternatives, and scope are explained.

## Summary

Draper compares theism with HI by asking which makes the observed distribution
of pain and pleasure less surprising. O includes human and animal pain and
pleasure reported through observation and testimony. O1 concerns biologically
useful experience among moral agents, O2 the corresponding experience among
nonmoral sentient beings, and O3 experience not known to be biologically
useful. Draper argues that the biological pattern, its extension beyond moral
agents, and its pathological or biologically appropriate spillover are more
expected on HI. This creates a prima facie evidential problem if the likelihood
comparison succeeds. It does not by itself settle priors, total evidence, or
the rationality of every believer. A Christian response must explain how its
larger theology changes expectation while refusing unsupported token purposes
and preserving practical care.
