# Evidence, Priors, and Explanatory Comparison

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture, the student should be able to:

1. state when evidence favors one hypothesis over another;
2. distinguish likelihood, prior probability, and posterior probability;
3. use a qualitative form of Bayes's theorem without inventing numerical
   precision;
4. explain how the choice and specification of comparison hypotheses affect an
   evidential argument;
5. reconstruct Draper's Weighted Average Principle at an introductory level;
6. state the two tests a theistic expansion must pass to improve theism's
   explanatory position;
7. present Draper's treatment of moral-progress, responsibility, and
   infinite-intellect responses accurately;
8. compare Rowe's case-based induction with Draper's distributional likelihood
   argument;
9. identify evidence dependence and avoid double counting; and
10. formulate a bounded Christian total-evidence response that preserves
    intellectual honesty and care for sufferers.

## Key terms

**Likelihood:** the probability of evidence E on the assumption that a
hypothesis H is true, written P(E given H). A likelihood treats the hypothesis
as given and asks how expected the evidence would be.

**Prior probability:** the rational probability assigned to a hypothesis before
the evidence currently being evaluated is incorporated.

**Posterior probability:** the rational probability assigned to a hypothesis
after updating on the evidence.

**Prior odds:** the comparative prior probability of one hypothesis against a
rival.

**Likelihood ratio:** the comparative support supplied by evidence, represented
by P(E given H1) divided by P(E given H2).

**Qualitative Bayesian update:** a direction-and-strength judgment about how
evidence should change comparative confidence without assigning unsupported
numbers.

**Comparison class:** the set of rival hypotheses among which evidence is being
evaluated.

**Expansion:** in Draper's usage, a statement that entails a less detailed
hypothesis while adding claims relevant to explanation.

**Auxiliary hypothesis:** an additional claim joined to a core hypothesis to
derive or raise expectations about evidence.

**Weighted Average Principle (WAP):** Draper's principle that the probability
of O on theism is the weighted average of O's probability on a theistic
expansion and on theism without that expansion, with weights set by how
probable the expansion is given theism.

**Predictive fit:** the degree to which a hypothesis or expansion gives reason
to expect the observed evidence, including liabilities as well as successes.

**Ad hoc rescue:** an auxiliary claim adopted mainly to protect a hypothesis
from known evidence while lacking adequate independent support or wider
explanatory value.

**Double counting:** treating the same evidential contribution as independent
support more than once.

## Lecture

### 1. Evidence can favor without proving

Evidence favors H1 over H2 when it is more expected on H1 than on H2. In
symbols:

P(E given H1) is greater than P(E given H2).

This relation is comparative. It does not say that E is impossible on H2. It
does not say that H1 entails E. It does not yet say that H1 is more probable
than H2 overall. It identifies the direction in which this body of evidence
pushes a comparison.

Suppose two medical hypotheses both permit a laboratory result, but one makes
the result routine while the other makes it unusual. The result favors the
first, even though it proves neither. Further evidence about prevalence,
measurement reliability, other symptoms, and alternative diagnoses may change
the final judgment. The same structure applies to Draper. If O is much more
probable on HI than on theism, O favors HI over theism even though theism can
accommodate O.

The phrase much more probable needs argument. It cannot be established merely
by the speaker's surprise. Lecture 1 reconstructed Draper's reasons from
biological usefulness, moral value, nonmoral sentience, pathological
experience, and biologically appropriate spillover. A student may judge those
reasons insufficient, but must address them at their actual points of contact.

### 2. Likelihood is not posterior probability

Three probabilities must remain distinct:

1. P(E given H): the likelihood of the evidence on the hypothesis;
2. P(H): the prior probability of the hypothesis relative to background
   knowledge; and
3. P(H given E): the posterior probability after updating on E.

Reversing the terms is a serious error. P(O given theism) asks how expected O
would be if theism were true. P(theism given O) asks how probable theism is
after O is included. The first contributes to the second but is not identical
with it.

A familiar diagnostic example helps. A medical test might produce a positive
result frequently when a disease is present. That high likelihood does not by
itself tell a patient how likely the disease is after a positive result. The
base rate and the rate of positive results without the disease also matter.
Likewise, a low P(O given theism) cannot alone supply P(theism given O).

Draper's central C is a likelihood claim: P(O given HI) is much greater than
P(O given theism). He then argues that this supplies a prima facie reason to
regard theism as less probable and to reject it unless offsetting evidence is
available.[^1] That later significance claim moves into comparative priors and
total evidence. The likelihood argument can be strong while the final
epistemic judgment remains contested.

### 3. Bayes's theorem as a discipline, not a number generator

For two rival hypotheses, a useful form of Bayes's theorem says:

posterior odds = prior odds multiplied by the likelihood ratio.

In this course, the formula is chiefly a bookkeeping discipline. It requires
the analyst to keep three questions separate:

1. Which hypothesis had greater rational support before this evidence?
2. Which hypothesis makes this evidence more expected, and by how much?
3. After the update, which hypothesis is better supported on the evidence now
   included?

No Week 9 task asks the student to assign decimals. Without a defensible model,
“P(theism) equals 0.62” merely disguises intuition as measurement. A qualitative
assessment can still be rigorous:

- the evidence is irrelevant because the likelihoods are equal;
- the evidence weakly favors H1;
- the evidence substantially favors H1;
- H2 has a prior advantage that may offset the evidence;
- the available prior comparison is indeterminate; or
- the total-evidence verdict remains suspended.

Qualitative language requires reasons and limits. “Strong evidence” should
identify what makes the likelihood ratio large. “High prior” should identify
the background evidence or theoretical virtue that supports it. A number is
not a substitute for either explanation.

### 4. Priors cannot contain the evidence twice

Draper defines the relevant probabilities antecedently to O: independently of
the observations and testimony O reports.[^1] This guards against using O in
the background to assign a low prior to theism and then using O again as new
evidence against theism.

The same warning applies to the Christian respondent. One cannot include a
belief in God's goodness within a prior solely because one already discounted
suffering through an assumed divine reason, then cite that prior as independent
counterevidence to suffering. Independent grounds must be genuinely distinct
or their dependence must be modeled openly.

Evidence may overlap without becoming useless. Scripture, religious
experience, moral knowledge, testimony, and philosophical arguments can
interact. The task is to identify dependence. If two witnesses repeat the same
unverified report, they are not two independent confirmations. If a theological
claim is inferred from the very doctrine under dispute, it cannot simply be
counted as external support.

**Curriculum synthesis:** an honest total-evidence argument labels the source of
each contribution, the way it depends on other beliefs, and the confidence
appropriate to it. This is more important than filling a probability table
with invented numbers.

### 5. The comparison class controls the question

Draper compares theism with HI, not theism with every conceivable worldview.
HI is logically inconsistent with theism because it denies that the condition
or nature of earthly sentient beings results from benevolent nonhuman action.
Yet HI is consistent with both naturalistic and some supernaturalistic
hypotheses.[^1]

This breadth gives HI an advantage and a liability. It can accommodate many
underlying accounts and carries few positive ontological commitments. Draper
argues that it is not obviously ad hoc and that theism is a more specific
claim.[^1] A critic may answer that a permissive disjunction predicts less than
a detailed positive theory. If HI groups many possibilities with different
expectations, how should their weights be assigned?

The objection must be precise. It is not enough to say that HI is atheistic and
therefore biased. Nor is it enough for Draper to say that theism is specific
and therefore improbable. The analyst asks:

- Are the hypotheses mutually exclusive?
- Are they exhaustive, or is the conclusion limited to this pair?
- Are they specified at comparable levels?
- Is background knowledge applied symmetrically?
- Does one hypothesis gain fit by leaving mechanisms unspecified?
- Would a more specific rival change the likelihood comparison?

A favorable comparison with HI supports the denial of Draper's theism because
HI entails that denial. It does not automatically establish naturalism, since
HI itself does not entail naturalism.

### 6. Explanatory detail can help or merely rescue

A bare hypothesis often needs auxiliary claims to generate predictions. Theism
alone says that a morally perfect creator exists, but it does not specify every
divine purpose, created order, or permitted history. A Christian theology adds
claims about creation, fall, providence, redemption, church, resurrection,
judgment, and new creation.

Additional detail can improve explanation. It can also overfit. Imagine adding
the claim “the creator intended exactly the distribution we observe” after
seeing O. The expanded statement entails theism and fits O, but it has little
independent support and no clear predictive risk. It protects rather than
explains.

A responsible expansion passes two tests:

1. **Internal or prior plausibility:** Is the expansion reasonably probable
   given the core hypothesis and evidence independent of O?
2. **Predictive improvement:** Does the expansion make O more expected, taking
   account of both successes and liabilities?

It also should connect naturally to the core view, avoid needless complexity,
and illuminate more than the single datum it was introduced to save. These are
explanatory virtues, not mechanical scorecards.

The same standards apply to HI. If an advocate adds a detailed mechanism only
after encountering a feature favorable to theism, that expansion also requires
independent support and predictive discipline.

### 7. Draper's Weighted Average Principle

Draper calls a more detailed statement Tn an expansion of theism when Tn is
known to entail theism.[^1] He then uses the Weighted Average Principle:

P(O given theism) =
P(Tn given theism) multiplied by P(O given Tn)
plus
P(not-Tn given theism) multiplied by P(O given theism and not-Tn).

The expression partitions theism into two cases: the expansion is true, or it
is not. O's probability on theism is the weighted average of O's probability
in those cases.

The principle clarifies why a possible theodicy is not enough. If Tn is highly
improbable given theism, even a strong fit between Tn and O may barely affect
P(O given theism). If Tn is probable given theism but does not make O more
expected than theism without Tn, it also contributes little. To raise the
overall likelihood significantly, the expansion must have enough weight and
enough predictive advantage.

This is an introductory use of the formula. The Week 9 assignment does not ask
the student to calculate a number. It asks for a two-column audit:

- reasons the expansion belongs plausibly within theism; and
- observations it predicts better or worse.

### 8. Free will and moral advancement

Draper examines a free-will expansion centered on a favorable balance of
freely performed right actions over wrong actions.[^1] He grants for argument
that the relevant incompatibilist freedom is coherent and valuable. The
expansion may predict pain that influences right action or is logically
necessary for some right actions.

That is a predictive success. Draper then considers liabilities. Pain also
influences wrong actions and can be involved in wrongdoing. Conditions such as
illness, poverty, and ignorance may demoralize, while conditions promoting
moral action are often absent. He argues that these liabilities offset the
success, so the expansion does not significantly raise P(O given theism).[^1]

The force of this assessment depends on the expansion's actual aim. A
respondent might say that divine purposes include not merely a favorable
aggregate balance but authentic relationships, particular virtues, justice,
mercy, or a redemptive history. That answer changes Tn and must be evaluated
again. It cannot inherit a favorable probability simply by being more
theologically rich.

The exchange also revives the necessity question. Some morally right actions
respond to pain, but it does not follow that the actual range and distribution
of pain was required. Nor does the good action of a rescuer make the injury
good in itself or show that the harmed person bears a justified cost.

### 9. Free will and serious responsibility

A second free-will expansion says that one of God's final aims is for humans to
have freedom to make very important moral decisions, including decisions with
serious effects on others.[^1] This could raise the expectedness of suffering
for which humans are responsible.

Draper presses two limits. First, an account of moral responsibility does not
automatically explain amoral pain: suffering for which no human is morally
responsible. Attempts to make such pain necessary for learning how to cause or
prevent harm face alternative-method and amount objections. An omnipotent God
might provide relevant knowledge by less costly means.[^1]

Second, Draper uses a good-parent analogy against unlimited responsibility.
Responsible parents increase a child's authority as capacity matures and
reduce it when it is persistently abused. The observation report contains
grave harms produced by agents unworthy of such power and no obvious
corresponding moral progress. Draper argues that the expansion generates
expectations the world does not satisfy.[^1]

A defender can dispute the analogy, the assessment of moral progress, or the
possibility of divine intervention without undermining meaningful agency. A
strong response must say which. “Freedom is valuable” does not yet show why
this degree of power, this distribution of vulnerability, and these limits on
intervention best serve it.

The moral boundary is firm: divine permission never exculpates the wrongdoer.
If a perpetrator acts freely, that agency grounds rather than removes human
responsibility. A theodicy about God's permission is not a defense of the
human act.

### 10. The infinite-intellect response

The infinite-intellect response emphasizes that an omniscient God understands
goods and relations far beyond human knowledge. If God exists, human inability
to explain O might be expected. This resembles the cognitive-limit strategy
previewed in Week 8.

Draper grants that God might know morally sufficient reasons unknown to us. He
argues that unknown knowledge cuts both ways: God might also know unknown
goods that require preventing the pain, or unknown means of achieving goods
without it.[^1] Ignorance by itself does not raise P(O given theism). HI, he
adds, gives even more reason to expect human failure to produce a successful
theistic explanation.

This is a serious reply to a bare appeal to mystery. “We do not know” does not
predict pain instead of its prevention. For cognitive limitation to undercut
the comparison, it needs a more specific principle about why humans should not
expect access to the relevant likelihoods at all.

Week 10 will examine that stronger skeptical-theist move. It will ask whether
human limitations block the probability comparison and whether the same
skepticism threatens ordinary moral reasoning. Week 9 should neither assume
that skeptical theism succeeds nor dismiss it on the basis of Draper's brief
treatment.

### 11. Rowe and Draper use different bridges

Rowe's compact argument centers on the claim that at least some intense
suffering is gratuitous: an omnipotent being could prevent it without losing a
greater good or permitting an equal or worse evil. The main epistemic dispute
concerns the move from no known justifying reason to probably no such reason.

Draper does not need to classify a token suffering as morally gratuitous. His
central evidence is the distribution of pain and pleasure and its relation to
biological function. The bridge is comparative expectedness: the pattern is
claimed to be much more likely on HI than on theism.

| Feature | Rowe | Draper |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence unit | apparently gratuitous intense suffering, particular and cumulative | broad observation report about pain and pleasure |
| Central rival claim | at least one suffering instance is genuinely gratuitous | HI explains O much better than theism |
| Main bridge | appearance and responsible search support probable absence | relative likelihood supports comparative evidential favoring |
| Unknown reasons | may undercut inference from appearance | must change predicted distribution, not merely remain possible |
| Initial conclusion | probable gratuity and an atheistic conclusion with the moral premise | O favors HI and supplies a prima facie reason against theism |

The arguments can reinforce each other, but they must not be merged without
notice. If the same animal suffering evidence appears in both, the analyst must
ask whether it supplies two independent reasons or one body of evidence
organized in two ways.

### 12. Dependence and double counting

Draper's partition of O into O1, O2, and O3 conditions later components on
earlier ones. This matters because related evidence cannot simply be
multiplied as though independent.[^1] Biological similarity may make O2 likely
after O1 is known. Counting the full force of O1 again inside O2 would
overstate the combined support.

The same risk appears in a comparative paper. A student may list:

- animal pain;
- the extension of pain systems to nonmoral agents;
- Rowe's animal example; and
- Draper's O2.

Those are not automatically four independent pieces of evidence. They may be
different descriptions or argumentative uses of overlapping facts. The paper
should identify the shared base and explain any genuinely new contribution.

Double counting can also occur with responses. “God's wisdom exceeds ours,”
“we cannot see all goods,” and “finite minds cannot survey providence” may be
one cognitive-limit strategy expressed three ways. Two materially different
responses must perform different argumentative work, such as undercutting a
likelihood judgment and supplying independent counterevidence.

### 13. Counterevidence and the total case

Draper acknowledges that the significance of his argument depends partly on
whether a theist has propositional or nonpropositional evidence favoring theism
over HI that offsets O.[^1] He doubts that common arguments or religious
experience readily provide enough such evidence, but the paper does not
calculate every person's total evidence.

A total-evidence response should therefore not say “priors solve the problem.”
It should identify the alleged counterevidence and answer at least four
questions:

1. Is the evidence genuinely independent of O?
2. Does it support the full moral perfection of the creator rather than only
   power or intelligence?
3. How strong is it relative to O's likelihood advantage?
4. What objections or defeaters apply to it?

Draper notes that some arguments might support a powerful, intelligent creator
without supporting moral perfection. He introduces an indifferent-deity rival
to show why creator evidence alone may not settle the moral comparison.[^1]
This is an important pressure on apologetic shortcuts. A conclusion must match
what its premises support.

A Christian may appeal to historical testimony concerning Jesus, the canonical
witness of Scripture, moral and metaphysical arguments, religious experience,
and the internal coherence and explanatory reach of Christian doctrine. Each
requires its own responsible case. Listing them is not the same as weighing
them.

### 14. Confessional evaluation

**Primary-source claim:** Draper's argument gives O a significant likelihood
advantage under HI and treats that advantage as a prima facie reason against
theism unless outweighed.[^1]

**Broad scholarly consensus:** evidential reasoning distinguishes the
probability of evidence on a hypothesis from the probability of the hypothesis
given the evidence. This distinction is standard probability logic and does
not require numerical values in every philosophical application.

**Contested interpretation:** the size of Draper's likelihood advantage, the
proper priors, the fairness of HI as a rival, and the predictive effect of
theistic expansions remain disputed. The course does not treat any of these as
settled by the notation.

**Confessional conclusion:** Christian faith affirms that nature is not
ultimately indifferent because the triune God creates, sustains, redeems,
judges, and renews. The confession supplies a substantive alternative to HI.
It must still face Draper's evidential question honestly. Christian doctrine
should change expectations in principled ways rather than operate as an
unfalsifiable label attached after the fact.

The biblical canon contributes both confidence and restraint. Creation is
called good; suffering and death belong to a disordered history; God acts in
judgment and mercy; Christ undergoes unjust suffering and rises; creation
groans in hope; death and pain are not final. The same canon contains lament,
unanswered questions, and condemnation of false explanations. It does not
teach that an analyst can compute providence or name the purpose of each
suffering.

Thus a Christian total-evidence judgment may conclude that Draper's O is
genuine evidence against bare theism while holding that the larger Christian
case remains rationally stronger. That judgment needs arguments, not a
confessional shortcut. Another inquirer may assess the weights differently
without being dishonest or morally defective.

### 15. A seven-step comparative audit

Use this protocol in the Week 9 study guide and A-05 milestone:

1. **Specify H1 and H2.** Preserve each source's actual content.
2. **Specify E.** State what the observation includes and excludes.
3. **Control the background.** Keep E out of the information used to assess
   its own likelihood.
4. **Compare likelihoods.** Give reasons for relative expectedness and address
   the strongest contrary reason.
5. **Audit expansions.** Test internal plausibility and predictive improvement.
6. **Audit dependence.** Identify overlap, conditional ordering, and possible
   double counting.
7. **State the ceiling.** Distinguish local favoring, posterior judgment, and
   total-evidence conclusion.

This audit can expose uncertainty. “The likelihood direction is plausible but
its magnitude is underdetermined” is a legitimate result if supported. So is
“the expansion raises theism's likelihood but not enough to erase HI's
advantage.” Precision concerns argumentative role and scope, not compulsory
decimals.

### 16. Pastoral restraint is not probabilistic hesitation

Hypothesis comparison is not a bedside script. A person seeking safety or care
does not need an analyst to decide whether HI or theism better predicts the
world before responding. Limited human agents ordinarily should prevent
foreseeable harm, protect vulnerable people, report abuse, seek qualified
medical or mental-health care, preserve evidence when appropriate, and offer
practical presence.

**Curriculum synthesis:** uncertainty about divine reasons and confidence in
providence both leave creaturely duties intact. One may trust God while
intervening against harm. One may find Draper's argument powerful while
supporting a religious sufferer without using the moment to press a worldview
verdict. Neither apologetic defense nor skeptical argument should instrumentalize
the person.

When a case involves immediate danger, legal duties, abuse, self-harm risk,
medical symptoms, or clinical needs, the philosophy student remains within
competence. Contact appropriate emergency, safeguarding, legal, pastoral, or
clinical resources. The course offers analysis, not professional advice.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Likelihood evidentialism:** O favors HI over theism if O is more probable
  on HI, regardless of whether theism remains possible.
- **Prior or total-evidence response:** a local likelihood disadvantage may be
  offset by stronger independent support for theism.
- **Theodicy-as-expansion response:** a plausible theistic auxiliary can raise
  P(O given theism) if it genuinely improves prediction.
- **Cognitive-limit response:** finite agents may lack warrant for the
  likelihood judgments Draper makes; the full case belongs to Week 10.
- **Comparison-class objection:** HI's breadth and theism's specificity may
  complicate the fairness and interpretation of the comparison.
- **Confessional total-evidence response:** Christian theism draws on a thicker
  canonical and historical account while conceding that it must address O and
  avoid ad hoc token explanations.

## Confessional analysis

The course's confession fixes its constructive conclusion but not each
probability assessment. Christians may judge the canonical and historical case
for the triune God strong enough to outweigh O while also acknowledging that O
presses theism in a negative direction. Such a judgment is more intellectually
responsible than claiming that suffering has no evidential force merely
because God is confessed as good.

Christian doctrine also resists an indifferent-deity fallback. Power and
intelligence without goodness do not identify the God confessed by the church.
Arguments supporting a creator must be joined to reasons concerning God's
character, culminating for Christians in God's action in Christ. This is a
theological and historical claim requiring its own evidence, not an automatic
probability assignment.

## Summary

Evidence favors one hypothesis over another when it is more expected on the
first, but a likelihood advantage is not automatically a posterior or
total-evidence victory. Priors, rival specification, auxiliary claims,
dependence, and counterevidence matter. Draper's WAP shows why a theodicy
improves theism's position only if it is sufficiently plausible given theism
and genuinely raises the expectedness of O. He argues that the moral-progress,
serious-responsibility, and infinite-intellect expansions he considers do not
do enough. Rowe and Draper organize overlapping suffering evidence through
different inferential bridges, so they should not be collapsed or double
counted. Christian confession can support a total-evidence response while
still recognizing O as a real evidential pressure, declining false numerical
precision, and refusing to assign a hidden purpose to a sufferer's pain.
