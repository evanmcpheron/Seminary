## Reading questions

1. Why does a free-will account of culpable human action not automatically
   explain disease, earthquake, or nonhuman animal suffering?
2. What kind of knowledge does Swinburne think agents need for morally
   significant choice, and how are natural processes supposed to provide it?
3. Does Swinburne claim only that natural evil can provide knowledge, or that
   it is needed or especially fitting for that purpose? Track the wording.
4. How does Stump challenge necessity? How does she separately challenge the
   claim that learning through actual natural evil is better?
5. Which goods named in this debate accrue to the sufferer, and which accrue
   principally to observers or responders?
6. Where does Lewis explicitly signal speculation in chapter 9? Why does that
   signal matter to a theodicy rather than merely to a defense?
7. Why is Lewis's proposal about animal consciousness insufficient by itself
   to establish that apparent animal suffering is unreal?
8. How does chapter 8 connect freedom and judgment? What objections concerning
   proportionality, finality, and divine goodness remain live?
9. Which claims can function as general accounts of a world containing
   suffering, and which would require evidence about a particular event?
10. How do Job 38–42 and Romans 8 constrain both a denial of providence and an
    overconfident explanation of one sufferer's loss?
