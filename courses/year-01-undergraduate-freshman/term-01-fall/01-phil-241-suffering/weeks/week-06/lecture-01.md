# Natural Evil, Animal Suffering, and Moral Choice

## Learning objectives

After completing this lecture, the student should be able to:

- distinguish moral evil, natural evil, and mixed causal cases;
- explain why animal suffering pressures a human free-will response;
- reconstruct Swinburne's knowledge-and-choice account of natural evil;
- state Stump's objections concerning necessity and better means;
- compare stable-order, higher-order-good, fall, and nonhuman-agency
  proposals without merging them;
- evaluate Lewis's animal-pain proposals as speculation rather than fact; and
- use Job and Romans 8 to discipline providential and pastoral claims.

## Key terms

**Moral evil:** culpable wrongdoing or suffering produced by culpable action or
omission. **Natural evil:** harmful suffering not classified, in the immediate
analysis, as the product of a culpable creaturely choice. **Mixed causal case:**
a case in which natural processes and human actions or omissions jointly shape
the harm. **Natural law:** a stable pattern or regularity in the created order.
**Stable-order account:** a proposal that a regular, knowable world makes
embodied agency, prediction, cooperation, and responsibility possible even
though the same regularities permit harm. **Induction:** reasoning from
observed patterns to expectations about unobserved cases. **Knowledge of
consequences:** information about what alternative actions are likely to cause
or prevent. **Higher-order good:** a good response to hardship, such as
compassion or courage, that logically presupposes some occasion for it.
**Necessary condition:** something without which a stated end cannot obtain.
**Better means:** a means preferable to available alternatives, even if it is
not strictly necessary. **Scope control:** limiting a conclusion to what its
premises support.

## Lecture

### 1. The free-will defense leaves a remainder

Week 5 ended with a qualified verdict. Plantinga's model can answer a strict
claim that a perfectly good, omniscient, omnipotent God and moral evil are
logically incompatible. It does so by appealing to the possible value and risk
of significantly free creaturely action. That model does not automatically
explain suffering that is not caused by a culpable human choice. Disease,
earthquake, genetic disorder, drought, predation, and the pain of nonhuman
animals can arise without a person's intending them.

Plantinga considers extending the free-will strategy by proposing nonhuman
free agents. Week 5 treated that as a possible model, not an established
history of actual suffering. The proposal may have logical relevance: if such
agents possibly caused natural harms, then natural evil need not produce a
bare contradiction. Yet a theodicy asks what actually explains the world. A
new causal hypothesis requires positive warrant, not merely freedom from
contradiction.

**Curriculum synthesis:** Natural evil is not an appendix to moral evil. It is
a distinct explanatory pressure, especially when suffering precedes or falls
outside responsible human choice. The question is not only whether God and
natural evil can coexist, but whether the world's kind, scale, distribution,
and apparent waste of suffering fit a good providence.

### 2. Classifying causes without hiding responsibility

The moral/natural distinction is useful, but it can oversimplify. Consider
three stipulated examples:

1. A person knowingly poisons a neighbor. The immediate harm is a moral evil.
2. A previously undetectable geological event harms an uninhabited ecosystem.
   The immediate harm is natural rather than moral.
3. A severe storm strikes a community after responsible persons ignored known
   safety duties. Natural forces and culpable omissions jointly shape the
   outcome. This is a mixed causal case.

Classification answers a causal and moral question: *What processes and
agents contributed, and which agents are culpable?* It does not yet answer an
evaluative question: *Why would God create or sustain an order in which those
causes can produce harm?* Nor should the word *natural* make a preventable
injury seem inevitable. In a mixed case, classifying the hazard as natural
must not conceal negligence, unequal protection, or a duty to mitigate future
harm.

The reverse error is also possible. Human action can reduce or intensify harm,
but it does not follow that every illness or disaster is traceable to the
victim's choice or to a particular person's sin. A causal classification
requires evidence. A theological story should never substitute for an actual
investigation of safety, medicine, environment, or culpability.

### 3. Why animal suffering sharpens the problem

Animal suffering tests explanations centered on human agency in at least four
ways. First, a nonhuman animal need not be a morally responsible agent in
order to undergo injury. Second, suffering among animals cannot generally be
described as punishment for the animal's culpable wrongdoing. Third, goods
such as a human sufferer's repentance do not directly apply to a creature that
does not exercise that kind of moral agency. Fourth, a benefit gained by human
observers raises a beneficiary question: may another creature's harm be
justified principally by what someone else learns?

Lewis recognizes the force of this pressure. He calls animal suffering a
grave problem precisely because his account of human guilt and spiritual
formation cannot simply be transferred to animals. He also says that claims
about animals move beyond the range of human knowledge and labels what follows
as speculation.[^3] Those cautions are important primary-source controls.

The question is not sentimental excess. If a perfectly good creator values
creatures, then apparently pointless pain among creatures matters. The number
of sufferers is relevant to distribution, policy, and the character of a
world, even though pain is experienced by individuals rather than by one
aggregate consciousness. A philosophical response must attend to each
sufferer rather than erase individuals inside a favorable total.

### 4. The appeal to a stable, knowable order

One response begins with regularity. Embodied agents act within a world where
similar causes often have similar effects. Fire can warm and burn; gravity
permits stable movement and dangerous falls; biological systems can sustain
life and fail. If God continually interrupted causal sequences whenever harm
threatened, agents might have difficulty learning, planning, cooperating, or
bearing responsibility.

This proposal identifies goods associated with a stable order:

- agents can form reasonable expectations;
- actions can make durable differences;
- warnings, skills, and institutions can transmit knowledge;
- prevention and rescue can be meaningful; and
- created causes possess a real integrity rather than functioning as a stage
  whose outcomes are constantly replaced.

The proposal is not yet a complete theodicy. Stability may explain why there
are regular consequences, but not why the laws and initial conditions have
exactly their actual form. It may explain why God does not prevent *every*
harm by miracle, but not why some harms are not prevented. It also must not
move from “some risk belongs to a law-governed world” to “every actual degree
and distribution of suffering is necessary.” A general structural reason does
not settle every token event.

**Contested interpretation:** A critic can grant the value of regularity while
asking whether a differently ordered but still stable creation could contain
less severe suffering. The critic can also distinguish preserving causal order
from never acting providentially within it. Theism itself often includes
claims of special divine action, so any stable-order account must say why such
action is fitting in some cases and absent in others.

### 5. Swinburne's knowledge-and-choice proposal

Swinburne connects natural evil to the free-will theodicy through knowledge.
Morally significant action requires more than an abstract ability to choose.
Agents must know something about what their choices can do. If I cannot know
whether an action will heal, injure, nourish, or poison, my power to choose
responsibly among outcomes is diminished. A patterned natural order, including
observed harms, permits agents to learn consequences by induction.[^1]

The argument can be reconstructed as follows. These lines are a curriculum
reconstruction, not a quotation:

1. Morally significant responsibility requires a substantial range of choices
   whose consequences agents can understand.
2. Knowledge of consequences must be available without eliminating the
   freedom that makes the choices morally significant.
3. Regular natural processes, including processes that sometimes cause harm,
   provide evidence from which agents can learn how to cause, avoid, or relieve
   similar outcomes.
4. Direct, unmistakable divine disclosure of every consequence could alter
   the epistemic setting of action and reduce the kind of responsibility at
   issue; it could also remove goods involved in inquiry and cooperation.
5. Therefore natural evil can contribute to conditions for knowledgeable,
   morally significant choice.
6. If the value of those conditions is sufficient and no better feasible means
   is available, God may have a morally sufficient reason to permit some
   natural evil.

Premise 6 displays the burden. Premises 1–5 show that natural suffering *can*
be connected to knowledge and choice. They do not yet show the amount needed,
that the connection justifies the cost, or that no better means is available.

### 6. Higher-order goods and serious responsibility

Swinburne also emphasizes opportunities created by suffering. Compassion
responds to pain; courage faces danger; rescue presupposes peril; patient care
presupposes need. A world without vulnerability would lack these exact goods.
The moral field would be thinner because fewer choices would bear seriously on
another creature's welfare.[^1]

This is a logically significant point. One cannot have *compassion toward a
sufferer* if no one suffers. But three different conclusions must be separated:

- some hardship is logically required for that exact higher-order good;
- the higher-order good is valuable enough to justify permitting hardship; and
- the actual hardship, in its actual severity and distribution, is justified.

Only the first follows from the definition. The second is a moral comparison.
The third requires evidence about the actual case. Moreover, an agent can have
valuable opportunities to give, create, protect, teach, or delight another
without that person's first being harmed. Goods responsive to evil are real,
but they are not the only serious goods.

The beneficiary issue remains. If one creature suffers so that another can
show courage, the account risks treating the sufferer as material for someone
else's excellence. A morally adequate theodicy must ask what goodness means
for the sufferer, not merely for the total history or the observer.

### 7. Stump's first challenge: necessity

Stump grants the promise of Swinburne's project but disputes a crucial bridge.
Even if agents need knowledge of consequences, why suppose that actual natural
evil is necessary for gaining it? Relevant knowledge might be communicated or
acquired through means other than a history of serious suffering. Her examples
press the difference between *this is one way to learn* and *this is the only
possible way to learn*.[^2]

The logical point can be stated compactly:

1. Swinburne identifies a good, knowledgeable moral choice.
2. He identifies a means, induction from natural processes that include harm.
3. A justifying necessity claim needs the further premise that no less costly
   route can provide the relevant knowledge while preserving the relevant
   freedom.
4. If that premise is unsupported, the argument has not shown natural evil to
   be necessary for the good.

This does not prove that divine communication or harmless simulation would be
equally good. It transfers the burden to comparison. A theodicist must explain
why an alternative would undermine agency, inquiry, responsibility, or some
other indispensable good rather than simply asserting that it would.

### 8. Stump's second challenge: better means

Swinburne can retreat from strict necessity. Perhaps natural suffering is not
the only route to knowledge, but learning through an independent natural order
is better. It may allow discovery, shared inquiry, responsibility for seeking
evidence, and action without an overwhelming awareness of divine surveillance.

Stump's reply then asks what makes the route better *all things considered*.
The value of discovering how to prevent an evil cannot straightforwardly
justify the evil if, without that evil, the preventive knowledge would not be
needed. The account risks circularity: suffering is permitted so that agents
can learn how suffering happens and how to respond to it. Learning and response
may be genuine goods once harm exists, but their presence does not by itself
show that creating the need for them is better.[^2]

The comparison also requires moral accounting. Who learns? Who pays? How much
knowledge is obtained? Could a lesser harm teach enough? Are some sufferers
unable to participate in the alleged good? Does the proposal include animals
whose pain teaches human observers? A theory that answers only at the level of
total social benefit may leave individual creatures unaddressed.

**Curriculum synthesis:** Stump does not need to prove that no natural evil is
ever justified. Her objection succeeds if it shows that the knowledge premise
alone does not justify all natural suffering. A source can offer a partial
reason without carrying the complete explanatory burden.

### 9. Lewis: explicit uncertainty and risky proposals

Lewis begins his animal-pain chapter with epistemic restraint. He says the
subject lies outside what humans know and that his proposals are speculative.
He then explores several possibilities: some animal sensation may lack the
temporally unified selfhood he associates with suffering; prehuman animal
disorder may reflect the action of a rebellious nonhuman power; humans may
have failed in a creaturely vocation toward animals; and some animals may
participate in restoration through their relation to human beings.[^3]

These proposals must remain separate:

- a philosophy-of-mind claim about animal consciousness;
- a historical-causal claim about corruption before human beings;
- a theological claim about human dominion and failure; and
- an eschatological speculation about animal identity and restoration.

Evidence for one does not establish the others. Lewis's inference from limited
self-consciousness to absence of suffering is not demonstrated merely by
defining a unified autobiographical self. A creature need not formulate “I am
the same subject across time” in order for a present aversive experience to
matter morally. The course therefore does not use Lewis's speculation to
dismiss apparent animal pain.

Lewis also acknowledges that his relational proposal about tame animals does
not cover wild animals or mistreated domestic animals. That admission prevents
the illustration from functioning as a complete answer. Its constructive
value lies elsewhere: human authority over animals, if granted, creates duties
of care and exposes cruelty as abuse rather than permission.

### 10. Fall and nonhuman-agency accounts

Christian interpretations commonly connect creation's disorder with sin, but
several claims can hide inside that sentence:

1. **Canonical claim:** human rebellion belongs within Scripture's account of
   death, alienation, and creation's groaning.
2. **Systematic claim:** the fall somehow disorders relations among God,
   humans, and nonhuman creation.
3. **Historical claim:** a particular animal harm began at a specified point
   because of human or angelic action.
4. **Case-specific claim:** this disaster or illness occurred because of a
   particular sin.

Genesis 3 and Romans 8 support a canonical connection among sin, frustration,
death, and hope, but they do not supply every causal detail in lines 3–4.[^5]
Lewis himself recognizes that a simple appeal to the human fall cannot explain
prehuman animal suffering and turns to nonhuman agency as a hypothesis, not as
a demonstrated chronology.[^3]

A nonhuman-agency account extends freedom beyond humans. Its logical form is
clear: harms that appear natural relative to human action might ultimately
result from another creature's moral action. Its evidential burden is equally
clear. The possibility of such agency does not show that it actually caused
predation, disease, or any particular disaster. Pastoral use is especially
unsafe: alleging occult causation without evidence can divert attention from
medical care, protection, responsible investigation, and ordinary prevention.

### 11. Six questions for every proposed account

Natural-evil proposals should be tested rather than merely listed.

1. **Target:** Is the proposal a defense, an actual theodicy, a doctrine, or a
   pastoral practice?
2. **Connection:** Is the evil merely followed by a good, useful as a means,
   or logically required for that exact good?
3. **Scale:** Does the reason support some risk, or the actual intensity and
   duration of suffering?
4. **Distribution:** Why does the cost fall on these creatures rather than
   others, and do the alleged benefits reach them?
5. **Alternatives:** Has the proposal considered less harmful ways to preserve
   regularity, knowledge, responsibility, and other goods?
6. **Application:** Does a general reason authorize a claim about this event?

The tests explain why several proposals may be complementary. Stable order can
address regularity; Swinburne can address knowledge and opportunity; a fall
account can locate suffering inside a theological history; eschatological hope
can address final restoration. Combining partial accounts does not
automatically remove their individual gaps. A cumulative theodicy still bears
the combined burden of truth, coherence, proportion, and application.

### 12. Job, Romans 8, and responsible action

Job 38–42 affirms divine wisdom and rule while withholding the simple
case-specific explanation Job's friends presumed to know. The divine speeches
attend to a more-than-human creation, including wild creatures outside Job's
control. The text reorients Job without teaching that every sufferer's friends
may silence lament by appealing to mystery.[^5]

Romans 8 portrays creation as subjected to frustration, groaning, awaiting
liberation, and held within hope. The chapter does not call groaning good. Its
movement is eschatological: present suffering is real and unfinished, the
Spirit accompanies weakness, and redemption includes creation rather than
escape from it.[^5]

**Confessional conclusion:** The curriculum affirms that creation remains
under God's providence, that evil and suffering are not ultimate, that human
sin has cosmic significance, and that resurrection and renewed creation ground
hope. It also affirms creaturely duties to relieve suffering. These claims do
not reveal God's full reason for each natural evil.

**Curriculum synthesis:** In a real case, investigate causes, protect life,
seek appropriate medical or safety expertise, care for animals, lament, and
offer practical aid. Philosophical analysis may be offered when invited and
appropriate. Do not tell a sufferer that harm was needed to teach others,
assert hidden guilt, assign demonic causation, or discourage prevention.

## Primary-source engagement

The lecture reconstructs Swinburne's attempt to connect natural evil with
knowledge and morally significant choice, then follows Stump's two-stage
challenge concerning necessity and preferable means. It treats Lewis's chapter
9 as Lewis treats it: an acknowledged field of speculation. Job and Romans 8
supply canonical constraints on providence and hope rather than premises the
philosophical interlocutors must accept. No extended copyrighted passage is
reproduced.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Stable-order account:** a regular natural world makes embodied agency,
  learning, planning, and responsibility possible, although it also permits
  harm.
- **Swinburne's extension:** natural evils provide knowledge of consequences
  and opportunities for morally serious response that support significant
  freedom.
- **Stump's critique:** natural evil is not shown to be necessary for the
  relevant knowledge, and learning by suffering is not shown to be preferable
  to less costly alternatives.
- **Fall account:** creation's suffering is located within a theological
  history of rebellion and disorder; precise causal extensions require further
  argument.
- **Nonhuman-agency account:** creaturely freedom beyond humanity could supply
  a consistency model, but actuality and case-specific causation need evidence.
- **Lewis's proposals:** animal consciousness, prehuman corruption, human
  vocation, and animal restoration are explored speculatively, not proved.

## Confessional analysis

The course affirms creation, fall, providence, creaturely responsibility,
lament, resurrection, judgment, and new creation within a broadly evangelical
Protestant framework. It refuses to identify natural processes as independent
of God, but also refuses to infer that every preventable consequence should be
accepted passively. Scripture and providence motivate care for creation and
neighbor; they do not supply an evidential shortcut around science, safety,
medicine, or moral accountability. These are independent curriculum judgments,
not official Wheaton College teaching.

## Summary

Natural evil requires analysis beyond a free-will account of culpable human
action. The moral/natural distinction clarifies causes but must allow mixed
cases and must never hide preventable responsibility. Swinburne argues that
natural processes involving suffering provide knowledge and morally serious
opportunities needed for significant choice. Stump replies that this route is
not shown to be necessary or preferable to alternatives. Animal suffering
intensifies the challenge because human guilt or formation does not directly
explain the animal's cost. Lewis honestly marks his responses as speculative;
the course therefore evaluates rather than adopts them as fact. Stable order,
fall, nonhuman agency, and higher-order goods can each offer partial proposals,
but scale, distribution, alternatives, beneficiary, and application remain.
Job and Romans 8 join providence with creaturely limits, groaning, and hope,
supporting active care without a claim to know why a particular harm occurred.
