# Value, Defeat, and the Limits of Providence Claims

## Learning objectives

After completing this lecture, the student should be able to:

- distinguish prevention, permission, outweighing, compensation, and defeat;
- explain Chisholm's contrast between balancing off and defeating evil;
- evaluate greater-good proposals using tests of connection, beneficiary,
  proportion, distribution, alternatives, and epistemic access;
- assess Lewis's account of judgment and creaturely refusal without treating
  its speculative elements as settled doctrine;
- distinguish a general doctrine of providence from kind-level and
  case-specific explanatory claims;
- interpret Job and Romans 8 as constraints on both skepticism and explanatory
  overconfidence; and
- apply philosophical categories without assigning hidden purposes to a
  sufferer.

## Key terms

**Prevention:** stopping an evil or its harmful effect from occurring.
**Permission:** not preventing an evil one could prevent; permission is not
identical to causing, intending, approving, or excusing it. **Outweighing** or
**balancing off:** the value of other goods is sufficient to make a larger
whole positive despite an evil part. **Compensation:** a benefit given in
response to loss that restores or benefits the one harmed; compensation need
not make the original evil necessary to the resulting good. **Defeat:** in the
Chisholmian tradition, an evil is organically integrated into a valuable whole
so that it is not merely offset by an independent good. **Organic unity:** a
whole whose value is not simply the arithmetic sum of separable parts.
**Necessary constituent:** a part without which the relevant whole or its
distinctive value could not obtain. **General providence claim:** a doctrinal
claim about God's governance of creation. **Kind-level explanation:** a claim
about why God might permit a class of risk or suffering. **Token explanation:**
a claim about why a particular event was permitted. **Epistemic restraint:**
refusal to claim more knowledge than the evidence warrants.

## Lecture

### 1. A cause is not yet a justification

Lecture 1 distinguished moral, natural, and mixed causal cases. That taxonomy
helps identify what happened and who, if anyone, is culpable. It does not yet
show why divine permission would be good. A complete causal description of an
earthquake would not answer why a good providence permits geological processes
to harm creatures. Conversely, a proposed divine reason would not replace the
causal work of geology, medicine, or a safety investigation.

The distinction prevents two shortcuts. The first says, “A natural process
caused it; therefore no moral or theological question remains.” The second
says, “God is provident; therefore no causal investigation is needed.” Both
fail. An event can have created causes within providence, and discovering those
causes can disclose human duties. Theological explanation and empirical
explanation can address different questions without competing as if only one
level were real.

The evaluative question is harder: under what conditions could a perfectly
good God be justified in permitting suffering? Philosophers answer with terms
such as *greater good*, *compensation*, and *defeat*. Unless these terms are
distinguished, a response can sound stronger than it is.

### 2. Five different relations

Begin with a stipulated evil `E`: an innocent creature experiences serious
pain. Consider five claims.

1. **Prevention:** an agent stops `E` before it occurs.
2. **Permission:** an agent able to prevent `E` does not prevent it.
3. **Outweighing:** some good `G` exists, and the whole containing `E + G` is
   better than a neutral or bad whole even though `G` may be independent of
   `E`.
4. **Compensation:** the sufferer receives a benefit in response to `E`, making
   the sufferer's life or condition better than it otherwise would be after
   the loss.
5. **Defeat:** `E` is integrated into a valuable whole whose distinctive value
   depends on that relation; `E` is not merely placed beside an unrelated good.

These relations do not entail one another. Permission does not entail approval.
Compensation does not entail that the original harm was necessary. A whole can
contain more good than evil while still using one individual as a means to
another's benefit. An evil might be defeated in a technical value relation
without making the agent who caused it innocent. And an agent might have a
reason to permit some risk without having a reason to permit every harmful
result.

The distinctions matter pastorally. “Something good happened afterward” may
be true without answering whether the harm was justified, whether it was
preventable, or whether the victim received the good. Gratitude for later help
does not require calling the original injury good.

### 3. Chisholm: balancing off and defeat

Roderick Chisholm distinguishes balancing off from defeat in his account of
intrinsic value. On a balancing model, a bad part remains bad and another good
part offsets it within the total. On a defeat model, the relation among the
parts affects the value of the whole. An evil can be caught up in an organic
unity whose value is not calculated by simply adding an unrelated benefit.[^3]

An aesthetic analogy can clarify the structure but must be handled carefully.
A dissonant passage may contribute to the beauty of a resolved musical whole;
the same dissonance inserted randomly might diminish it. The later harmony
does not merely pay a numerical debt. The relation between tension and
resolution partly constitutes the value.

Human suffering is not paint or sound. Persons are not raw material for a
beautiful universe. The analogy illustrates an evaluative relation; it does
not establish that any actual suffering is organically integrated, that a
victim will affirm the whole, or that God needed the evil. Moving from a work
of art to a person's life introduces agency, consent, justice, memory,
embodiment, and the sufferer's own good.

**Primary-source claim:** Chisholm's proposal is deliberately cautious. The
conceptual framework shows how a world containing evil could possibly have its
evil defeated; it does not claim human observers know that every actual evil is
defeated. The wise theodicist's posture, on Chisholm's account, is not
omniscient confidence.[^3]

### 4. Necessary connection is doing moral work

Defeat requires more than sequence. Suppose an injured person later wins an
unrelated prize. The prize can improve that person's life, but the injury did
not contribute to the prize. Calling the injury “defeated” would hide the lack
of connection. By contrast, if the person freely incorporates a truthful
memory of the injury into a restorative achievement, the relation may be more
integrated. Even then, the person's courageous response does not retroactively
justify a wrongdoer.

A rigorous defeat proposal must specify:

- the larger valuable whole;
- the relation between the evil part and the whole;
- why a neutral replacement would not yield an equally good or better whole;
- whose good the whole is;
- whether the sufferer participates in or affirms the good; and
- what agent, if any, was justified in permitting the risk.

This is why words such as *redemption* and *purpose* cannot do the work by
themselves. They name a hoped-for relation; they do not demonstrate it. A
Christian may confess that God will redeem creation while remaining unable to
show how a particular loss is a necessary constituent of a particular good.

Stump's critique of Swinburne illustrates the alternatives test. If relevant
knowledge and responsibility could exist through a less harmful route, then
actual natural suffering has not been shown necessary to the good. The same
test applies to defeat: if an equally valuable whole could exist with a neutral
part in place of the evil, the strict necessity claim fails.[^2]

### 5. The beneficiary and agency questions

Greater-good arguments often move too quickly from “the world contains more
good overall” to “permission was good to each person affected.” The move
requires argument. Imagine one creature bears a severe cost while many others
gain knowledge or display compassion. An aggregate calculation may be
positive while the arrangement remains unjust to the one used.

At least four perspectives must be distinguished:

- **agent-relative:** was the permitting agent justified in imposing or
  allowing this risk?
- **sufferer-relative:** is the resulting whole good for the one harmed?
- **beneficiary-relative:** do others receive goods, and were those goods
  obtained at another's expense?
- **world-relative:** is the complete history good considered as a totality?

A theory can answer one perspective and fail another. Swinburne's emphasis on
knowledge and serious responsibility identifies agent and social goods.
Stump's criticism asks whether those goods require the price. Animal suffering
intensifies the beneficiary problem when human moral development supplies the
principal alleged benefit.

Agency also survives providence. If a wrongdoer causes an evil that God later
defeats, the wrongdoer does not gain the right to cause it. The agent intended
or recklessly accepted the harm, not the redeeming whole. A later good may
display divine wisdom or creaturely resilience while leaving the original act
culpable. Week 5's distinction among causation, permission, intention, and
approval remains controlling.

### 6. Lewis on judgment, refusal, and finality

Lewis's chapter on hell approaches a different kind of suffering: final
judgment. He takes the doctrine's moral difficulty seriously and addresses
objections about retribution, disproportion between temporal wrongdoing and
an eternal result, the apparent adequacy of further chances, and terrifying
scriptural images. He brings together judicial language with an account in
which a settled creaturely refusal becomes its own ruin.[^4]

The account extends the Week 5 freedom theme. Evil is not only an external act
with consequences for victims; persistent self-centered willing can deform the
agent. On Lewis's picture, divine judgment does not merely add arbitrary pain
to an otherwise healthy person. It ratifies or discloses the end toward which a
will fixed against God moves.

Lewis also treats imagery carefully. Punishment, destruction, and exclusion
are not simply interchangeable literal descriptions, and he acknowledges
limits in imagining eternity. That interpretive restraint should prevent the
reader from turning one image into a complete philosophical mechanism.

**Primary-source claim:** Lewis's closing moral emphasis is self-application.
The doctrine is not supplied so that a reader can confidently diagnose the
destiny of enemies, friends, or a particular sufferer. It confronts the reader
with the seriousness of one's own refusal.[^4]

### 7. Objections to Lewis's account

Lewis's proposal deserves serious questions rather than either dismissal or
automatic adoption.

**Proportionality:** Does a settled final condition proportionately answer
finite wrongdoing under limited knowledge and damaged circumstances? Lewis's
appeal to eternity as more than endless clock time reframes the issue, but the
metaphysical picture is not itself a demonstration.

**Freedom and fixity:** Why would a created will become irrevocably fixed? If
freedom explains refusal, could freedom also allow later repentance? Lewis
appeals to divine knowledge of when further chances would not help. The reply
depends on a claim about God's knowledge and the agent's condition that human
observers cannot verify in a particular case.

**Grace and agency:** How do divine initiative, human inability, freedom, and
final judgment relate? Broadly Protestant traditions answer with different
accounts of grace and freedom. Lewis's popular treatment does not settle those
systematic disputes.

**Divine goodness:** Does respecting refusal explain nonintervention, or could
perfect love transform the will without violating personhood? The objection
presses both the theory of freedom and the scope of omnipotent love.

**Pastoral misuse:** Speculation about a deceased person's final state can
multiply grief and exceed human authority. Lewis's self-directed caution rules
out using the doctrine as a weapon.

The course affirms final judgment as part of historic Christian confession but
does not make Lewis's explanatory picture the only faithful account of its
mode. Confessional commitment requires accurate interpretation and humility,
not certainty about details the assigned sources leave contested.

### 8. Providence claims come in levels

The sentence “God permitted this for a reason” can express very different
claims.

1. **General doctrinal claim:** God wisely preserves and governs creation; no
   event lies outside God's ultimate rule.
2. **Kind-level explanatory claim:** God has reason to permit a stable natural
   order, significant creaturely action, or some range of risk.
3. **Token explanatory claim:** God permitted this particular illness, storm,
   death, or animal pain in order to produce this specified good.

The move downward requires additional evidence. A general doctrine does not
by itself identify a kind-level reason. A kind-level reason does not identify
the reason for a token. Even if stable laws justify allowing some natural
risk, it does not follow that a particular loss was necessary for a neighbor's
growth. Even if God defeats all evil eschatologically, it does not follow that
human observers can map each evil to its role now.

This is not a retreat from providence. It is an epistemic distinction between
what a doctrine affirms and what an interpreter knows. A person can trust an
agent's wisdom without claiming to know every intention. Within Christian
theism, the gap is wider because divine knowledge is not measured by the
observer's access.

The distinction also disciplines skeptical inference. “I cannot identify a
sufficient reason” does not deductively entail “there is none.” But the reverse
is equally important: “There may be a reason” does not establish an actual
reason, explain its content, or remove evidential pressure. Week 10 will study
skeptical theism directly. Here the point is modest scope control.

### 9. Job: providence without the friends' explanation

The book of Job places general providence beside resistance to false token
explanations. Job's friends offer a morally ordered scheme in which suffering
discloses guilt. Their general conviction that God is just does not authorize
their inference about Job. The narrative rejects the simple application even
while refusing to portray the world as outside divine rule.[^5]

In Job 38–42, the divine speeches do not give Job a direct account of the
heavenly scene in chapters 1–2. Instead, they display divine governance of a
vast creation, including weather and wild creatures beyond Job's management.
The speeches challenge Job's comprehensive grasp; they do not make his losses
unreal or declare the friends' formula correct.

Two errors follow. A skeptical reader may say that because Job does not receive
the demanded explanation, the text denies meaningful providence. That ignores
the speeches' strong claim about divine wisdom and rule. A careless defender
may say that because God is wise, every friend may tell a sufferer exactly what
God is doing. That repeats the friends' presumption.

**Curriculum synthesis:** Job supports trust joined to lament and epistemic
restraint. It does not license silence imposed by observers. Job speaks,
questions, grieves, and receives an encounter; the book preserves rather than
erases his protest.

### 10. Romans 8: groaning and unfinished history

Romans 8 locates suffering within a story moving toward liberation. Creation
is subjected to frustration, groans, and awaits freedom; believers groan; the
Spirit accompanies weakness; hope concerns what is not yet seen. The chapter's
logic is not that pain becomes pleasant once named providential. It is that
present suffering is neither ultimate nor abandoned.[^5]

This eschatological frame can support a doctrine of defeat: God can bring
created life into a whole in which evil does not have the final word. But the
text does not provide a one-to-one map showing how each harm was necessary for
the final good. Resurrection can overcome what should never be called good in
itself. Restoration can be more than compensation without making perpetrators
agents of redemption.

Romans 8 also resists a purely individual solution. Creation itself awaits
liberation. Christian hope therefore includes bodily life and nonhuman
creation, not only an inward reinterpretation of pain. That scope gives a
theological reason to oppose harm and care for creation now.

**Confessional conclusion:** Eschatological hope is not an arithmetical claim
that enough future pleasure cancels present suffering. It is trust that the
triune God will judge evil, raise the dead, liberate creation, and bring God's
people into communion that evil cannot destroy. The exact metaphysics of how
each evil is defeated remains partly hidden.

### 11. Six tests for a providential proposal

When evaluating any proposed reason, ask six ordered questions.

1. **Truth:** Is the causal, moral, theological, or value claim supported, or
   merely possible?
2. **Connection:** Is the evil necessary to the good, useful under actual
   conditions, followed by the good, or unrelated to it?
3. **Proportion:** Does the proposed good justify the intensity, duration, and
   probability of harm?
4. **Beneficiary:** Is the whole good for the sufferer, only for others, or
   only in aggregate?
5. **Alternatives and distribution:** Could the good arise through less harm,
   and why are risks distributed as they are?
6. **Epistemic and pastoral warrant:** Does a general theory authorize this
   token claim, and would stating it serve truth, safety, lament, and care?

No single answer automatically decides the entire problem. Swinburne can
identify genuine goods of knowledge and responsibility while Stump shows a
gap in necessity or comparative value. Chisholm can provide a stronger value
relation than balancing while denying human knowledge that every evil is
defeated. Lewis can connect freedom and judgment while leaving proportionality
and fixity contested. Job and Romans 8 can ground trust and hope while
restraining token explanation.

The tests also keep objections charitable. A critic need not claim that
compassion, stable order, or redemption lacks value. The critic can ask whether
those goods justify this cost. A theist need not claim to solve every case in
order to argue that some proposed goods make divine permission coherent.

### 12. Confessional and pastoral synthesis

Christian confession makes strong claims: God creates and governs; creatures
are responsible; evil is judged; Christ's death and bodily resurrection are
central to God's victory; creation will be renewed. It also creates duties.
Providence is never a reason to ignore danger, refuse treatment, protect an
abuser, neglect an animal, or withhold practical aid. Human means of relief can
themselves be instruments of providence.

In academic analysis, a student may reconstruct competing models, test
necessity and value, and state a provisional judgment. In pastoral presence,
the first questions are different: Is anyone unsafe? What help is wanted? What
medical, legal, safeguarding, or mental-health expertise is needed? Is there
space for lament? Philosophical discussion belongs only when the person wants
it and the context can sustain it.

**Confessional conclusion:** The course affirms providence and eschatological
defeat without claiming that evil is intrinsically good or that every divine
reason is available to human observers. Job and Romans 8 support trust that
does not abolish lament. Chisholm's distinction helps explain why Christian
hope is more than an unrelated reward, while leaving the actual integration of
particular evils to God's knowledge and action.

**Curriculum synthesis:** Never infer that a sufferer needed harm for growth,
that another's compassion justified the cost, that an animal's pain is unreal,
or that a disaster signals particular guilt. General providence supports
faithful action under uncertainty; it does not make the student an interpreter
of hidden decrees.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Outweighing account:** sufficient independent good can make a total whole
  positive despite an evil part.
- **Compensation account:** the sufferer receives a responsive benefit, though
  the original evil need not become a necessary constituent of the good.
- **Chisholmian defeat:** evil is organically integrated into a whole whose
  distinctive value is not simple arithmetic offset; actual defeat still
  requires evidence.
- **Swinburne's greater-good proposal:** natural evil supports knowledge and
  serious responsibility.
- **Stump's objection:** necessity and comparative superiority over less
  costly means are not established by identifying one useful route.
- **Lewis's judgment account:** persistent creaturely refusal and divine
  judgment converge, but fixity, proportion, grace, and imagery remain live
  questions.
- **Providential restraint:** general governance, kind-level reason, and token
  explanation require different evidence.

## Confessional analysis

The curriculum affirms comprehensive divine providence, final judgment,
Christ's atoning work and bodily resurrection, creaturely responsibility,
lament, and the renewal of creation. It treats eschatological defeat as a
ground of hope but does not use it to call evil good, justify wrongdoing, or
claim knowledge of God's hidden reason for a particular harm. Important
Protestant questions about freedom, grace, judgment, and the mode of final
punishment remain broader than Lewis's proposal. These are independent broadly
evangelical Protestant judgments, not official Wheaton College teaching.

## Summary

Natural evil raises an evaluative question after its causes have been
classified. Permission, prevention, outweighing, compensation, and defeat name
different relations. Chisholmian defeat is stronger than adding an unrelated
good: evil must be organically integrated into a valuable whole. That concept
does not prove any actual evil is defeated, and person-centered questions make
human suffering unlike an aesthetic part. Swinburne identifies goods of
knowledge and responsibility; Stump presses necessity and alternatives. Lewis
connects final judgment with persistent refusal while leaving proportion,
fixity, grace, and interpretive imagery contested. Providence claims occur at
general, kind, and token levels, and evidence does not move automatically
between them. Job affirms divine rule while opposing presumptuous application;
Romans 8 joins groaning to hope for creation's liberation. Confessional trust
therefore belongs with action, lament, and epistemic restraint.
