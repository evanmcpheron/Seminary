## Swinburne argument audit

- The lecture identifies the target as natural evil rather than simply
  repeating Plantinga's moral-evil defense.
- Swinburne's proposal is reconstructed around knowledge of consequences,
  significant moral choice, stable natural processes, inquiry, responsibility,
  and higher-order opportunities.
- The numbered reconstruction is explicitly labeled curriculum reconstruction,
  not a quotation or the author's own numbered formulation.
- The lecture distinguishes three increasingly strong claims: natural evil can
  supply relevant knowledge; it is needed for the relevant good; and its actual
  amount and distribution are justified.
- Compassion, courage, rescue, and care are treated as goods responsive to
  suffering, not as automatic justification for creating any amount of it.
- The account is tested by the beneficiary question and is not used to treat a
  sufferer or animal as material for someone else's moral development.

## Stump argument audit

- Stump is presented as granting that Swinburne's project is sophisticated
  enough to merit a focused reply.
- Her first challenge targets necessity: identifying induction from harmful
  processes as one route to knowledge does not show there is no less costly
  route.
- Her second challenge targets comparative value: even if discovery through a
  natural order has distinctive goods, the complete moral cost must be
  compared with alternatives.
- The circularity pressure is stated narrowly: knowledge of how to prevent or
  produce suffering cannot by itself justify creating the need for that
  knowledge.
- The lecture does not infer from Stump's objection that all stable-order,
  freedom, formation, or providence accounts fail.

## Lewis chapter audit

- Chapter 8 is accurately distinguished from chapter 9. The lecture does not
  present hell as a species of natural evil.
- Lewis's treatment of punishment, destruction, exclusion, finality, and
  creaturely refusal is paraphrased without collapsing multiple images into a
  single literal mechanism.
- Proportionality, fixity, further chances, grace, and divine transformation
  are presented as objections the popular account does not conclusively settle.
- The self-applicative caution at the chapter's end controls pastoral use; no
  diagnosis of a deceased person or sufferer is authorized.
- Chapter 9's statement that the discussion is speculative controls every
  animal-pain proposal attributed to Lewis.
- Lewis's claims about animal selfhood and pain are not presented as current
  scientific findings. The lecture supplies a philosophical objection and
  refuses to infer that apparent animal suffering is unreal.
- The proposed prehuman nonhuman-agent corruption is described as a causal
  hypothesis, not an article of the historic creeds, a scientific result, or a
  warranted account of any particular event.
- Lewis's relational restoration illustration is not extended to wild or
  abused animals after he expressly acknowledges that it does not cover them.

## Chisholm and value-relation audit

- *Outweighing*, *compensation*, and *defeat* are not treated as synonyms.
- Balancing off is described as offset by distinct value; defeat involves an
  organic relation within a whole whose value is not a simple sum.
- The aesthetic analogy illustrates formal structure only. The lecture
  explicitly refuses to treat persons or animals as artistic material.
- The account identifies necessary connection, beneficiary, agency,
  proportion, alternatives, and sufferer participation as additional burdens.
- Chisholm's cautious conclusion is preserved: conceptual possibility does not
  establish that every actual evil is defeated.

## Scripture, providence, and position audit

- Job's divine speeches are not described as disclosing the case-specific
  reason given to the reader in chapters 1–2. The lecture distinguishes divine
  rule from the friends' false application.
- Romans 8's creation-wide groaning and hoped-for liberation are not reduced to
  private attitude change or used to call suffering good.
- Genesis 3 and Romans 8 support a canonical creation-fall-groaning-hope frame;
  they are not made to specify the historical cause of each natural process.
- General providence, kind-level reason, and token explanation are separated.
  The lectures neither infer “there is no reason” from human ignorance nor
  infer a specific reason from bare possibility.
- Stable order, higher-order goods, fall, nonhuman agency, compensation,
  defeat, judgment, and eschatological hope are represented as distinguishable
  proposals with different burdens.
- The confessional conclusions follow rather than replace fair presentation of
  the Swinburne–Stump dispute and the objections to Lewis.

## Pastoral, safety, and copyright audit

- No personal disclosure or real trauma narrative is required.
- The lectures do not attribute illness, disaster, animal pain, or grief to a
  victim's guilt, hidden need for growth, or demonstrated demonic cause.
- Providence is not used to discourage medical care, scientific inquiry,
  safety action, reporting, intervention, animal care, lament, or referral.
- Philosophical explanation is subordinated to consent, context, immediate
  safety, and practical support in pastoral use.
- The lectures are original synthesis. They reproduce no extended passage from
  Peterson, Swinburne, Stump, Lewis, Chisholm, an encyclopedia, or a
  copyrighted Bible edition.
