# Instructor Grading Guide — A-01-J06

Apply the student-facing rubric in `../rubric.md`. This guide clarifies
boundaries; it does not provide prose for the student's submission.

## Source and argument control

Accept a focused claim from:

- Swinburne, Peterson chapter 25, concerning natural processes, knowledge of
  consequences, significant choice, or responsive goods;
- Stump, Peterson chapter 26, concerning necessity, alternative means, or
  comparative value; or
- Lewis, *The Problem of Pain*, chapter 9, with its speculative status and
  limits preserved.

The entry must distinguish source statement from reconstruction. A
chapter-level location is sufficient for Lewis if the student's edition lacks
stable pagination; Peterson claims should use the verified chapter/page span
and a more precise page or digital location when available.

Full-credit pressure must touch the selected claim. Common valid pairings are:

- Swinburne's one-route claim with Stump's necessity or better-means challenge;
- Stump's criticism with a reply concerning independent inquiry, freedom, or
  serious responsibility;
- Lewis's consciousness or nonhuman-agency hypothesis with an evidential
  burden; or
- Lewis's admitted ignorance with a restrained conclusion that does not deny
  animal suffering.

Do not require one approved conclusion.

## Scripture and scope control

Strong entries use Job to join divine rule with rejection of the friends'
case-specific guilt inference, or Romans 8 to join present groaning with future
liberation. The text must not be used to claim that the selected philosophical
author accepts Scripture as a premise.

Major errors include:

- converting a possible or kind-level reason into a token explanation;
- claiming that Romans 8 identifies why one injury occurred;
- treating Lewis's speculation as scientific fact;
- claiming Stump proves that no natural evil could be justified;
- describing an unrelated later good as technical defeat; or
- blaming a sufferer or discouraging care.

## Feedback and authorship boundary

Feedback may identify a conflated source, missing premise, overclaimed scope,
or proof-text. It may ask the student to locate, reconstruct, or revise. Do not
write a replacement claim, objection, biblical synthesis, judgment, or pastoral
sentence. Apply course policy to fabricated citations or concealed prohibited
AI authorship rather than silently repairing the entry.
