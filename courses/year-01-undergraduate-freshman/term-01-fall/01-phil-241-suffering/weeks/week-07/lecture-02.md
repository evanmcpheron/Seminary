# Historical and Logical Synthesis

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lecture, the student should be able to:

1. sort the first half's sources by the question and argumentative task each
   addresses;
2. reconstruct a strict logical argument from evil and a Plantinga-style
   consistency response without confusing possibility, feasibility, and
   actuality;
3. compare privation, causal analysis, world comparison, evidential critique,
   free-will defense, stable-order proposal, and eschatological hope;
4. explain why the history is a conversation rather than a straight line of
   successive refutations;
5. use biblical sources as confessional norms without pretending that a
   contested premise has thereby been established for every interlocutor; and
6. state the unresolved questions that remain after logical consistency is
   defended.

## Key terms

**Privation:** corruption or lack of a good that ought to be present in an
otherwise good created nature.

**Accidental cause:** a cause that produces one effect while an evil follows
through a defect or as an unintended accompaniment, rather than as the object
directly intended under the description “evil.”

**Bridge principle:** an additional claim needed to derive a contradiction
from propositions that are not formally contradictory by themselves.

**Possible world:** a maximally complete way reality could coherently be.

**Feasible world:** in this course's Plantinga analysis, a possible world God
can actualize given the counterfactual free choices of creatures.

**Defense:** a possibly true model sufficient to answer a strict claim of
logical inconsistency.

**Theodicy:** an attempted account of God's actual reasons for permitting
evil.

**Compatibility:** the relation in which two claims can both be true.

**Evidential support:** the degree to which observed facts count for or against
a hypothesis; compatibility alone does not establish it.

**Conclusion scope:** the precise strength and range of what an argument has
established.

## Lecture

### 1. Synthesis is not a list of names

A weak survey answer gives each author one slogan. Augustine says evil is
privation; Aquinas says God does not cause evil; Leibniz says this is the best
world; Hume says evil makes theism unreasonable; Mackie says theism is
inconsistent; Plantinga says free will; Swinburne says natural evil teaches;
Stump objects; Lewis says pain has a purpose. Every phrase contains some
recognizable material, yet the list is misleading because it hides different
questions, kinds of argument, and levels of conclusion.

A genuine synthesis begins by separating at least four questions.

1. **What is evil?** This is an ontological question about whether evil is a
   substance, a positive nature, a privation, a relation, or something else.
2. **How is evil caused?** This asks about agency, defect, natural process,
   responsibility, and divine permission.
3. **Are God and evil logically compatible?** This is a formal question about
   whether a contradiction follows from a specified set of premises.
4. **What should suffering lead us to believe about God, and how should a
   Christian respond?** This includes evidential, theological, existential,
   biblical, and pastoral questions.

An answer to one can matter to another without replacing it. Privation may
clarify what evil is while leaving its amount unexplained. A free-will defense
may remove a strict contradiction while leaving animal suffering or
probability open. Eschatological hope may identify the confessed end of
creation without identifying the reason for a token loss.

Spend three minutes placing the following terms under their principal
question: *privation*, *accidental cause*, *bridge principle*, *transworld
depravity*, *stable order*, *defeat*, *lament*. Several may belong under more
than one heading, but explain each placement.

### 2. Augustine: the nature of evil and the will

The assigned Augustinian selection presents created natures as good insofar as
they exist. Evil is not a rival substance that God had to create. It is a
corruption, disorder, or deprivation in something that remains good as a
creature. Blindness, for example, is not “nothing” in the sense of being
unreal; it is the absence of a capacity that belongs to the flourishing of a
kind of creature. Injury can be painfully real while lacking the status of an
independently created evil substance.

This account has several strengths. It protects the goodness of creation,
refuses a dualism of coequal good and evil realities, and makes moral evil
intelligible as a disordered will rather than a new substance produced by the
sinner. It also prevents the misleading inference that if God creates every
substance, God must create evil as evil.

But notice the conclusion scope. A privation theory does not tell us why God
permits a particular deprivation, how much privation a good world may contain,
or whether the deprivation will be healed. It does not make suffering
illusory. It answers a metaphysical question and contributes to a causal
account; a full theodicy would need more.

### 3. Aquinas: causal precision

The assigned Aquinas selection adds a careful causal grammar. A good cause can
be involved in an effect in which evil occurs without intending the evil as
evil. A creaturely cause may aim at some apparent good while its action is
defective. The positive act and powers involved remain created goods; the
failure of right order belongs to the deficient operation. Aquinas also
distinguishes the evil of fault from penalty, which prevents every use of the
word *evil* from carrying the same causal analysis.

The key is not to reduce the argument to “God has nothing to do with events
containing evil.” Classical providence is a stronger doctrine than divine
absence. The causal distinctions aim to show why God's causation of creaturely
being and powers does not make God the defective moral cause of sin. The
account must still answer questions about permission, governance, natural
harm, and judgment.

Augustine and Aquinas thus offer related but nonidentical contributions.
Augustine's privation analysis asks what evil is and why it need not be a
created substance. Aquinas's causal analysis asks how good causes, creaturely
defects, fault, and penalty may be distinguished. Neither contribution is yet
a complete response to Hume's evidential challenge or to a grieving person's
question.

### 4. Leibniz: complete worlds and comparative judgment

Leibniz shifts attention toward complete possible worlds. The assigned
selection answers an objection that a perfectly wise and good creator would
choose a world without evil. His reply requires judging a world as a whole
rather than isolating one local feature. A world that contains no instance of
a certain evil is not necessarily better overall if the difference changes
the complete order and its goods.

This move supplies a powerful scope warning: “I can imagine this local evil
removed” does not yet describe a complete feasible alternative. Removing an
event while leaving every dependency unchanged may not be a coherent world
description. The comparison must be world-to-world rather than fragment-to-
fragment.

At the same time, the world-level perspective increases the burden. It is not
enough to say that an unknown connection might exist. A theodicy must explain
why the actual whole is appropriately related to divine wisdom and goodness.
It must address the standing of persons within the whole rather than treating
them as expendable parts of an aggregate. It must also distinguish logical
possibility, feasibility, and evidence that this world has the claimed value.

Leibniz therefore contributes a method of comparison and a substantive
proposal, not a shortcut around evidence. His response is stronger than the
bare claim that God and evil could coexist. That strength makes it open to
stronger criticism.

### 5. Hume: dialogue and the evidence of the world

Hume's *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* must first be read as a
dialogue. The assigned selection gives claims to Demea, Cleanthes, and Philo;
the student should not flatten every speech into “Hume says.” Philo's pressure
concerns what can reasonably be inferred from the experienced world. Even if
perfect power, wisdom, and goodness are compatible with suffering, the world's
mixture of pleasure, pain, regularity, vulnerability, and apparent
indifference may fail to provide the expected evidence for those attributes.

This distinction between compatibility and support is decisive:

- “The data do not contradict hypothesis H” is a compatibility claim.
- “The data are what we would expect if H were true” is an evidential claim.
- “H best explains the data compared with alternatives” is a comparative
  explanatory claim.

The first does not entail either of the others. A successful answer to strict
inconsistency may leave the evidential contest untouched. Conversely, an
evidential critic should not announce a formal contradiction without
supplying the premises that generate one.

Hume's assigned dialogue also pressures easy inferences from world to divine
character. A defender may propose reasons why a perfect God would permit a
world like this; the critic can still ask whether those reasons are probable,
whether they explain the observed patterns, and whether rival hypotheses fit
at least as well. Those issues prepare later study, but the present point is
already available: logical and evidential questions are not interchangeable.

### 6. Mackie: expose the bridge principles

Mackie's “Evil and Omnipotence” sharpens the logical challenge. The initial
triad is familiar:

1. God is omnipotent.
2. God is wholly good.
3. Evil exists.

No pair in this wording is a direct negation of the third. To derive a
contradiction, the argument needs bridge principles connecting goodness and
power to the elimination of evil. A simplified curriculum reconstruction is:

4. A wholly good being eliminates every evil it can eliminate.
5. An omnipotent being has no relevant limit on eliminating evil.
6. Therefore, if an omnipotent and wholly good God exists, no evil exists.
7. Evil exists.
8. Therefore, no omnipotent and wholly good God exists.

This reconstruction makes the points of pressure visible. A theist might
qualify premise 4: perfect goodness may permit an evil if preventing it would
lose a sufficiently related good or require a worse evil. Or the theist might
qualify premise 5: omnipotence does not include contradictions, and bringing
about certain freely chosen goods may not be compatible with determining the
choices. Mackie's own discussion tests replies that affirm the original
doctrines verbally while introducing qualifications inconsistently.

A responsible response must therefore do more than utter “free will.” It must
state the bridge principle rejected or qualified, provide a coherent model,
and preserve rather than quietly abandon the claimed divine attributes.
Likewise, a responsible critic should target the model's premise, coherence,
or scope rather than repeat the opening triad as though it were self-
contradictory.

### 7. Plantinga: possible worlds, feasibility, and limited victory

Plantinga's free-will defense answers the strict challenge by offering a
possibly true account. The morally significant freedom of creatures may be a
great good. If choices are genuinely free in the relevant sense, God cannot
both determine that a creature choose rightly and leave that same choice free.
The existence of a possible world in which creatures always choose rightly
does not establish that God can actualize it while preserving those choices as
their undetermined free acts.

This is where **feasibility** matters. A world can be logically possible in
itself yet unavailable for divine actualization given what free creatures
would do in relevant circumstances. The possible condition Plantinga calls
transworld depravity extends the point across creaturely essences and feasible
worlds. For the defense's narrow purpose, Plantinga need not prove that
transworld depravity is actual or necessary. He needs a coherent relevant
possibility that blocks the claimed entailment from divine attributes to a
world containing no moral evil.

The proper conclusion is limited:

1. It is possible that significantly free creatures sometimes choose wrongly.
2. It is possible that God could not determine their right choices while
   preserving significant freedom.
3. It is possible that every feasible world with the relevant creaturely
   freedom includes some wrongdoing.
4. Therefore the existence of moral evil need not be logically inconsistent
   with an omnipotent, wholly good God.

This does **not** establish that significant freedom is God's actual reason,
that every evil follows from freedom, that moral evil was inevitable in every
possible sense, that the actual amount and distribution are justified, or
that theism is evidentially probable. Plantinga briefly considers a possible
extension to natural evil, but Week 6 correctly refused to let a moral-evil
defense explain disease, earthquake, or animal pain automatically.

Take six minutes to write two columns titled “needed for defense” and “needed
for theodicy.” Place in them: coherence, possible moral reason, actual divine
reason, evidential support, amount, distribution, feasible alternatives, and
token explanation. Some items can appear in both if you explain why.

### 8. Swinburne and Stump: the natural-evil extension

Swinburne's assigned essay asks how natural evil could relate to morally
significant choice. Stable natural processes can provide knowledge of
consequences, make inquiry and prediction meaningful, and supply serious
opportunities for courage, rescue, compassion, negligence, or harm. In
curriculum reconstruction, the proposal moves from natural regularity and
knowledge toward responsible choice and higher-order goods.

But several strengths of conclusion must be separated:

1. Natural evil **can** provide relevant knowledge or opportunity.
2. Natural evil is **necessary** for that knowledge or opportunity.
3. The actual route through suffering is **better than feasible alternatives**.
4. The actual **amount and distribution** are justified.

The first does not entail the remaining three. Stump's reply presses precisely
this gap. Identifying induction from natural suffering as one means of learning
does not show that no less costly means is available. Even if the route has
distinctive value, the complete benefits and costs must be compared. Knowledge
of how to prevent suffering cannot by itself justify creating the need for
that knowledge.

The Swinburne–Stump exchange therefore illustrates how debate progresses
without a total victory. Swinburne explains a morally serious connection
between a regular world and agency. Stump asks whether the connection is
necessary and comparatively best. Her objection does not prove that no
natural evil could be justified; his proposal does not show that actual
natural evil is justified in detail.

### 9. Lewis as a cumulative Christian interpreter

Lewis crosses several categories. His account of omnipotence contributes to a
logical reply. His treatment of divine goodness proposes a more adequate
concept of love. His fall and freedom discussions give causal and theological
interpretations. His human-pain chapters suggest actual purposes and thus
assume a theodical burden. His animal chapter openly turns to speculation. His
chapters on hell and heaven locate suffering within judgment and final
communion.

That range is both a strength and a risk. Lewis sees that a Christian response
cannot be reduced to formal logic. Yet a cumulative story can acquire
rhetorical plausibility even when one connection lacks support. Each claim
must retain its own burden:

- coherence for a defense;
- textual and theological warrant for doctrine;
- evidence for an actual causal claim;
- comparison for a better-world or necessary-means claim;
- proportionality and beneficiary analysis for a morally sufficient reason;
- humility and explicit limits for a speculative hypothesis; and
- consent, safety, and non-coercion for pastoral use.

Chapter 10 supplies an eschatological end but does not erase these burdens.
Future communion can be integral to Christian hope without functioning as a
token explanation of every route to it.

### 10. Scripture: canon, evidence, and confessional norm

The Week 2 sources operate in several ways, and those roles should not be
conflated.

For Christian theology, Scripture is a **canonical norm**. Genesis 1–3 frames
creation as good and human rebellion as disordering; the lament psalms teach
faithful complaint; Job distinguishes the reader's partial information from
the sufferer's perspective and condemns the friends' overconfident speech;
Romans 8 joins groaning, the Spirit's aid, providence, conformity to Christ,
inseparable love, and future liberation; Revelation 21–22 joins judgment,
renewed creation, divine presence, and the end of death and pain.

In a philosophical exchange, biblical claims may also function as **premises**
within a stated Christian model. The student should name that role openly. A
critic who does not accept the authority claim can still assess whether the
model is coherent and whether the conclusion follows. A Christian cannot
answer an external evidential question merely by repeating a premise whose
warrant is itself disputed.

Scripture also controls **scope and practice**. Job does not authorize the
friends' movement from general doctrine to a token diagnosis. Psalm 88 does
not require lament to resolve before it counts as faithful speech. Romans 8
does not call groaning good or reduce providence to an explanation available
for each event. Revelation's promise that evil ends prevents a Christian
theodicy from making evil an eternal necessity.

Thus biblical integration is neither decorative proof-texting nor the removal
of Scripture from theology. It is disciplined role assignment: what does this
text state, how does it function in a Christian argument, what conclusion does
it support, and what inference does it forbid?

### 11. A first-half decision tree

When evaluating any argument from Weeks 1–7, move through these questions in
order.

**Question 1: What is the target?** Is the claim about evil's nature, causal
responsibility, logical consistency, evidential probability, actual divine
reason, Christian doctrine, existential orientation, or pastoral action?

**Question 2: What are the premises?** Write them in numbered form. If a
contradiction is alleged, expose every bridge principle. If a response appeals
to a good, state the premise connecting that good to permission.

**Question 3: What modal strength is claimed?** Is something possible,
feasible, necessary, probable, actual, or known? Do not allow movement among
these terms without an argument.

**Question 4: What kind of evil is addressed?** Moral, natural, and mixed
cases may need different causal accounts. Human goods cannot automatically
explain animal suffering.

**Question 5: What comparison is required?** Ask about amount, distribution,
proportion, alternatives, beneficiary, and agency. A local improvement does
not alone determine the value of a complete world; a global surplus does not
alone justify a cost imposed on a person.

**Question 6: What is the conclusion scope?** A defense gives compatibility,
not actuality. A general providence claim is not a token explanation. A
theological confession does not automatically supply shared evidence. A
pastoral response is not measured only by argumentative validity.

**Question 7: What remains morally required under uncertainty?** Protection,
treatment, investigation, lament, reporting, justice, referral, and practical
care do not wait for a completed theodicy.

### 12. What the first half has established

The first half supports several defensible conclusions.

- The basic divine-attribute claims and “evil exists” require bridge
  principles before a contradiction follows.
- A Plantinga-style possible-world model is a serious answer to the strict
  logical problem, especially for moral evil.
- That logical success does not settle evidential, natural-evil,
  distributional, animal, or pastoral questions.
- Privation and causal analysis clarify how evil can depend on created goods
  without being a separately created substance.
- World comparison and stable-order proposals expose dependencies ignored by
  simple local subtraction.
- Stronger proposals require stronger premises about actuality, necessity,
  comparative value, beneficiary, and feasible alternatives.
- Christian Scripture supplies a creation–fall–lament–providence–judgment–hope
  grammar while forbidding confident token diagnosis.
- Lewis offers a wide-ranging Christian synthesis whose insights and limits
  must both be retained.

The first half has **not** established that every actual evil is justified,
that every sufferer benefits, that theism's probability is unaffected by evil,
that a named general reason explains a token event, or that philosophical
analysis can replace lament and care. Those are not minor omissions. They are
the boundary between a precise conclusion and intellectual overreach.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Augustinian privation account:** evil corrupts created good rather than
  existing as a rival substance; disordered will helps explain moral evil.
- **Thomistic causal account:** good causes, creaturely defect, fault, penalty,
  intention, and accidental result must be distinguished.
- **Leibnizian whole-world account:** the relevant comparison concerns complete
  worlds and their ordered goods, not an isolated subtraction.
- **Humean dialogical critique:** compatibility does not by itself make perfect
  divine attributes a warranted inference from the experienced world.
- **Mackie's logical critique:** divine attributes and evil become inconsistent
  only through strong bridge principles, and verbal solutions must be tested
  for internal stability.
- **Plantinga's free-will defense:** a possibly true model involving
  significant freedom, feasibility, and possible transworld depravity blocks
  the strict entailment for moral evil.
- **Swinburne's natural-evil proposal:** stable natural consequences can supply
  knowledge and morally serious opportunity.
- **Stump's reply:** one route has not thereby been shown necessary or better
  than feasible alternatives.
- **Lewis's cumulative account:** Christian doctrines, conceptual arguments,
  analogies, and qualified hypotheses culminate in eschatological communion.

Each position deserves a direct reconstruction before criticism. None should
be reduced to the weakest popular version of its claim.

## Confessional analysis

A broadly evangelical Protestant synthesis can affirm the triune Creator's
goodness, power, providence, justice, mercy, human responsibility, the reality
of lament, Christ's redemptive work, resurrection, judgment, and renewed
creation. It can receive Augustine, Aquinas, and Lewis as important Christian
voices without treating their every philosophical or speculative proposal as
confessionally required.

The framework also requires intellectual honesty. A doctrinal commitment does
not authorize a false attribution, an invalid inference, an invented source,
or a claim to know God's reason for a specific loss. Christian hope should
make the student more willing to name evil truthfully, protect sufferers, seek
justice, and live within creaturely limits—not less willing.

## Summary

Weeks 1–7 contain a structured conversation, not a row of interchangeable
answers. Augustine and Aquinas clarify evil's ontological and causal
dependence on created good. Leibniz compares complete worlds; Hume pressures
the evidence of the experienced world. Mackie exposes bridge principles in a
strict inconsistency claim; Plantinga offers a limited consistency defense.
Swinburne connects stable natural processes to knowledge and choice; Stump
presses necessity and alternatives. Lewis gathers conceptual, theological,
imaginative, and eschatological strands into a popular Christian account.
Scripture norms the Christian synthesis while constraining overconfident token
claims. The disciplined student always identifies the question, premises,
modal strength, kind of evil, comparison class, conclusion scope, and duties
that remain under uncertainty.
