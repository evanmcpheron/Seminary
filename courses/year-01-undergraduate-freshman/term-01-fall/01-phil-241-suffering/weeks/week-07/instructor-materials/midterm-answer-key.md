## Part I — Concepts and distinctions, 20 points

Each item is worth 2.5 points under the student-facing scale.

### 1. Defense and theodicy

A **defense** supplies a possibly true, coherent model sufficient to show that
the relevant divine claims and evil are not logically inconsistent. A
**theodicy** proposes God's actual reason or reasons and consequently carries a
stronger truth and evidential burden. A defense's success does not establish a
theodicy, the truth or probability of theism, or a token explanation.

### 2. Privation

Evil is a corruption, disorder, or lack of a good that ought to be present in
a created nature, not an independently created rival substance. The subject,
capacity, injury, and deprivation can be real even though evil is ontologically
dependent on good. An answer that says “evil is unreal” is materially wrong.

### 3. Bridge principle

A bridge principle connects propositions that do not display a direct formal
contradiction. Divine power, divine goodness, and the existence of evil need
additional premises such as “a wholly good being eliminates every evil it can”
and “an omnipotent being has no relevant limit on eliminating evil.” Wording
may vary if scope is strong enough to derive the conclusion.

### 4. Possible and feasible worlds

A possible world is a maximally complete coherent way reality could be. A
feasible world is a possible world God can actualize given what significantly
free creatures would choose in the relevant circumstances. The mere
possibility of always-right free choices does not show God can determine or
actualize those same choices while preserving their relevant freedom.

### 5. Transworld depravity

Accept a precise course-level definition: the possible condition in which a
creaturely essence would go wrong at least once in every world feasible for
God in which that essence is instantiated with significant freedom. The
free-will defense invokes its **possibility**, not a proof that it is actual or
necessary. Do not require terminology beyond the assigned Plantinga selection.

### 6. Moral, natural, and mixed evil

- Moral evil is suffering or wrongdoing immediately attributable to a
  culpable creaturely choice.
- Natural evil identifies suffering not immediately classified as the result
  of such a choice.
- A mixed case contains natural processes and morally relevant action or
  omission.

Classification does not establish full causal history, evidence of
culpability, divine reason, or the response owed. A “mixed” label must not be
used to blame a victim.

### 7. Compatibility and evidential support

Claims are compatible if they can both be true. A fact evidentially supports a
hypothesis when it counts in its favor relative to relevant expectations or
alternatives. Failure to contradict a hypothesis does not by itself make the
fact evidence for it or show it is the best explanation.

### 8. Compensation, outweighing, and defeat

Compensation gives a benefit in response to loss without making the loss an
integral good. Outweighing means a whole contains more good than evil in
aggregate. Defeat is strongest: the evil is integrally related to a more
valuable whole rather than merely balanced by a separate good. Full credit
also requires recognizing that even defeat as a value category does not by
itself establish permission, necessity, or a token reason.

## Part II — Argument reconstruction and analysis, 30 points

### 9. Mackie and Plantinga, 15 points

#### Logical reconstruction, 6 points

Accept any valid reconstruction that includes the following functions:

1. God is omnipotent.
2. God is wholly good.
3. Evil exists.
4. A wholly good being eliminates every evil it can eliminate.
5. An omnipotent being has no relevant limit on eliminating evil.
6. Therefore, if an omnipotent and wholly good God exists, evil does not.
7. Evil exists.
8. Therefore, an omnipotent and wholly good God does not exist.

The student's wording may include qualifications concerning preventable evil
or morally sufficient reasons. If a qualification makes the argument invalid,
score the missing inference rather than insisting on this exact wording.

- divine-attribute and evil premises: 2 points;
- bridge principles strong enough to do the work: 2 points;
- valid inference and explicit conclusion: 2 points.

#### Plantinga analysis and limits, 9 points

Full-credit analysis includes:

- the exact resistance: premise 4 or 5, or their unqualified conjunction,
  fails if eliminating moral evil would require eliminating a greater good of
  significant freedom or determining acts that must remain free (2 points);
- significant freedom involves morally meaningful choices for which the
  creature, not God, determines the act in the relevant sense (2 points);
- possible/feasible distinction: an always-right world may be possible without
  being actualizable by God with those same free choices (2 points);
- possible transworld depravity may block every relevant feasible world from
  containing significant freedom without wrongdoing (1 point); and
- two accurate limits, 1 point each: no proof of actual divine reason, no proof
  that transworld depravity is actual or necessary, no evidential victory, no
  justification of amount/distribution, no automatic natural-evil account, no
  token explanation (2 points).

Do not accept “God cannot stop evil because humans are free” without the modal
and feasibility analysis.

### 10. Swinburne and Stump, 15 points

#### Swinburne reconstruction, 7 points

An acceptable curriculum reconstruction should contain these steps:

1. Morally significant agency requires relevant knowledge of consequences and
   serious opportunities to affect others.
2. A stable natural order allows agents to learn consequences through
   experience, induction, testimony, and inquiry.
3. Natural harms and risks can make consequences knowable and create serious
   choices involving prevention, rescue, courage, compassion, negligence, or
   harm.
4. Such knowledge and opportunities are connected to goods of responsible
   freedom.
5. Therefore natural evil can have a role in a world containing morally
   significant choice.

Score 2 points for the knowledge/choice good, 2 for stable process and
learning, 2 for serious opportunity and higher-order goods, and 1 for a
properly limited conclusion. A student may reconstruct a stronger Swinburne
claim if the later objections target its added premise accurately.

#### Stump and verdict, 8 points

- **Necessity pressure, 3 points:** showing that harmful processes are one
  route to relevant knowledge does not show there is no less costly route or
  that actual suffering is required.
- **Alternative-means/comparative-value pressure, 3 points:** any distinctive
  value of learning through actual harm must be compared with feasible
  alternatives and full moral costs; knowledge of preventing harm cannot alone
  justify creating the need for it.
- **Limited verdict, 2 points:** Swinburne identifies an intelligible
  connection between stable order, knowledge, and agency; Stump shows that
  necessity and comparative superiority require more. Neither side thereby
  establishes a complete verdict about every actual natural evil.

## Part III — Historical and theological comparison, 25 points

Use the public rubric's four criteria. The following are minimum content
anchors, not a required essay outline.

### Historical contributions

- **Augustine:** the ontological question; evil as privation/corruption of good
  created nature and moral disorder of will. This avoids rival-substance
  dualism but does not explain permission, amount, or distribution.
- **Aquinas:** the causal question; good causes, direct and accidental result,
  creaturely defect, fault, and penalty. This distinguishes God's causation of
  created being and power from the defect in culpable action but does not make
  providence absence or complete a natural-evil theodicy.
- **Lewis:** a cumulative Christian interpretation using coherent omnipotence,
  perfecting love, rebellion/fall, proposed uses of pain, judgment, admitted
  animal speculation, and eschatological communion. Its various moves carry
  different confidence and burdens.

### Required distinctions

- Nature asks what evil is; cause asks how it arises and who is responsible;
  reason for permission asks why a good, powerful God allows it. An answer may
  inform another without replacing it.
- Lewis's omnipotence/freedom moves can contribute to a consistency proposal.
  Claims that God actually uses pain for awakening, formation, surrender, or
  an eschatological whole assume the stronger theodical burden.

### Acceptable critical pressures

A strong **Mackie-style** answer identifies a bridge between perfect goodness,
power, and preventable evil and asks whether Lewis's qualifications remain
consistent or merely restrict a divine attribute. A strong **Humean** answer
grants possible compatibility and asks whether observed kinds, amounts, or
distributions support the asserted divine character or Lewis's actual-purpose
claims. Either must name a particular Lewis premise or inference.

### Evaluation

Full-credit judgments may be favorable, critical, or mixed. They should grant
that Lewis supplies an internally relevant eschatological horizon and several
coherent possible connections, while testing actuality, necessity, feasible
alternatives, amount, distribution, beneficiary, animal suffering, and token
scope. Do not require the student's verdict to match the curriculum's
confessional reception.

## Part IV — Biblical-philosophical synthesis, 25 points

### Job anchors, 6 points

- The reader of Job 1–2 receives heavenly-scene information unavailable to Job
  and the friends.
- Job is not told that his suffering results from the retributive guilt alleged
  by the friends.
- The divine speeches display creaturely epistemic limits and a wider divine
  governance; they do not provide Job with a simple token causal explanation.
- The concluding verdict rejects the friends' overconfident application rather
  than authorizing it.

### Romans 8 anchors, 6 points

The answer should relate creation-wide groaning and bondage to hope of
liberation; the Spirit's help in weakness; conformity to Christ as the context
of providential “good”; inseparable divine love; and future glory. It should
not say the text calls each event good, promises immediate explanation, or
reduces redemption to a private benefit produced by harm.

### Invalid inferences and scope, 7 points

Any two well-explained errors can earn full credit:

- ignorance entails an explanation;
- a possible unknown reason is a known morally sufficient reason;
- divine governance entails the friends' retributive diagnosis;
- comparison with glory makes suffering good in itself;
- a promised final end entails that every means to it is necessary;
- a general providence claim entails a token reason;
- later benefit automatically justifies earlier permission;
- Christian confession can be imposed as shared evidential warrant without
  argument.

The student must distinguish general Christian confession from a reason for a
particular event.

### Constructive synthesis, 6 points

A sufficient conclusion may affirm divine presence, providence, faithful
lament, the Spirit's aid, inseparable love, future judgment and liberation,
and hope, while declining a token diagnosis. It must keep protection,
treatment, truth-telling, justice, referral, and practical care in view. Penalize
within this criterion any claim assigning hidden guilt or purpose to a victim
or discouraging appropriate care.

## Common diagnostic errors

- **Attribution:** “Hume says” without attending to dialogue speakers;
  Plantinga vocabulary attributed to Augustine; Lewis's speculation reported
  as doctrine.
- **Structure:** the initial triad repeated without bridge principles; an
  objection stated without its point of contact.
- **Modal scope:** possible becomes feasible, actual, necessary, or known;
  actual benefit becomes sufficient justification.
- **Problem type:** moral-evil freedom response applied automatically to
  natural or animal suffering.
- **Value relation:** compensation, outweighing, and defeat treated as
  synonyms.
- **Biblical context:** reader information given to Job; the friends' diagnosis
  endorsed; groaning called good; hope made a token cause.
- **Conclusion scope:** defense becomes theodicy; general providence becomes
  token explanation.

Mark these categories in feedback and point to a completed course location.
Do not rewrite the student's answer.
