## Part I — Concepts and distinctions, 20 points

Answer each item in two to four precise sentences. Each item is worth 2.5
points.

1. Distinguish a **defense** from a **theodicy** by task, evidential burden,
   and proper conclusion.
2. Define **privation** and explain why calling evil a privation does not make
   an injury unreal.
3. Define a **bridge principle** and explain why the familiar propositions
   about divine power, divine goodness, and the existence of evil need one to
   yield a contradiction.
4. Distinguish a **possible world** from a **feasible world** in the
   Plantinga-style analysis used in this course.
5. Define **transworld depravity** and state the modal strength at which it is
   used in the free-will defense.
6. Distinguish **moral evil**, **natural evil**, and a **mixed causal case**.
   State why the categories do not by themselves settle responsibility.
7. Distinguish **compatibility** from **evidential support**.
8. Distinguish **compensation**, **outweighing**, and **defeat**. State which
   relation is strongest and why.

## Part II — Argument reconstruction and analysis, 30 points

### 9. Mackie and Plantinga, 15 points

In numbered premise-conclusion form, reconstruct a strict logical argument
from evil that includes claims about divine power, divine goodness, and evil,
plus the bridge principles needed to derive the conclusion. Then:

- identify the exact premise or inference a Plantinga-style free-will defense
  resists;
- explain the roles of significant freedom and the distinction between
  possible and feasible worlds; and
- state **two conclusions** that the defense does not establish.

Do not treat the mere words “free will” as an argument.

### 10. Swinburne and Stump, 15 points

Reconstruct the Swinburne natural-evil proposal assigned in Week 6 as a
numbered argument connecting stable natural processes, knowledge of
consequences, and morally significant choice. Then present separately:

- Stump's pressure on the claim of **necessity**;
- Stump's pressure concerning **alternative means or comparative value**; and
- a limited verdict stating what the exchange does and does not establish
  about actual natural evil.

## Part III — Historical and theological comparison, 25 points

### 11. Nature, cause, and reason

Compare the principal questions and contributions of:

- Augustine's privation account;
- Aquinas's causal distinctions; and
- Lewis's cumulative account in *The Problem of Pain*, including chapter 10.

Your comparison must:

1. explain why an account of evil's **nature** is not yet a complete account
   of its **cause** or God's **reason for permission**;
2. identify one part of Lewis's project that functions like a consistency
   proposal and one that assumes the stronger burden of a theodicy;
3. present the strongest relevant Humean or Mackie-style pressure on a
   specified Lewis claim; and
4. give a reasoned, limited judgment about whether Lewis answers that pressure.

Represent every position charitably before evaluation. Agreement with the
course's confessional framework is neither necessary nor sufficient for full
credit.

## Part IV — Biblical-philosophical synthesis, 25 points

### 12. Canonical hope and explanatory restraint

Evaluate this composite claim:

> Job shows that human ignorance is itself a complete explanation of every
> instance of suffering. Romans 8 shows that every harmful event is good
> because it produces glory. Therefore a Christian can tell a sufferer that
> the particular loss was necessary for that person's good.

Using Job 1–2 and 38–42 and Romans 8:18–39, write an integrated response that:

1. distinguishes what the reader, Job, and Job's friends know;
2. explains what the divine speeches and the verdict concerning the friends
   support and do not support;
3. relates creation's groaning, hope, the Spirit's aid, conformity to Christ,
   inseparable love, and future liberation without calling suffering good in
   itself;
4. identifies at least **two invalid inferences** in the composite claim;
5. distinguishes a general Christian confession from a token explanation; and
6. states a constructive Christian conclusion that preserves lament, care,
   protection, truth-telling, and hope under uncertainty.

You may paraphrase the passages. No exact quotation or recalled
translation-specific wording is required.
