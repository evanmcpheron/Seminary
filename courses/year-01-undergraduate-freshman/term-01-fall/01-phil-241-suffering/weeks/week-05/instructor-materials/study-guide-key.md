# Instructor Key — Week 5 Study Guide

This key supports ungraded self-correction. Accept substantively equivalent
answers grounded in the assigned sources. Do not distribute it before the
student attempts every section.

## 1. Source-control anchors

- Plantinga's distinction between a defense and a theodicy appears in the
  assigned chapter in Peterson, chapter 8, pp. 95–129.
- His account of significant freedom, possible worlds, feasible worlds, and
  transworld depravity develops within that same assigned chapter. Require a
  page in the student's lawful edition or a stable licensed location marker.
- Lewis's chapters 6–7 discuss human pain, creaturely self-sufficiency, the
  possible formative uses of pain, and God's ordering of creaturely evil
  without converting the wrongdoer's intention into good.
- Lewis does not treat pain as intrinsically good or authorize readers to
  identify God's purpose in a particular sufferer's case.

A chapter-wide citation identifies the discussion but does not by itself
support a student's evaluative inference. The student must distinguish a
source report, a reconstruction of reasoning, and a personal judgment.

## 2. Defense and theodicy

| Question | Defense | Theodicy |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate target | A stated objection, especially alleged logical inconsistency | The demand for an account of God's actual reasons or purposes |
| Must the added proposition be actual? | No; relevant possibility may suffice against a necessity claim | Yes, or at least the account is offered as true of the actual world |
| Evidence burden | Coherence and relevant possibility | Positive warrant for the proposed actual explanation |
| Moral burden | The proposed reason must be possibly consistent with perfect goodness | The asserted reason must be morally adequate for actual evils |
| If successful | The targeted incompatibility objection fails | Some actual evil receives the proposed explanation |
| Still open | Truth, probability, natural evil, distribution, and pastoral response | Further evils, competing evidence, and pastoral response |

Classification:

1. **D:** a possibility claim aimed at moral-evil consistency.
2. **T, but overclaimed:** it asserts an actual universal reason without the
   evidence or moral argument needed to sustain it.
3. **D:** this is the limited conclusion of a successful logical defense.
4. **T-shaped but irresponsible as stated:** it asserts a case-specific reason
   without evidence and risks instrumentalizing a sufferer.
5. **N:** this is a pastoral and safeguarding response, not a defense or
   theodicy.

## 3. World and feasibility map

| World | Logical status | Feasibility status | Moral content |
|---|---|---|---|
| `W-A` | Possible as described, but lacks significant freedom regarding the determined acts | The description alone supplies no Plantingan freedom obstacle to divine actualization | Right action without significantly free moral good; no moral evil stated |
| `W-B` | Possible | Not automatically feasible; a true counterfactual free response may place it outside God's power to actualize without determining the choice | Significantly free moral good; no moral evil |
| `W-C` | Possible | May be feasible, but the description alone does not settle feasibility | Significantly free moral good may occur, and at least one moral evil occurs |
| `W-D` | Impossible on the stipulated same-choice, same-respect description | Therefore not feasible | The moral inventory is not reached because the description is contradictory |

`W-B` most clearly exposes the gap between possibility and feasibility.

A sound eight-line reconstruction should include this sequence:

1. Mackie claims that an omnipotent God could make significantly free persons
   who always freely do right.
2. A world containing such choices may be logically possible.
3. It does not follow that this world is feasible for God to actualize while
   preserving those choices as undetermined in the relevant sense.
4. Which world results partly depends on what free creatures would do in the
   relevant circumstances.
5. It is possible that every creatable significantly free person suffers from
   transworld depravity.
6. If so, every feasible world with significant creaturely freedom includes
   at least some wrongdoing.
7. God cannot causally guarantee the contrary choice while preserving that
   same choice as libertarian-free.
8. Therefore Mackie's always-right-world inference has not established a
   contradiction; no actual theodicy follows.

Lines 3–8 are normally reconstructions or implications and must not be
presented as quotations unless exact source wording is verified.

## 4. Transworld-depravity scope test

1. **Required by the defense:** more precisely, its possibility is the relevant
   premise; the defense does not claim actuality.
2. **Misstatement:** some possible worlds contain free creatures who always do
   right; the issue is whether such a world is feasible for God.
3. **Misstatement:** a never-sinning free creature is possible; sin is not
   included in the definition of freedom.
4. **Misstatement:** the defense need not assert that universal transworld
   depravity is actual.
5. **Misstatement:** the defense does not claim that universal transworld
   depravity is necessary.
6. **Required by the defense:** this is the core possible/feasible distinction.
7. **Misstatement:** causal determination and libertarian indetermination
   cannot both characterize the same choice in the same respect.
8. **Required by the defense:** a coherent possibility model answers a strict
   necessary-incompatibility claim.

Accept corrected formulations that preserve possibility language and do not
turn transworld depravity into an empirical diagnosis of actual persons.

## 5. Objection classification

1. **Theory of freedom** and, secondarily, **theological coherence**.
2. **Feasibility machinery**, especially the status of counterfactuals of
   creaturely freedom.
3. **Actual theodicy** and **evidential probability**; it questions whether the
   value of undetermined intention warrants permitting completed harm.
4. **Theological coherence** and **theory of freedom**.
5. **Actual theodicy** or explanatory scope; moral freedom does not directly
   account for natural evil.
6. **Evidential probability** and moral adequacy within an **actual theodicy**.
7. **Pastoral use**; it also identifies a safeguarding failure, not a modal
   counterexample.

The selected objection must be attached to a particular premise or inference.
Credit a partial reply when it narrows a conclusion without resolving the full
objection.

## 6. Lewis and constructive synthesis

- **Simple good:** creaturely good received from and ordered toward God.
- **Simple evil:** a creature's rebellious willing or action considered as
  evil, without its later providential context.
- **Complex good:** God's good ordering or redemptive use of a history that
  includes creaturely evil, without calling the evil good.
- **Why complex good does not justify the wrongdoer:** the wrongdoer's evil
  intention remains the wrongdoer's; providential good is not the agent's
  moral achievement.
- **Why possible formation proves no case-specific purpose:** a general
  possible good neither identifies God's actual reason nor authorizes a
  diagnosis of another person's suffering.

The synthesis should limit Plantinga's result to possible consistency, identify
Lewis's stronger formative or providential proposal, state a serious objection,
affirm providence without denying creaturely responsibility, and require
safety, lament, presence, and humility in pastoral use.

The final sentence should say, in substance: Plantinga needs universal
transworld depravity (or the relevant feasibility condition) to be **possible**;
he does not need to prove that it is **actual or necessary**.
