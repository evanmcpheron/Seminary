# Plantinga's Free-Will Defense and Its Limits

## Learning objectives

After completing this lecture, the student should be able to:

- reconstruct Mackie's claim that God could create free creatures who always
  choose rightly;
- distinguish significant freedom from causal determination and randomness;
- distinguish a logically possible world from a world feasible for God to
  actualize while preserving creaturely freedom;
- explain the role and exact modal scope of transworld depravity;
- test the defense against objections involving freedom, feasibility,
  intervention, heaven, natural evil, amount, and providence; and
- state precisely what the defense establishes if successful.

## Key terms

**Significant freedom:** freedom concerning an action that is morally
significant and for which praise or blame can be appropriate. **Libertarian
freedom:** freedom understood to exclude causal determination of the choice by
prior conditions or another agent. **Compatibilism:** the view that relevant
freedom and moral responsibility can coexist with determinism. **Possible
world:** a complete possible way reality could be. **Feasible world:** a world
God can actualize given what significantly free creatures would do in its
circumstances. **Actualization:** making a possible state of affairs actual.
**Strong actualization:** bringing about a complete state of affairs by
determining all of its relevant components. **Counterfactual of creaturely
freedom:** a claim about what a creature would freely do if placed in specified
circumstances. **Creaturely essence:** the properties essential to a possible
created person, used in Plantinga's technical formulation. **Transworld
depravity:** roughly, the condition under which a possible creature would go
wrong in at least one relevant free action in every all-right world God could
actualize for that creature without determining the choice. **Selective
intervention:** preventing particular wrong free actions while allowing other
free actions to proceed.

## Lecture

### 1. Mackie's sharper objection

The simplest free-will response says that God created free creatures, freedom
is valuable, and creatures misused it. Mackie's reply is not merely that the
story lacks detail. He asks why an omnipotent God could not create persons who
are free and nevertheless always choose rightly. A person sometimes chooses
rightly without being forced. Why could every person not do so on every
morally significant occasion?[^1]

The objection blocks a careless dilemma:

- either God creates programmed automata with no moral agency; or
- God creates free persons and moral evil must occur.

Mackie correctly observes that there is a third description: free persons who,
on every occasion, choose the good. The description is not self-contradictory.
Plantinga agrees. His reply cannot therefore be that moral evil follows by
logical necessity from free will.

Instead, Plantinga asks a more exact question: even if such a world is possible,
is it necessarily within God's power to *actualize* it while leaving the
creatures significantly free? A possible world may include actions whose
outcomes depend on agents other than God. The difference between *possible*
and *feasible for God to actualize* is the center of the defense.

### 2. Significant freedom

Plantinga begins with moral significance. A person is significantly free with
respect to an action when the action is morally significant and the person is
free to perform or refrain from it. On Plantinga's libertarian understanding,
God does not causally determine which option the person chooses.[^1]

First, the defense concerns **responsible agency**, not mere unpredictability.
A random muscular spasm is undetermined in an ordinary sense but is not a
morally responsible act. Significant freedom belongs to a person who acts for
reasons and whose action can properly receive praise or blame.

Second, freedom need not mean absence of character, desire, persuasion, law,
or grace. A person's history and loves may strongly shape choice. The thesis
needed by Plantinga is narrower: in the relevant respect, the action is not
causally settled by God or antecedent conditions so that the creature could not
do otherwise.

Third, significant freedom does not make every option valuable. The freedom to
betray is not valuable because betrayal is valuable. The proposed good is the
capacity for responsible love and obedience, a capacity that includes a real
possibility of refusal on this account.

Compatibilists instead locate freedom in voluntary action from the person's own
desires and reasons, even with a determining history. Plantinga's defense does
not refute that view; it constructs a consistency model using libertarian
freedom.

### 3. Possible worlds are not alternate planets

A possible world is a complete possible way reality could be. It is not a
second planet or a region elsewhere in space. When we say, “There is a possible
world in which Mara returns the wallet,” we mean that reality could have been
such that Mara returned it, along with a complete consistent account of
everything else.

Possible-world language helps separate two claims:

1. There is no contradiction in describing free creatures who always choose
   rightly.
2. God can bring about that complete world while those choices remain free in
   Plantinga's sense.

Claim 1 does not entail claim 2. There can be a possible history in which a
student freely reports accurate data. A teacher can arrange the circumstances
and explain the duty but cannot both cause that truthful choice and leave it
undetermined by the teacher in the same respect. Greater power would not make
that combination coherent.

### 4. Actualization and creaturely contribution

Plantinga's assigned text asks whether God can create any possible world God
pleases. The answer is no if a complete world contains significantly free acts
not determined by God. God can bring about the creatures and circumstances,
but what the creatures freely do also contributes to which complete world
becomes actual.[^1]

The technical discussion distinguishes what God **strongly actualizes**—what
God directly brings about or causally determines—from a fuller world completed
through creaturely free action. Introductory presentations sometimes call the
latter *weak actualization*: God brings about a relevant prior world segment,
knowing what the creature would freely do, and the creature supplies the
undetermined action.

The distinction is not a limitation imposed by a power outside God. It follows
from the content of the proposed task:

- “Cause Ari to choose truthfully” describes causal production.
- “Leave Ari's choice undetermined in the relevant respect” excludes that
  causal production.
- “Cause Ari's choice while not causing it in that respect” is contradictory.

Omnipotence does not include the power to make a contradiction true. This does
not yet show that God cannot prevent any particular wrong or create different
persons. It establishes only why causal guarantee and libertarian significant
freedom cannot be combined in the same action under the same description.

### 5. The difference between possibility and feasibility

Imagine world `W-good`, in which every created person is significantly free
and always chooses rightly. Plantinga can grant that `W-good` is possible. Why
might it not be feasible for God?

Because the true counterfactuals about what creatures would freely do may not
be under God's causal control. For any candidate person and circumstances, it
may be that if God placed that person in those circumstances, the person would
freely choose wrongly. God can choose whether to create the person and provide
the circumstances. On the libertarian model, God cannot determine the free
choice's outcome while preserving it as undetermined.

A world can be possible because its description contains
no contradiction, yet not be actualizable by a particular agent whose action
must respect other agents' freedom. “It is possible that Ari freely tells the
truth” does not entail “I can make Ari freely tell the truth.” The latter would
make Ari's allegedly free response partly my causal product.

Plantinga therefore does not answer Mackie by denying the possibility of an
always-right free world. He denies the inference from that possibility to the
claim that such a world must be available for God to actualize.

### 6. Transworld depravity without caricature

Plantinga introduces **transworld depravity** to describe a possible barrier to
an all-right feasible world. His full definition uses creaturely essences,
world segments, significantly free actions, and counterfactuals. The
undergraduate operational point is this:

> It is possible that every candidate free creature is such that, in any
> candidate all-right world God could actualize for that creature without
> determining its choices, the creature would freely go wrong in at least one
> relevant circumstance.

1. It does **not** say that the phrase “free creature who never sins” is
   contradictory.
2. It does **not** say that every possible world containing freedom contains
   moral evil.
3. It does **not** require Plantinga to prove that every actual person in fact
   has transworld depravity.
4. It does **not** say transworld depravity is necessary.

The defense needs the relevant universal transworld-depravity proposition to be
**possible**. If it is possible, then it is possible that no world with the
desired quantity of significant moral good and no moral evil was feasible for
God. That possible state of affairs can join the theistic claims and the
existence of moral evil without contradiction.[^1]

The modal placement of *possible* is decisive. The argument is not:

> Necessarily, every free creature sins.

It is closer to:

> Possibly, the creaturely counterfactuals are such that every candidate world
> God could actualize with significantly free creatures includes some wrong
> free action.

Confusing these claims makes Plantinga's position both stronger and less
plausible than the defense requires.

### 7. The free-will defense reconstructed

The argument can be displayed as a consistency model:

1. If God exists, God is omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good.
2. A world containing significantly free creatures who sometimes do right has
   a kind of moral good unavailable in a world of creatures whose every action
   is causally determined.
3. God cannot causally determine a significantly free choice while leaving it
   undetermined in the respect required for significant freedom.
4. A possible world in which creatures always freely choose rightly need not be
   a world God can actualize.
5. It is possible that every creaturely essence suitable for significant
   freedom suffers from transworld depravity.
6. If 5 is true, every world feasible for God that contains the relevant
   significant freedom includes some moral evil.
7. It is possible that the good of significant freedom gives God a morally
   sufficient reason to actualize such a world despite its included moral evil.
8. Therefore it is possible that God is omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly
   good while moral evil exists.

Premises 2, 5, and 7 carry much of the contestable content. Against a logical
incompatibility claim, they do not all need to be shown actual; the combined
model must be possible. Against an evidential argument or as a theodicy, their
mere possibility is insufficient.

**Primary-source claim:** Plantinga's defense is aimed at the consistency of
theism and evil, not at producing a revealed history of God's actual reasons.
The world-actualization and transworld-depravity material answers Mackie's
specific claim that omnipotence guarantees access to a morally perfect world
containing free creatures.[^1]

### 8. Objections about freedom and feasibility

The defense faces serious objections even within its limited scope.

**The libertarian-freedom objection:** A compatibilist denies premise 3's
definition. If persons can be morally responsible for determined actions, God
might causally secure righteous action without removing relevant freedom. This
does not immediately revive Mackie's contradiction; it challenges the
particular countermodel. The defender must either support libertarian
possibility or offer another consistency model.

**The counterfactual-grounding objection:** What makes a claim about what a
never-actualized creature *would freely do* true? If nothing grounds such
counterfactuals, the feasible-world framework may lack the truth conditions it
needs. This is a metaphysical objection to the machinery, not evidence that a
specific person would choose differently.

**The possibility objection:** A defense cannot establish possibility merely
by failing to notice a contradiction. Critics may ask for positive reason to
think universal transworld depravity is coherent. Plantinga's task is not to
show it probable, but the possibility premise must be more than a verbal
placeholder.

**The value objection:** Why think libertarian significant freedom has enough
value to justify the risk of severe harm? A bare ranking of “freedom” above
“safety” ignores the variety of freedoms, victims' agency, and possible limits.
This objection most directly pressures premise 7 and any move from defense to
theodicy.

### 9. Selective intervention, heaven, and divine freedom

Even if God cannot determine every free choice, a critic may ask why God could
not selectively prevent the worst consequences. Preventing a weapon from
functioning need not determine the assailant's intention. Warning a potential
victim, strengthening resistance, or limiting harm may preserve substantial
agency. Why is a world with fewer successful atrocities not feasible?

This **selective-intervention objection** may not restore a contradiction with
the bare existence of *some* moral evil. It powerfully challenges the amount
and distribution of evil and any claim that freedom explains every divine
nonintervention. A defense of compatibility needs only some moral evil; a
theodicy of this world must say much more.

**Heaven** creates a related theological question. Christian hope anticipates
redeemed persons who are no longer enslaved to sin. If they remain free, why
could God not create that condition initially? Replies distinguish mature from
unformed freedom, an achieved history from an initial state, or freedom to act
according to perfected character from the power to sin. These proposals need
argument. The objection pressures definitions of freedom and a complete
Christian theodicy, even if it does not by itself disprove the possibility of
Plantinga's model.

**Divine freedom** adds another test. God is perfectly good and does not sin,
yet Christian theology calls God's acts free. If the ability to choose evil is
required for every valuable freedom, divine goodness seems to remove divine
freedom. A defender may distinguish God's perfect freedom to act according to
the divine nature from a finite creature's morally significant choice among
alternatives. The distinction prevents a creaturely model from becoming a
complete definition of freedom as such.

### 10. Natural evil and the quantitative problem

The defense begins with moral evil. Earthquakes, congenital disease, predation,
and suffering not directly caused by culpable human choice are not explained by
the ordinary appeal to human freedom. Plantinga explores a logically possible
extension in which natural evil results from the free action of nonhuman
created persons. For a defense against strict inconsistency, such a proposal
needs possibility rather than evidence of actuality.[^1]

The proposal's very modesty must be explicit. It does not show that nonhuman
agents actually cause disease or disaster. It does not give the student
permission to make that diagnosis. Its theological plausibility and
explanatory power are separate questions. Week 6 addresses natural evil and
providence without treating this modal extension as an established account.

The **amount problem** also remains. If some moral evil is compatible with God,
does the defense justify every degree of it? As a matter of strict logic, a
model might be extended to a great amount of freely chosen wrongdoing. But
questions about whether a good God would permit this severity, whether fewer
harms were feasible, and whether the goods are proportionate are evidential
and moral questions. Logical compatibility does not make them disappear.

The **distribution problem** focuses on who bears the consequences. The
freedom of powerful wrongdoers can destroy the freedom, safety, and life of
victims. A theodicy must not count only the agent's capacity to choose while
ignoring the sufferer's agency and good. An account of final judgment and
restoration may be relevant within Christian theology, but it must not be used
to minimize present wrong or postpone preventable protection.

### 11. Providence, culpability, and action

Plantinga's defense is compatible with the claim that God knowingly permits
creaturely evil. Does that make God culpable? The logical model says permission
can be justified by a morally sufficient reason; it does not prove that every
actual permission has been justified.

A responsible Christian synthesis needs several distinctions:

- God gives and sustains created powers; the creature exercises agency.
- God may permit a wrongful action without sharing the wrongdoer's intention.
- Creaturely culpability does not disappear under divine foreknowledge.
- Divine providence does not make the wrong action good.
- Human agents remain obligated to prevent harm, protect victims, resist evil,
  and pursue justice.

Genesis 1–3 portrays creaturely rebellion within God's world and under God's
judgment. James 1 denies that God tempts people to evil. Acts 2 holds together
God's saving purpose in the cross and the culpability of those who acted
lawlessly. These texts do not provide Plantinga's modal premises, but they
constrain a Christian use of the defense.[^2]

Lewis's distinction among simple good, simple evil, and complex good helps
here. God may bring redemptive good from a history containing creaturely evil
without the evil becoming God's moral intention or the wrongdoer becoming an
innocent instrument. Providence supplies hope that evil will be judged and
overcome; it does not excuse avoidable human inaction.[^3]

### 12. What the defense establishes

The conclusion should be stated in two columns.

**If Plantinga's relevant possibility claims are coherent, the defense
establishes:**

- Mackie's claim that an omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good God and moral
  evil are logically incompatible has a countermodel;
- omnipotence need not include causally determining an action while leaving it
  libertarian-free in the same respect; and
- the possibility of an always-right free world does not entail that such a
  world is feasible for God to actualize.

**The defense does not by itself establish:**

- that libertarian freedom is the correct full theory of human agency;
- that universal transworld depravity is actual, necessary, or probable;
- that freedom is God's actual reason for every permission;
- that natural evil has been explained;
- that this amount and distribution of suffering are justified;
- that theism is probable on the total evidence; or
- that a sufferer has received an adequate pastoral response.

**Broad scholarly consensus:** Contemporary surveys commonly treat Plantinga's
defense as a successful answer to the classical logical inconsistency charge.
That assessment is narrower than a consensus that the problem of evil as a
whole is solved. The move from logical to evidential arguments in later debate
reflects precisely this distinction.[^4]

**Confessional conclusion:** The course judges Plantinga's model to be a
serious and successful defense against the strict logical problem of moral
evil. It also judges that Christian doctrine requires more than the model:
providence, judgment, the cross, resurrection, new creation, lament, mercy, and
active opposition to evil. These conclusions are independent curriculum
judgments, not official Wheaton College teaching.

**Curriculum synthesis:** Use the defense to answer the argument it addresses.
Do not use it to explain a particular person's trauma, blame a victim, protect
an offender, or obstruct reporting and intervention. The philosophical claim
that God may value freedom does not imply that human beings should preserve
another person's opportunity to abuse.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Mackie's improved-world objection:** if free right action is possible on
  each occasion, an omnipotent God should be able to create a world in which
  everyone always freely chooses rightly.
- **Plantinga's free-will defense:** a possible all-right free world need not
  be feasible for God; possibly, every candidate significantly free creature
  would go wrong in at least one relevant circumstance.
- **Libertarian critique:** significant freedom excludes causal determination
  of the action. Critics dispute whether this account is coherent or necessary
  for responsibility.
- **Compatibilist alternative:** moral responsibility may coexist with
  determinism, requiring a different account of divine providence and evil.
- **Evidential and moral continuation:** selective intervention, heaven,
  natural evil, amount, distribution, and proportion pressure any actual
  theodicy even if the logical defense succeeds.

## Confessional analysis

The curriculum accepts the free-will defense as a coherent answer to the
strict logical charge while refusing to make libertarianism a complete
doctrine of providence. It affirms divine goodness and rule, real creaturely
responsibility, judgment of wrongdoers, protection of neighbors, lament, and
eschatological hope. It does not infer God's reason for a particular evil or
call evil good. These are independent broadly evangelical Protestant
judgments.

## Summary

Mackie correctly observes that a world of free creatures who always choose
rightly is possible. Plantinga replies that possibility does not guarantee
feasibility for divine actualization: God cannot determine a significantly
free choice while leaving it undetermined in the relevant respect, and it is
possible that every candidate free creature would go wrong in at least one
relevant circumstance. This possibility of universal transworld depravity
provides a countermodel to the strict logical problem. It does not make sin a
necessary consequence of freedom or establish the model as actual. Objections
from compatibilism, counterfactual grounding, selective intervention, heaven,
divine freedom, natural evil, severity, and distribution remain important.
The defense answers a logical charge; Christian explanation and pastoral care
require much more.
