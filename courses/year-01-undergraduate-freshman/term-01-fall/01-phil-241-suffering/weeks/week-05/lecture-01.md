# Defense, Theodicy, and the Explanatory Burden

## Learning objectives

After completing this lecture, the student should be able to:

- distinguish logical, evidential, explanatory, existential, and pastoral
  problems concerning evil;
- state how the aim and burden of a defense differ from those of a theodicy;
- explain why a possible consistency model is not yet an actual explanation;
- compare Plantinga's targeted defense with Lewis's account of human pain;
- evaluate claims about suffering's possible uses without calling pain or
  wrongdoing good in itself; and
- formulate a restrained confessional synthesis of freedom, providence,
  responsibility, and care.

## Key terms

**Defense:** a response intended to show that a specified objection does not
establish its conclusion, without claiming to identify God's actual reason for
permitting evil. **Theodicy:** a proposed account of God's actual reasons that
aims to justify divine permission. **Logical compatibility:** the possibility
that a set of propositions is true together. **Possible world:** a complete
possible way reality could be. **Feasible world:** in this lecture's
introductory use, a possible world God can actualize given what free creatures
would do without divine causal determination of those choices. **Moral evil:**
wrong or suffering arising from culpable creaturely action. **Morally
significant freedom:** freedom with respect to actions for which praise or
blame can be appropriate. **Providence:** God's wise preservation and
governance of creation. **Instrumental good:** something good as a means to a
further end. **Intrinsic good:** something good in itself rather than merely
as a means. **Scope control:** stating no more than an argument warrants.

## Lecture

### 1. From consistency to explanation

Week 4 reached a narrow conclusion: the claims that God is omnipotent,
omniscient, and perfectly good do not directly contradict the claim that evil
exists. A possible morally sufficient reason can block the added principle that
perfect goodness must prevent every evil without exception. That result matters.
If the strict logical argument fails, it cannot function as a deductive proof
that God does not exist.

The result also creates a temptation. A defender may move from “these claims
are possibly compatible” to “this is why suffering occurs.” The first is a
modal claim about consistency. The second is an explanatory claim about the
actual world. They do not carry the same burden. Week 5 begins by separating
them before studying Plantinga's free-will defense in detail.

Consider a simple analogy. A detective alleges that two statements cannot both
be true: “Kai was inside the locked building” and “Kai did not possess a key.”
A defender answers that it is possible someone admitted Kai. That possibility
defeats the claim of contradiction. It does not show that anyone actually did
so, identify that person, or explain why Kai entered. If the question changes
from *Could both reports be true?* to *What actually happened?*, a merely
possible story is no longer enough.

**Curriculum synthesis:** A consistency defense can be philosophically
successful and explanatorily incomplete at the same time. Intellectual honesty
requires keeping both judgments visible.

### 2. “The problem of evil” names several tasks

The phrase *the problem of evil* can conceal different questions:

1. **Logical:** Are the divine attributes and evil possibly true together?
2. **Evidential:** Does the amount, kind, or distribution of evil lower the
   probability of theism?
3. **Explanatory:** If God exists, what reasons actually account for divine
   permission of evil?
4. **Moral:** Would those reasons be adequate, proportionate, and just toward
   persons who suffer?
5. **Existential:** Can a person continue to trust, hope, worship, or act after
   devastating loss?
6. **Pastoral:** What truthful and fitting response should be offered to a
   sufferer now?

One response may be relevant to several tasks, but no response should be
assumed to solve all six. A possible-world model may answer a logical question
while leaving the evidential question untouched. A full theodicy may propose
an explanation while still being pastorally premature. Compassionate presence
may be exactly right in grief without functioning as a premise in a deductive
argument.

Plantinga's assigned chapter is disciplined about its principal target. It
asks whether the theist is committed to contradiction and develops a defense
against that charge. Lewis announces an intellectual aim, but chapters 6–7
make claims about what human pain may actually do under God's providence. Lewis
therefore assumes a stronger explanatory burden at important points.[^1][^2]

### 3. What a defense must do

Plantinga distinguishes defending theism from offering a theodicy. A defender
need not know God's actual reasons. Against a logical inconsistency claim, the
defender seeks a proposition or set of propositions that meets three tests:

- it is logically possible;
- it is consistent with the challenged theistic claims; and
- together with those claims, it shows how evil could exist.

The added proposition need not be established as true in the actual world.
That limited burden follows from the critic's universal claim. If the critic
says there is *no possible way* for God and evil to coexist, one genuinely
possible countermodel is enough to answer that statement.

The word *genuinely* carries important weight. A defender cannot write “God has
some reason” and declare victory. The proposal must describe a coherent
relation between divine goodness, power, creaturely action, and permitted
evil. It must not combine conditions that contradict each other, equivocate on
freedom, or secretly abandon divine goodness.

Suppose a defender proposes:

1. God creates persons who can perform morally significant actions.
2. Such action is not causally determined by God in the respect in which it is
   free.
3. Some persons freely choose wrongly.
4. The value of morally significant agency can be a morally sufficient reason
   to permit the risk of wrong choice.

If that set is possible, it supplies the required kind of countermodel for
moral evil. It does not yet show that libertarian freedom exists, that this is
God's actual reason, or that every actual permission is justified.

### 4. What a theodicy must do

A theodicy makes a more ambitious proposal: it attempts to explain why God in
fact permits evil. Its burden therefore includes more than consistency.

First, the explanation needs a **truth connection**. There must be reason to
believe that its account applies to the actual world. Second, it needs a
**coverage claim**. The proposal must specify which evils it addresses rather
than moving without warning from some moral evil to every form of suffering.
Third, it needs **moral adequacy**. An alleged good cannot justify any amount of
harm merely because the good is valuable. Necessity, proportion, the rights and
good of the sufferer, and the possibility of less harmful alternatives matter.
Fourth, it needs **theological coherence**. It must fit the account of God,
creation, sin, providence, judgment, redemption, and hope that the theodicy
claims to represent.

A theodicy also inherits an epistemic problem: how could finite interpreters
know that this is God's reason for a particular event? A general doctrine of
providence does not authorize detailed case-specific diagnosis. Job's friends
speak confidently about the meaning of Job's suffering and repeatedly
misdescribe his moral situation. The book does not deny providence; it denies
their easy access to its hidden particulars.

The contrast can be summarized without ranking every item as stronger or
weaker:

| Question | Defense | Theodicy |
|---|---|---|
| Primary aim | Defeat a specified objection | Explain and justify actual divine permission |
| Modal burden | Show a coherent possibility | Show more than possibility |
| Truth claim | Need not identify the actual reason | Proposes an actual explanatory account |
| Evidence | Enough to establish possibility | Evidence or warrant for actual application |
| Moral burden | Coherent possible justification | Necessity, proportion, justice, and person-centered adequacy |
| Result if successful | The objection does not prove its conclusion | The proposed explanation has warranted reach |

### 5. A defense can be modest without being evasive

Critics sometimes hear “defense rather than theodicy” as refusal to confront
suffering. That criticism is justified if the defender uses modal language to
avoid every question. It is not justified merely because the defender answers
the argument actually presented.

If a critic alleges a contradiction, identifying a possible counterexample is
relevant. The defender should then acknowledge what remains. The limited form
of the answer is not a defect when it is honestly labeled. It becomes evasive
when possibility is used rhetorically to imply probability, actuality, or
pastoral adequacy.

Similarly, a critic should not preserve a claim of *logical impossibility* by
shifting to “the defense is not a satisfying explanation.” Lack of explanation
does not restore a contradiction. The critic may have moved to a serious
evidential or moral objection, but the new objection should be named.

Good argument therefore requires reciprocal scope control:

- the defender must not advertise a consistency result as a complete theodicy;
- the critic must not treat the failure of a theodicy as proof that no
  consistency defense can work; and
- both must distinguish intellectual assessment from immediate care.

### 6. Moral evil and the value of agency

The free-will defense primarily addresses **moral evil**: wrong and suffering
that arise from culpable creaturely choices. Murder, betrayal, deception, and
oppression are not merely unpleasant states. They involve agents who act toward
other persons and whose choices may be assessed as wrong.

Freedom matters to this domain because responsibility seems connected to
agency. Plantinga's model uses a libertarian account: with respect to a
significant action, the person can act or refrain, and the choice is not
causally determined by factors beyond the person. The action is morally
significant when praise or blame can properly attach to it.[^1]

The model does not say any undetermined movement is valuable. Random behavior
is not yet responsible choice. Nor does it say every available option must be
equally attractive. The relevant good is personal agency capable of love,
obedience, generosity, betrayal, and refusal in a morally assessable relation.

This helps answer one misconception. To say God permits a person to exercise
agency is not to say God approves the person's wrong. Permission, causation,
approval, and culpability are distinct. A government may permit citizens to
make choices within law while condemning and punishing assault. The analogy is
limited—God's knowledge and power exceed a government's—but it shows that
permission need not transfer the wrongdoer's intention to the permitting
authority.

The analogy also exposes a question. A good authority does not merely value the
offender's choice; it protects potential victims, restrains wrongdoing, and
pursues justice. A free-will account must therefore address why and when God
permits, limits, judges, and redeems wrong action. Freedom alone cannot serve
as a slogan that silences these demands.

### 7. Lewis's more ambitious account of human pain

Lewis's chapters on human pain are not a technical possible-world defense. He
offers a Christian account of what pain may do in fallen human life. He first
distinguishes bodily sensation from suffering as an experience the person
dislikes or resists. He then argues that suffering can expose the instability
of creaturely self-sufficiency, interrupt the attempt to treat finite goods as
ultimate, and become an occasion of surrender to God.[^2]

These are claims about formation and providence, not merely compatibility. If
Lewis is saying that God actually uses suffering to loosen idolatrous
self-reliance, the reader must ask whether the account fits lived evidence.
Some sufferers report deepened dependence, courage, or compassion. Others are
traumatized, embittered, disabled, or killed. Many outcomes are mixed. An
account of possible growth does not show that growth occurs in every case or
that the suffering was needed for it.

Lewis's proposal is strongest when read as a claim about how grace may work
through an evil without making the evil good. It is weakest when converted into
a universal formula: “This person suffered so that this lesson would be
learned.” The universal claim exceeds both the evidence and the sufferer's
knowledge of providence.

**Primary-source claim:** Lewis does not treat pain as intrinsically good. In
chapter 7 he distinguishes the evil of suffering from goods that may occur in
the sufferer's faithful response or in another person's compassion and mercy.
He also holds that suffering should ordinarily be relieved rather than
pursued.[^3] Those controls are essential, not optional corrections added by
modern pastoral sensitivity.

### 8. Simple good, simple evil, and complex good

Lewis develops a useful three-part distinction. In paraphrase:

- **Simple good** is the good will and action that conforms to God's will.
- **Simple evil** is the creature's wrongful intention and action.
- **Complex good** is the good God brings about while ordering a history that
  includes creaturely evil.

The distinction aims to affirm providence without confusing intentions. A
wrongdoer may intend harm; God may govern the event toward judgment, rescue,
repentance, solidarity, or redemption. The good outcome does not retroactively
make the wrong intention righteous.[^3]

This structure resonates with biblical narratives in which human agents intend
evil while God purposes good, most explicitly Joseph's retrospective judgment
in Genesis 50:20. It also resonates with the crucifixion: human betrayal,
injustice, and violence remain culpable even as the New Testament places the
cross within God's saving purpose. Divine providence and human responsibility
are affirmed together rather than treated as competitors.[^4]

Yet *complex good* is not a mathematical license. One cannot simply total
someone's agony against another person's virtue. If the proposal treats a
sufferer only as material for someone else's character, it violates the
personal and moral character it attributes to divine goodness. Christian hope
must include God's just relation to the sufferer—presence, vindication,
resurrection, and restoration—not merely benefits distributed elsewhere.

### 9. Strong objections to Lewis's account

Lewis's account deserves a charitable but demanding evaluation.

**First, the formation objection:** Pain does not reliably improve character.
It can disclose need, but it can also narrow attention, break trust, or destroy
the capacities a proposed lesson would require. A theodicy must explain why a
perfect God would use so variable and dangerous an instrument.

**Second, the proportionality objection:** Even if limited discomfort can
produce attention or discipline, horrendous suffering may appear radically
disproportionate. The movement from “some pain can serve formation” to “these
extreme evils are justified by formation” is invalid without further premises.

**Third, the agency objection:** If one person's cruelty becomes the occasion
of another's growth, that outcome does not justify the cruelty. A providential
account must preserve blame, protection, resistance, and justice. Otherwise it
can become an ideology for tolerating abuse.

**Fourth, the epistemic objection:** General doctrines do not reveal the reason
for a particular event. The claim that God can bring good from evil is not the
claim that the interpreter knows what good God intends in this case.

**Fifth, the distribution objection:** Suffering falls unequally. Some endure
far more than others, and the persons least responsible may bear devastating
costs. A theodicy needs resources beyond a generic appeal to surrender.

Lewis anticipates parts of these concerns by refusing to call suffering good
in itself and by requiring compassion and relief. He does not thereby settle
every objection. His proposal should be evaluated as a partial Christian
account of possible providential use, not as a complete explanation of all
evil.

### 10. Freedom within providence

Plantinga's defense and Lewis's account can appear to pull in different
directions. The first emphasizes that significantly free creaturely action is
not causally determined by God. The second emphasizes that God can order a
history containing creaturely evil toward good. The tension is real, but the
claims are not directly contradictory.

One may distinguish levels of agency. A creature intends and performs a wrong
for which that creature is responsible. God permits the action without sharing
the wrongful intention, limits or judges it, and can bring goods from the
resulting history. Providence need not mean that every creaturely intention is
divinely caused under that same description.

This synthesis raises difficult questions about foreknowledge, concurrence,
permission, and control. Protestant traditions answer them differently.
Reformed accounts often emphasize meticulous providence and compatibilist
freedom; Arminian and many free-will accounts emphasize libertarian creaturely
choice; Molinist proposals appeal to divine knowledge of what creatures would
freely do in specified circumstances. Week 5 cannot adjudicate those entire
systems.

**Contested interpretation:** Plantinga's logical defense relies on significant
freedom in a libertarian sense. A compatibilist may affirm responsibility while
rejecting that definition, then construct a different account of evil and
providence. The free-will defense's success should not be mistaken for a proof
of one complete Protestant doctrine of providence.

### 11. Confessional and pastoral boundaries

The course's broadly evangelical Protestant framework affirms that God is good,
wise, powerful, and provident; creation is good yet fallen; creatures are
responsible; evil is opposed and judged; and God's saving purpose culminates in
resurrection and new creation. Scripture does not portray evil as a necessary
part of God's character or as something the church should preserve for its
educational value.

Genesis 1–3 connects moral rebellion with creaturely agency without denying
God's sovereignty. James 1 refuses to attribute moral temptation to God.
Genesis 50 and the cross show that divine good purpose can govern events whose
human intentions remain evil. Romans 8 presents suffering creation as groaning
toward liberation. Revelation 21–22 portrays evil and death as defeated, not
eternally retained because adversity is useful.[^4]

**Confessional conclusion:** Morally significant freedom is a plausible good
that helps explain how moral evil can coexist with God's perfect goodness. A
doctrine of providence can also affirm that God brings good from creaturely
evil without approving or causing the creature's wicked intention. These
claims support a coherent Christian synthesis, but they do not identify God's
complete or case-specific reasons.

**Curriculum synthesis:** In pastoral use, the first duty is not to place the
sufferer inside a theodicy. Listen, protect, lament, provide practical help,
report or interrupt abuse where required, and seek qualified care. A wrongdoer
cannot appeal to “free will” to escape accountability, and a bystander cannot
appeal to providence to excuse inaction. Philosophical explanation becomes
appropriate when the sufferer wants it and the setting can sustain it.

## Primary-source engagement

The lecture follows Plantinga's assigned distinction between defense and
theodicy and his use of morally significant freedom as a possible consistency
model. It reads Lewis's chapters 6–7 as a more ambitious account of the
providential use of human pain, including his claims about surrender,
compassion, relief of suffering, and the distinction among simple good, simple
evil, and complex good. The comparison does not attribute Lewis's explanatory
claims to Plantinga or turn either source into a complete course synthesis.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Strict logical critic:** the divine attributes and evil are inconsistent;
  no possible morally sufficient reason can reconcile them.
- **Plantinga's defense:** a possible circumstance involving significant
  creaturely freedom can rebut the strict inconsistency charge without
  identifying God's actual reason.
- **Free-will theodicy:** creaturely freedom is not only a possible consistency
  condition but an actual major reason God permits moral evil. This claim
  assumes a stronger evidential and moral burden than the defense.
- **Lewis's providential-formation account:** suffering may expose false
  self-sufficiency, occasion surrender, and elicit compassion, while remaining
  evil in itself and properly subject to relief.
- **Critical objections:** freedom or formation may not justify the severity,
  distribution, or nonmoral forms of suffering; general accounts may fail to
  apply to particular cases and may be morally misused.

## Confessional analysis

The curriculum affirms responsible creaturely agency and comprehensive divine
providence while distinguishing permission, causation, intention, and approval.
It receives the defense/theodicy distinction as essential scope control and
treats Lewis's account as a partial proposal requiring biblical, evidential,
moral, and pastoral qualification. These are independent broadly evangelical
Protestant judgments, not official Wheaton College teaching.

## Summary

A defense and a theodicy answer different questions. A defense can rebut a
strict logical objection by presenting a coherent possible circumstance in
which God and moral evil coexist. A theodicy proposes God's actual reason and
therefore assumes burdens of truth, coverage, moral adequacy, theological fit,
and warranted application. Plantinga's free-will defense is deliberately
targeted. Lewis's account of human pain is more explanatory: suffering may
expose self-sufficiency, occasion surrender, and elicit compassion, yet Lewis
denies that suffering is good in itself or should ordinarily be preserved.
Christian synthesis may affirm freedom and providence together, but neither
category excuses wrongdoing, blocks protection and justice, or reveals why a
particular person suffered.
