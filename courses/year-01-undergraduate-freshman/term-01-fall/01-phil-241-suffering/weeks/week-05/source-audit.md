## Plantinga argument audit

- The lectures identify the target as Mackie's strict consistency claim and
  his sharper contention that God could make persons who always freely choose
  rightly.
- Plantinga is not represented as denying that an always-right free world is
  logically possible. The dispute concerns whether every possible world is
  feasible for God to actualize while creaturely choices remain significantly
  free.
- Significant freedom is linked to morally assessable action and exclusion of
  causal determination in the relevant respect. Randomness is not treated as
  sufficient for responsibility.
- The introductory distinction between strong and weak actualization is
  signaled as explanatory terminology rather than a quotation. God supplies
  the circumstances in the model; the undetermined creaturely action helps
  determine which complete world is actual.
- Transworld depravity is presented as a technical possible condition involving
  creaturely essences and counterfactual free action. The lectures explicitly
  deny four common misstatements: free creatures who never sin are not
  contradictory; not every possible free world must contain evil; the
  condition need not be actual; and it is not claimed to be necessary.
- The reconstructed argument makes the possibility of relevant universal
  transworld depravity, not its actuality or probability, do the work required
  against a universal impossibility thesis.
- The conclusion is limited to compatibility of the classical divine
  attributes with moral evil. It is not represented as a proof of theism, a
  full theory of providence, or an actual explanation of every evil.

## Objection and position audit

- Mackie's always-right-world objection is stated as a serious improvement
  objection rather than caricatured as a demand for programmed automata.
- Compatibilism is presented as a live alternative concerning freedom and
  responsibility, including its importance for some Reformed accounts. The
  lecture does not claim that Plantinga refutes it.
- Counterfactual grounding, positive warrant for possibility, value and
  proportion, selective intervention, heaven, divine freedom, natural evil,
  amount, and distribution are distinguished by their targets.
- Selective prevention is not treated as logically equivalent to causal
  determination of an agent's intention. Its force is located principally
  against a comprehensive theodicy or the evidential adequacy of the model.
- Plantinga's possible nonhuman-agent extension concerning natural evil is
  identified as a modal proposal only. It is not endorsed as actual, probable,
  or pastorally usable.
- The survey claim that Plantinga is widely judged to answer the classical
  logical problem is attributed to Beebe and expressly separated from any
  claim that the broader problem of evil is solved.

## Lewis comparison audit

- Lewis's chapters 6–7 are assigned by verified chapter title without an
  unverified HarperOne page span.
- The lectures distinguish Lewis's claims about the possible providential use
  of suffering from Plantinga's narrower consistency model.
- Lewis is not made to say that pain is intrinsically good. His own controls
  concerning relief of suffering, compassion, and distinct moral intentions
  are retained.
- The categories *simple good*, *simple evil*, and *complex good* are
  paraphrased and used to distinguish a creature's wrongful purpose from God's
  providentially good purpose.
- The lectures test Lewis's proposal with objections concerning unreliable
  formation, proportionality, victim-centered justice, epistemic access, and
  unequal distribution. They do not portray those objections as proof of bad
  faith.

## Scripture, confessional, pastoral, and copyright audit

- Genesis 1–3; 50:15–21; Job; the lament psalms; James 1; Acts 2; Romans 8;
  and Revelation 21–22 are used within the previously approved canonical
  framework. They are not represented as premises Mackie grants or as a
  verbatim source of Plantinga's modal analysis.
- Confessional conclusions follow the philosophical reconstruction and are
  labeled as independent curriculum judgments, not official Wheaton College
  teaching.
- The lectures distinguish divine permission, creaturely causation, wrongful
  intention, providential ordering, and moral approval. They do not use
  providence to dissolve creaturely culpability.
- No personal disclosure or real sufferer is required. Free will is not used to
  excuse abuse, block protection or reporting, blame a victim, or infer God's
  reason for a particular tragedy.
- Both lectures are original synthesis. They reproduce no extended passage
  from Plantinga, Peterson, Lewis, Beebe, or a copyrighted Bible edition.
