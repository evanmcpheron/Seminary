# Formal Consistency and the Logical Problem of Evil

## Learning objectives

After completing this lecture, the student should be able to:

- distinguish direct contradiction from indirect inconsistency;
- separate consistency, truth, validity, soundness, possibility, and
  probability;
- reconstruct Mackie's logical challenge with its bridge principles visible;
- test the scope of claims about goodness, omnipotence, and preventable evil;
- explain the limited burden carried by a consistency defense; and
- keep a formal result distinct from evidential, existential, and pastoral
  resolution.

## Key terms

**Proposition:** a claim capable of being true or false. **Conjunction:** a
combined claim asserting two or more propositions together. **Contradiction:**
a claim of the form `p and not-p`, or a set from which such a claim follows.
**Consistency:** the possibility that all members of a set are true together.
**Entailment:** a relation in which the truth of some propositions guarantees
the truth of another. **Validity:** an argument's form makes it impossible for
its premises to be true and conclusion false. **Soundness:** validity plus true
premises. **Bridge principle:** an added premise connecting terms that do not
by themselves yield a conclusion. **Logical possibility:** freedom from
contradiction. **Morally sufficient reason:** a reason that, if present, would
make permission of an evil consistent with perfect goodness. **Defense:** an
attempt to defeat an objection without claiming to identify God's actual
reason. **Theodicy:** a proposed account of God's actual justifying reasons.

## Lecture

### 1. The Week 3 handoff: one problem becomes several

Week 3 ended with a distinction that now governs the course. Showing that God
and evil are *compatible* is not the same as showing that evil supplies
positive evidence for God, that belief in God remains probable after all the
evidence is considered, or that a suffering person has received a satisfying
answer. The logical problem addresses the first and narrowest question:

> Is it possible for the central claims of classical theism and the claim that
> evil exists to be true together?

The word *narrow* does not mean unimportant. Contradictory beliefs cannot all be
true. If classical theism entails both that evil exists and that no evil exists,
then no appeal to comfort, mystery, or religious experience repairs that formal
defect. The contradiction must be removed. Conversely, if a contradiction has
not been shown, the critic cannot honestly announce a deductive disproof.

The formal question is therefore severe but limited. It asks whether a set of
claims can be true together. It does not yet ask which explanation is most
likely, whether all actual suffering is justified, or what should be said at a
hospital bedside. Much confusion arises when a result in one domain is
advertised as a result in every domain.

**Curriculum synthesis:** Week 4 will grant Mackie's argument its intended
strength by treating it as an internal consistency challenge. It will also
restrict any theistic answer to what that answer actually establishes.

### 2. Direct contradiction is not the same as tension

A direct contradiction has a recognizable form:

1. `p`.
2. `not-p`.

For example, “Every permitted event is prevented” and “Some permitted event is
not prevented” cannot both be true. No further information is needed. By
contrast, the following pair is not directly contradictory:

1. Mara is a competent physician.
2. A patient under Mara's care experiences pain.

The claims may prompt questions, but a competent physician may permit limited
pain for treatment, may encounter pain she cannot safely eliminate, or may care
for an injury she did not cause. To derive a contradiction, one needs added
premises about what competence includes, what Mara knows and can do, what
caused the pain, and whether any sufficient reason could justify permission.

The same logical care is required in the problem of evil. Mackie begins with a
set centered on three claims:

1. God is omnipotent.
2. God is perfectly good.
3. Evil exists.

Omniscience belongs to the classical conception under discussion and is often
made explicit in fuller reconstructions. Yet none of these sentences is
literally the negation of another. “God is omnipotent” is not syntactically
“evil does not exist.” Mackie recognizes this and adds principles connecting
goodness and power to the elimination of evil.[^1]

Beebe makes the same point by distinguishing a set that contains a direct
contradiction from one whose contradiction must be deduced. The logical critic
therefore bears a derivational burden: the added claims must be clear, true or
otherwise defensible, and strong enough to yield the conclusion.[^2]

### 3. Consistency, truth, validity, and soundness

Four terms should not be exchanged casually.

- A **consistent set** could be true together. Its members need not actually be
  true.
- A **true proposition** describes reality correctly. Truth is a property of a
  claim, not an argument form.
- A **valid argument** cannot have true premises and a false conclusion. It may
  still contain false premises.
- A **sound argument** is valid and has true premises.

Suppose a defender describes a possible circumstance in which God has a
morally sufficient reason to permit some evil. If the description is coherent,
it can show that “God exists” and “evil exists” are consistent. It does not
show that the circumstance is actual. It does not prove theism, and it does not
show that the proposed reason probably explains the amount and distribution of
evil.

The distinction between possibility and probability is equally important. A
possibility claim says that a state of affairs is not contradictory. A
probability claim compares how credible or expected it is. A highly improbable
story can still be logically possible. That may be enough to answer a claim of
*impossibility*, but it will not answer an argument that suffering is
*unexpected* if God exists.

This asymmetry follows from the competing theses:

- The logical critic says there is **no possible** world in which God and evil
  coexist.
- The consistency defender needs **one possible** world in which they coexist.

The defender does not win by merely attaching the word *possible* to a
contradictory story. The description must actually be coherent. But the critic
does not win by showing that the story is strange, incomplete, or unsupported
by evidence. Those may become powerful objections to a theodicy or to the
probability of theism, but they do not by themselves reestablish impossibility.

### 4. Mackie's bridge principles

Mackie's initial claims require bridges. In paraphrase, his two central
connections are these:

1. A wholly good being opposes evil and eliminates evil so far as it can.
2. An omnipotent being has no relevant limit on what it can do.

Omniscience is commonly added so that no actual evil escapes divine knowledge.
The result is intuitively forceful. If God knows every evil, can prevent every
evil, and perfect goodness always prevents every preventable evil, then no evil
should occur. Evil does occur. Therefore the classical claims appear unable to
stand together.[^3]

The logical work is carried by the bridge principles, especially the word
*every*. Consider a more explicit reconstruction:

1. If God exists, God is omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good.
2. An omniscient being knows every evil and every available way to prevent it.
3. An omnipotent being can prevent every evil unless prevention is logically
   impossible.
4. A perfectly good being prevents every evil it knows and can prevent.
5. Evil exists.
6. Therefore, if God exists, God knows every evil, can prevent every evil, and
   prevents every evil.
7. Therefore, if God exists, no evil exists.
8. Evil exists.
9. Therefore God does not exist.

The argument is valid if the premises are read with sufficient precision. The
central question becomes whether premises 3 and 4 are true under the intended
scope. A defender need not deny that God is powerful or good. The defender may
deny that perfect goodness entails preventing *every* preventable evil without
exception. A good agent can sometimes permit a harm when preventing it would
exclude a sufficiently weighty good, violate an obligation, or require an equal
or worse evil.

### 5. The scope test for perfect goodness

The bridge from goodness to prevention can be interpreted in two ways.

**Unqualified prevention principle:** A perfectly good being prevents every
evil it can prevent.

**Morally qualified prevention principle:** A perfectly good being prevents
every evil it can prevent unless permitting that evil is morally justified.

The first is strong enough to produce the contradiction quickly. It also seems
too strong. A parent may permit the pain of setting a broken bone, and a judge
may permit the distress of truthful testimony, because prevention would block
a serious good or duty. Human analogies do not settle the divine case, but they
show that goodness does not conceptually equal maximal short-term pain
prevention.

The second principle is more plausible, but it changes the argument. It yields:

1. If God exists, every permitted evil has a morally sufficient reason.
2. At least one actual evil lacks any morally sufficient reason.
3. Therefore God does not exist.

Premise 2 is no longer a bare logical truth. It is a substantive claim about
actual evil and available reasons. Establishing it requires attention to the
kinds, amounts, distribution, and apparent pointlessness of suffering. That is
the terrain of the evidential problem studied later.

This does not trivialize the critic's moral protest. It locates the burden
correctly. The critic may have strong evidence that certain suffering is
gratuitous. The defender may have difficulty explaining it. But an evidential
burden should be named as evidential rather than converted into a hidden axiom
of logic.

### 6. The scope test for omnipotence

Mackie's second bridge also requires definition. Does omnipotence include the
power to make contradictions true? If so, “power” no longer behaves coherently:
an omnipotent agent would both perform and fail to perform the same action in
the same respect.

Classical theists ordinarily understand omnipotence as power to do whatever is
logically possible and consistent with the divine nature. On that account, an
inability to make a square circle is not weakness because “square circle” does
not describe an object or task. Likewise, God cannot lie or act unjustly, not
because an external power defeats God, but because such action conflicts with
perfect truth and goodness.[^4]

This clarification cannot become a blank check. A defender must show that the
proposed prevention really is logically impossible or morally inseparable from
a relevant good. It is not enough to say, after any tragedy, “God could not
have prevented it.” The logical limitation needs an argument, and finite
ignorance does not establish a specific hidden necessity.

The important question is therefore relational: can God secure the relevant
good while preventing the evil? A free-will defense, introduced more fully in
Lecture 2 and developed in Week 5, argues that causally determining a person to
perform a morally significant action is incompatible with that action's being
free in the required sense. That is a claim about the joint possibility of
conditions, not a denial of divine power.

### 7. Knowledge closes one route but not every route

Making omniscience explicit blocks the suggestion that God permits an evil
because God simply overlooked it. Classical theism cannot preserve perfect
goodness by treating God as well-intentioned but uninformed. If an actual evil
occurs, the classical theist holds that God knows it fully.

Yet knowledge alone does not determine what goodness requires. Knowing that a
pain will occur, having power over the situation, and permitting the pain do
not form a contradiction unless another premise says permission lacks adequate
reason. The moral premise remains indispensable.

This matters when arguments use emotionally powerful cases. Severe suffering
rightly intensifies moral attention. It may provide stronger evidence against
a proposed justification. But severity is not itself a formal rule converting
“God knows and permits E” into both `p` and `not-p`. The argument must state why
no sufficient reason could justify permission of that evil.

**Contested evaluation:** Some critics argue that the moral truth is obvious:
no perfect being could permit at least certain horrendous evils. Some defenders
reply that we are poorly placed to rule out all possible reasons. The dispute
is serious, but once it concerns our warrant for judging actual reasons, it has
moved beyond a purely formal contradiction.

### 8. Consistency models and morally sufficient reasons

Beebe introduces the notion of a morally sufficient reason: a reason that
would make permission of evil compatible with perfect goodness. A consistency
model can be expressed this way:

1. God is omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good.
2. Evil exists.
3. It is possible that God has a morally sufficient reason for permitting
   evil.

If the three claims can be true together, the universal inconsistency thesis is
false. The model does not need to reveal the actual reason. It must only
describe a genuinely possible relation between permission and some outweighing
good, duty, or avoidance of worse evil.[^2]

A simple human analogy can clarify the structure but cannot establish the
theology. A clinician may permit the pain of an injection to protect a patient
from a dangerous disease. The pain is not called good; the permission is
justified by a good that cannot be obtained in that circumstance without it.
The analogy shows that “goodness always prevents every known pain” is not
conceptually necessary. It does not show that the vast sufferings of the world
are comparable to a medical procedure or that God has the same limitations as
a clinician.

Candidate consistency models include morally significant creaturely freedom,
stable natural order, moral formation, judgment, redemption, or goods of
relationship. Each carries further questions. Are the alleged goods really
incompatible with less evil? Do they respect sufferers as persons rather than
as means? Can they address natural as well as moral evil? Are they possible,
actual, or probable? The narrow logical task requires possibility; intellectual
responsibility continues asking the other questions.

### 9. A counterexample defeats a universal claim

The form of the logical debate explains why one coherent model matters. The
claim “No possible world contains both God and evil” is universal. A single
counterexample—a possible world containing both—defeats it.

This is parallel to ordinary logic. The claim “All birds are flightless” is
defeated by one flying bird. The counterexample need not prove how many birds
fly or explain why some do not. In the same way, a possible morally sufficient
reason defeats a universal impossibility claim without explaining every evil.

The analogy also identifies the defender's temptation. After producing one
counterexample, the defender may speak as if the entire problem of evil has
been solved. That is false. The model shows at most that the initial set is not
inconsistent. It does not show:

- that theism is more probable than its rivals;
- that every actual evil is non-gratuitous;
- that the candidate reason is God's actual reason;
- that the distribution of suffering is morally intelligible;
- that natural evil follows from creaturely freedom; or
- that a grieving person has received attention, safety, justice, or hope.

Logical success can therefore be real and modest at once.

### 10. Confessional commitments are not exemptions from logic

This curriculum confesses one omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good God.
It also affirms that evil and suffering are real. Those commitments make the
logical challenge an internal test rather than an outsider's irrelevant game.
Faithfulness does not permit hiding an invalid inference, changing a definition
midargument, or dismissing Mackie because his conclusion conflicts with the
course's confession.

The biblical material from Week 2 provides a more complex pattern than the
unqualified prevention principle. Scripture presents God as good and powerful,
permits creaturely rebellion to have real consequences, hears lament, governs
without being charged with evil, judges wrongdoing, and promises final renewal.
Job denies easy case-specific diagnosis. Romans 8 describes creation as
groaning under suffering while locating hope in God's action. Revelation
21–22 portrays evil as overcome rather than eternally normalized.[^5]

**Confessional conclusion:** The course judges that God's attributes and the
existence of evil are logically compatible because perfect goodness need not
prevent every evil without exception and because a morally sufficient reason
for permission is possible. That conclusion is a confessional and
philosophical judgment, not an official teaching of Wheaton College.

**Curriculum synthesis:** The judgment must not be inflated. Scripture does
not provide the student with God's hidden reason for each event. Logical
compatibility does not authorize passivity toward preventable harm, silence
lament, or reduce the sufferer to a premise. The proper formal conclusion is:
the strict contradiction has not been established. Later evidential and
theodical work remains.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Mackie's logical challenge:** divine omnipotence and perfect goodness,
  joined to the existence of evil and suitable bridge principles, form an
  inconsistent set. The bridge principles do substantial argumentative work.
- **Attribute revision:** one may remove the inconsistency by revising divine
  power, goodness, knowledge, or the reality of evil. Whether that remains
  classical theism is a further theological question.
- **Consistency defense:** a possible morally sufficient reason is sufficient
  to rebut a universal claim of logical impossibility. It need not assert God's
  actual reason.
- **Evidential continuation:** a critic may grant logical compatibility while
  arguing that the amount, kinds, or distribution of evil count strongly
  against theism. Logical defeat does not decide that argument.

## Confessional analysis

The curriculum retains the classical divine attributes and the reality of
evil. It rejects an unqualified premise that perfect goodness must prevent
every known evil immediately. It accepts logical scrutiny, refuses to use
mystery as a substitute for valid argument, and limits the conclusion to
compatibility. These are independent broadly evangelical Protestant judgments,
not official Wheaton College material.

## Summary

The logical problem of evil alleges that classical theism and the existence of
evil cannot possibly be true together. Mackie's initial claims do not contain
a direct contradiction; bridge principles connecting goodness, knowledge,
power, and prevention are required. An unqualified claim that perfect goodness
prevents every preventable evil yields a valid argument but is vulnerable to
morally sufficient reasons. A qualified principle moves the dispute toward the
actual or probable absence of such reasons. Because a strict impossibility
claim is universal, one coherent consistency model can defeat it. That limited
result neither proves theism nor resolves evidential, natural-evil,
existential, and pastoral questions.
