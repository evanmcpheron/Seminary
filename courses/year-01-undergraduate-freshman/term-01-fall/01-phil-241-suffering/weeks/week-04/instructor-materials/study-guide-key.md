# Instructor Key — Week 4 Study Guide

This key supports ungraded self-correction. Accept substantively equivalent
answers grounded in the assigned sources. Do not distribute it before the
student completes the guide.

## 1. Source-control anchors

- Mackie's opening and added bridge principles: Peterson, chapter 7, especially
  the opening statement of the problem.
- Adequate and fallacious solutions: Mackie's two labeled divisions and the
  particular proposed solutions within them.
- Consistency and morally sufficient reason: Beebe, sections 2–3.
- Free-will-defense scope and objections: Beebe, sections 7–10.
- A source report states what the assigned author or entry supports. A lecture
  reconstruction makes implicit steps explicit. A student evaluation judges
  the reasoning and must not be attributed to the source.

## 2. Formal reconstruction

A sound answer should say that none of the four starting claims has the form
`p` alongside `not-p`. A full derivation needs connections such as:

1. an omniscient being knows every evil and every way to prevent it;
2. an omnipotent being can prevent every evil unless prevention is logically
   impossible; and
3. a perfectly good being prevents every evil it knows and can prevent.

The central pressure falls on the unqualified third bridge. When it is changed
to include “unless permission is morally justified,” the critic must add that
at least one actual evil lacks any morally sufficient reason. That premise is
substantive and normally shifts the dispute toward evidential argument.

## 3. Countermodel standard

The model need not be actual or probable, but it must be coherent. A reasonable
example will connect morally significant freedom to the logical impossibility
of causally determining the same choice while leaving it undetermined in the
relevant respect. It should conclude only that coexistence is possible. It
should not conclude that God exists, that freedom explains natural evil, or
that the model states God's reason for an actual tragedy.

Flag a model that merely says “God has a reason” without describing a possible
relation, or one that treats a person's devastation as justified solely by an
unrelated person's benefit.

## 4. Objection classification

1. Natural evil: scope and actuality; it pressures explanatory coverage, not
   necessarily the possibility of coexistence involving moral evil.
2. Heaven: theological coherence and definition of freedom.
3. Possible versus actual reason: actuality.
4. Disproportion: moral adequacy and evidential probability.
5. Divine freedom: definition and theological coherence.
6. Bereavement response: pastoral use.
7. Determined and undetermined in the same respect: logical possibility.

Accept additional labels when the student explains the connection. Require the
student to distinguish defeating the model from pressuring a fuller theodicy.

## 5. Retrieval definitions

- **Direct contradiction:** `p` and `not-p` in the same respect.
- **Indirect inconsistency:** a contradiction can be derived after implications
  or connecting premises are made explicit.
- **Bridge principle:** a premise connecting initially separate claims or
  terms.
- **Validity:** impossible for all premises to be true and the conclusion
  false.
- **Soundness:** validity with true premises.
- **Morally sufficient reason:** a reason that would justify permission of an
  evil consistently with perfect goodness.
- **Defense:** defeats an objection without claiming to identify God's actual
  reason.
- **Theodicy:** proposes an account of God's actual justifying reasons.

The concluding sentence should distinguish the possibility question from
actuality, probability, full explanation, and pastoral sufficiency.
