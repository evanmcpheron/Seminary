# Week 4 Study Guide — Consistency, Countermodels, and Scope

Complete this ungraded guide after the readings and lectures. Use the assigned
sources during every stage except the timed retrieval check in section 5. Keep
the completed guide for A-03 revision and cumulative review; do not submit it
for a grade.

## 2. Formal reconstruction lab — 25 minutes

Begin with these four claims:

- God is omnipotent.
- God is omniscient.
- God is perfectly good.
- Evil exists.

1. Explain why no two lines are direct negations.
2. Add the minimum bridge premises needed to derive “If God exists, no evil
   exists.”
3. Number the complete argument and identify the conclusion.
4. Mark every use of **all**, **every**, **none**, **can**, or **must**.
5. Test validity by asking whether all premises could be true while the
   conclusion is false.
6. Test soundness separately by selecting the most contestable bridge premise.

Now replace an unqualified prevention premise with this qualified form:

> A perfectly good being prevents every evil it can prevent unless permitting
> that evil is morally justified.

State the new premise required for an atheistic conclusion. Explain whether the
argument has remained purely logical or has moved toward an evidential dispute.

## 3. Countermodel and burden test — 20 minutes

Construct a deliberately minimal consistency model using these components:

1. classical divine attributes;
2. real evil;
3. one candidate morally sufficient reason stated as *possible*;
4. a relation showing why prevention would exclude a relevant good or require
   an equal or worse evil; and
5. no claim that the model is God's actual reason.

Then audit it with five questions:

- Is any line internally contradictory?
- Does the proposed good merely outweigh the evil, or does the model explain
  why permission is necessary?
- Does the model concern moral evil, natural evil, or both?
- Does it treat a sufferer only as a means to another person's good?
- Which later evidential question is untouched?

Finish with two sentences: one giving the strongest result the model supports,
and one naming a conclusion it does not support.

## 4. Objection classification — 15 minutes

For each objection, identify its target as **logical possibility**,
**definition**, **actuality**, **probability**, **moral adequacy**,
**theological coherence**, or **pastoral use**. More than one label is allowed
only with an explanation.

1. A free-will model directly addresses moral evil but not earthquakes or
   disease.
2. The redeemed are expected not to sin in the new creation.
3. A possible reason has not been shown to be God's actual reason.
4. Some actual horrors seem vastly disproportionate to the proposed good.
5. If God is unable to sin yet acts freely, inability to sin may not eliminate
   every important kind of freedom.
6. Telling a newly bereaved person that logical compatibility has been proved
   fails to attend to grief.
7. An allegedly free choice is also described as causally determined in the
   same respect.

For each item, state whether it defeats the consistency model, pressures a
theodicy, or redirects the conversation.

## 5. Closed-notes retrieval and correction — 15 minutes

Close all sources for eight minutes and define:

1. direct contradiction;
2. indirect inconsistency;
3. bridge principle;
4. validity;
5. soundness;
6. morally sufficient reason;
7. defense; and
8. theodicy.

Use the remaining seven minutes to correct the definitions in a different
color or under a **Correction** label. Record the source or lecture section for
each correction. Conclude:

> The logical problem asks ____. A consistency defense establishes at most
> ____. It does not establish ____.
