# Mackie's Challenge and Theistic Replies

## Learning objectives

After completing this lecture, the student should be able to:

- explain why Mackie's argument is an internal critique of classical theism;
- distinguish Mackie's adequate solutions from replies he considers internally
  unstable;
- reconstruct his objections to counterpart, means-end, higher-order-good, and
  free-will replies;
- distinguish premise revision, a consistency defense, and a theodicy;
- explain Beebe's account of how a free-will consistency model answers the
  strict logical problem; and
- identify the natural-evil, heaven, divine-freedom, evidential, and pastoral
  questions left open.

## Key terms

**Internal critique:** an objection that provisionally grants a position's own
claims in order to test whether they cohere. **Adequate solution:** Mackie's
term for a reply that removes the contradiction by genuinely rejecting or
qualifying at least one premise. **Internally unstable solution:** this
lecture's descriptive label for what Mackie calls a fallacious solution—a reply
that verbally retains all the original claims while its explanation
substantively abandons one. **First-order good:** an ordinary good such as
pleasure or safety. **Higher-order good:** a good involving responses to other
goods or evils, such as courage in danger. **Morally significant freedom:**
freedom under which actions can properly receive moral praise or blame.
**Moral evil:** wrong or suffering arising from culpable creaturely action.
**Natural evil:** suffering arising from natural processes rather than directly
from a creature's culpable choice. **Consistency model:** a possible state of
affairs in which the challenged claims are true together.

## Lecture

### 1. Mackie's target is a set of beliefs

Mackie does not need to begin by establishing atheism from premises every
reader already accepts. He grants, for the sake of argument, central claims of
traditional theism and asks whether they can be held together with the reality
of evil. His charge is internal: if the theist accepts divine omnipotence,
perfect goodness, and real evil, does the theist also accept principles that
make those claims inconsistent?[^1]

This method deserves a careful answer. Responding “Mackie does not believe in
God” would miss the argument. An internal critic may expose a contradiction
without sharing the challenged worldview. Likewise, citing a Bible verse as an
authority Mackie rejects would not by itself repair the logic. The Christian
must show how the propositions cohere, then may explain the wider reasons for
believing them.

Mackie's article is also more than the familiar three-line puzzle. He examines
several proposed solutions and asks whether each genuinely changes the premises
or preserves them only by shifting the meanings of *good*, *evil*, *power*, or
*freedom*. His recurring demand is semantic and logical stability: do the words
mean the same thing at the end of the reply that they meant at the beginning?

**Primary-source claim:** Mackie distinguishes solutions that openly abandon or
qualify a member of the inconsistent set from solutions that appear to retain
the set but covertly surrender part of it. His label for the second group is
“fallacious solutions.”[^1] This lecture uses *internally unstable* to identify
the specific defect he alleges without treating every defender as careless or
dishonest.

### 2. Adequate solutions: logical success with theological cost

Once the full argument includes bridge principles, one can remove the
contradiction by rejecting something. Mackie's examples include denying that
God is wholly good, limiting divine power, denying the reality of evil, or
qualifying the principle that goodness always eliminates evil whenever power
permits.[^2]

These are logically adequate in a minimal sense: change a necessary premise and
the original derivation no longer goes through. They are not therefore equally
plausible, religiously acceptable, or well supported. A view on which God is
finite may avoid the contradiction but cease to be classical theism. A view on
which evil is merely an illusion must still explain whether the harmful
illusion is itself evil. A privation account does not escape by wordplay if it
continues to affirm real deprivation; then the argument can be restated using
*harm* or *wrong* rather than treating evil as a substance.

This gives a general lesson in argument analysis:

1. Identify exactly which premise a reply rejects or qualifies.
2. State the new premise without using the old term ambiguously.
3. Test whether the revised view still represents the position it claims to
   defend.
4. Assess the evidence for the revision separately from its logical effect.

An adequate logical solution can carry a high doctrinal cost. Conversely, a
reply may retain classical doctrine while rejecting an overstrong bridge
principle that was never part of the doctrine. The evaluation depends on what
perfect goodness and omnipotence actually entail.

### 3. When verbal preservation hides substantive revision

Mackie directs much of the article against replies that say all three central
claims remain unchanged. His question is whether the explanation nevertheless
denies one of the connections required for the contradiction.

Suppose someone says:

- God is wholly good.
- Good always eliminates evil as far as it can.
- God is omnipotent.
- Evil is necessary for a greater good.

If the greater good is literally impossible without the evil, then either
divine power is limited by that necessary relation or perfect goodness does not
eliminate every evil it can eliminate. One of the original bridge principles
has been qualified. That may be the correct move, but it should be acknowledged
rather than concealed.

This demand cuts both ways. A critic cannot define goodness as “prevents every
evil without exception” and then announce that theism is contradictory if
traditional theism never accepted that definition. Nor can a defender call
every unknown permission “good” and thereby remove the data that generated the
problem. Both parties must state the moral principle that connects goodness to
prevention.

### 4. “Good cannot exist without evil”

One proposed reply treats good and evil as logical counterparts. Perhaps we
could not recognize or describe good without contrast. Mackie answers that a
need for conceptual contrast does not establish a need for actual evil. People
may understand a term's negation without every corresponding condition being
instantiated. A world can contain a quality even if its inhabitants never
encounter the opposite.

There are at least three different theses here:

1. We learn the word *good* by contrast with *evil*.
2. The concept of good logically includes the concept of not-good.
3. Good things cannot exist unless evil things also exist.

The first is a psychological claim, the second a conceptual claim, and the
third a metaphysical necessity. The first two do not entail the third. Even if
contrast helps human recognition, an omniscient God would not need actual
cruelty in order to recognize love.

**Contested interpretation:** A richer counterpart theory might argue that
certain goods—rescue, forgiveness, perseverance—require danger, wrong, or
difficulty. That is not the claim that *all good as such* requires evil. It is a
higher-order-good claim and must be evaluated good by good.

### 5. Evil as a means to good

A second proposal says some evil is necessary as a means to good. Pain may
alert a creature to injury; costly sacrifice may make possible a rescue; a
stable natural process may permit both ordinary goods and occasional harm.

Mackie's pressure is sharp. If God can obtain a good only through an evil means,
then there appears to be some limit on divine power. If no limit exists, why
not produce the good directly? If the relation between means and end is
logically necessary, the defender has revised the claim that omnipotence has no
limits at all. Again, the revision may be coherent: many theists deny that
omnipotence includes making a contradiction true. But that is an explicit
qualification of the bridge principle.[^2]

The reply also needs a moral constraint. It is not enough that total good
outweigh total harm numerically. Persons cannot casually be used as raw
material for someone else's virtue. A proposed greater good must explain:

- why the good cannot be achieved without permitting the evil;
- why the evil is proportionate to that good;
- how the good relates to the person who suffers;
- why an equal or worse evil is not introduced; and
- why permitting the evil is consistent with justice and love.

Those requirements exceed the minimum logical task. They matter when a defense
is promoted into an actual theodicy.

### 6. Higher-order goods—and higher-order evils

A related proposal ranks goods by order. Compassion responds to suffering,
courage responds to danger, forgiveness responds to wrongdoing, and just
resistance responds to oppression. A world containing such responses may have
values unavailable in a world with only uncomplicated pleasure.

Mackie argues that this move does not automatically solve the problem. If
second-order goods arise as responses to first-order evils, second-order evils
also arise: cruelty about suffering, cowardice in danger, delight in injustice,
or refusal to forgive. A complete account cannot count only the virtues made
possible by adversity while ignoring the vices that accompany it.[^1]

The defender can reply that morally significant agency makes both responses
possible and that a world with responsible love has distinctive value. Yet the
questions recur. Could there be less severe evil while retaining those goods?
Could agents often choose rightly? Why are some people crushed rather than
formed by suffering? Does eschatological healing defeat rather than merely
outweigh a person's loss?

**Curriculum synthesis:** A higher-order-good model may be logically possible.
Its possibility does not license the claim that a known sufferer's pain was
sent to improve someone else's character. Moving from a world-level model to a
case-specific explanation requires evidence the student does not possess.

### 7. Mackie's pressure on the free-will reply

The free-will reply says moral evil originates in created agents rather than in
God and that freedom is a great good. Mackie grants the importance of freedom
for the sake of analysis but asks why God could not create persons who freely
choose rightly on every occasion. If it is logically possible that a person
freely chooses good in one instance, why could every person not do so in every
instance?

He also exposes a dilemma about created agency. If God determines every feature
of persons and their actions, blaming freedom may not remove divine
responsibility. If free choices are independent enough to escape determination,
then divine power does not include determining those choices while preserving
their freedom. The reply must state which notion of freedom it uses and which
worlds God can actualize.[^1]

Mackie's article further develops a paradox of omnipotence: can an omnipotent
being make agents or systems it can no longer control? An affirmative answer
seems to permit self-limitation; a negative answer seems to identify something
the being cannot do. The paradox presses an unrestricted concept of power. A
classical reply distinguishes genuine tasks from contradictory descriptions
and distinguishes voluntary nonexercise of power from loss of power.

These issues cannot be solved by the sentence “free will explains evil.” One
must define freedom, show the relevant relation to moral value, explain why
divine determination would defeat that freedom, and distinguish moral from
natural evil. Week 5 will study the developed free-will defense rather than
assuming its success here.

### 8. Defense is not theodicy

A *theodicy* claims, more or less ambitiously, to describe God's actual reasons
for permitting evil. A *defense* has a more limited aim: show that the critic's
argument does not establish its conclusion. Against the strict logical problem,
a defense can succeed by giving a possible consistency model.

The distinction changes the burden of proof. A theodicy must provide reason to
think its explanation is true and morally adequate. A defense need only show
that its model is possible and that the challenged propositions can all be true
within it. Thus:

1. God is omnipotent, omniscient, and perfectly good.
2. God creates persons with morally significant freedom.
3. It is possible that God cannot causally determine every significantly free
   choice while leaving it significantly free.
4. Such persons sometimes choose moral evil.
5. God has a morally sufficient reason to permit the possibility of moral evil
   for the good of significantly free agency.
6. Therefore God and moral evil can coexist without contradiction.

This map does not claim that every line describes the actual reason for the
actual world. It supplies a candidate possible circumstance. To defeat the
logical problem, the circumstance must be coherent; to become a theodicy, it
would need much more.

### 9. Beebe's account of the consistency-model reply

Beebe presents the free-will defense as the most discussed contemporary answer
to the logical problem. Morally significant freedom includes the ability to
perform or refrain from morally assessable action without causal determination
of the choice. On this definition, “God causes an agent freely to choose only
the good” combines conditions that cannot obtain together. The limitation is
logical, not a stronger power overcoming God.[^3]

The reply does not say that a world of free persons who always choose well is
itself contradictory. The sharper issue is whether God can *guarantee* that
result by causally determining significantly free choices. A description in
which creatures contingently choose well is different from a description in
which God determines every choice while those choices remain undetermined in
the relevant sense.

**Broad scholarly consensus:** Beebe reports that the free-will defense is
widely taken to rebut the classical logical inconsistency charge. He also
records dissatisfaction with treating that formal result as a complete answer.
The defense may establish possible coexistence while providing little evidence
about God's actual reasons or the full range of suffering.[^4]

This is a modest but significant result. Mackie's own burden was strong: no
possible world may contain both the God of classical theism and evil. If the
free-will model is possible, that universal claim fails. The critic may then
reformulate the challenge evidentially rather than insist on a contradiction.

### 10. Natural evil, heaven, and divine freedom

Several objections remain.

**Natural evil:** Human choices do not directly cause every disease, natural
disaster, congenital condition, or instance of animal suffering. A defense
focused on human freedom therefore does not automatically cover all evil.
Beebe reviews an attempt to connect natural evil to a broader fall account but
also notes that mere possibility and theological plausibility are different
questions.[^5] Week 6 treats natural evil directly.

**Heaven:** Christian hope includes a state in which redeemed persons no longer
sin. If freedom is valuable, why could God not create that condition from the
beginning? Possible replies distinguish formed from unformed character,
freedom's history from its perfected end, and the freedom to act from the power
to sin. Each requires development. The objection does not by itself recreate a
formal contradiction, but it pressures an oversimplified definition of freedom.

**Divine freedom:** God is perfectly good and does not sin. If inability to sin
eliminates meaningful freedom, it becomes difficult to call divine action both
free and praiseworthy. A defender may distinguish creaturely deliberation among
moral alternatives from the perfect freedom of acting according to a good
nature. Again, the terms must remain stable.

**Evidential force:** Even if freedom can justify some permission, why this
amount, severity, and distribution of evil? Could God prevent particular
atrocities without turning agents into automata? These are not negligible
questions. They challenge the explanatory adequacy and probability of the
model rather than the bare possibility of some coexistence.

### 11. What has been settled?

A careful conclusion needs four lines.

1. Mackie correctly forces the theist to state the relations among goodness,
   power, knowledge, and permission rather than recite attributes separately.
2. The initial theistic claims and the existence of evil do not form a direct
   contradiction; the universal prevention principle is contestable.
3. A coherent morally sufficient reason, especially one involving morally
   significant freedom, can provide a countermodel to strict inconsistency.
4. The countermodel does not show that the reason is actual, explain natural
   evil, justify every permission, or answer the evidential and existential
   problems.

Calling the logical problem “answered” is appropriate only if the object is the
specific universal inconsistency claim. Calling the entire problem of evil
“solved” would conceal the questions the argument has not touched.

Mackie's critique also produces a lasting discipline for Christian thought.
The defender must not shift between unlimited power and logically coherent
power, between preventing every evil and opposing evil wisely, or between a
possible reason and an actual revealed reason. Precision is not a retreat from
faith; it is part of truthfulness.

### 12. Biblical-confessional and pastoral evaluation

The course's confessional framework retains God's perfect goodness, knowledge,
and power and the reality of evil. Genesis 1–3 locates the created order under
divine goodness and moral rebellion within creaturely action. James 1 denies
that God is morally enticed by evil or entices persons to it. Job and the lament
psalms authorize protest and resist the claim that the sufferer can read God's
specific reason from circumstances. Romans 8 and Revelation 21–22 frame hope
as liberation, judgment, and renewal rather than as the declaration that evil
is harmless.[^6]

**Confessional conclusion:** A morally qualified account of goodness and a
logically coherent account of omnipotence allow the project to affirm God and
real evil without contradiction. Morally significant freedom supplies one
possible part of that account, but it is not the whole Christian doctrine of
providence or a complete theodicy.

**Curriculum synthesis:** The formal defense must never be aimed at a suffering
person as though grief were a logical mistake. In immediate care, the first
questions concern safety, presence, truthful lament, practical help, justice,
and qualified referral. Philosophical analysis becomes appropriate when the
person wants it and when the context can bear it. If an agent can prevent abuse
or protect someone in danger, divine permission is not an excuse for human
inaction.

The result is deliberately restrained: Mackie's strongest logical charge has a
serious theistic answer. Mackie's moral pressure remains valuable, and the
remaining problems are not rhetorical debris. They set the agenda for the next
stages of the course.

## Primary-source engagement

The lecture follows Mackie's assigned chapter through his distinction between
adequate and fallacious solutions and his criticisms of counterpart,
means-end, higher-order-good, and free-will replies. Beebe's sections 4–10
control the presentation of morally significant freedom, natural evil, the
scope of a consistency defense, and remaining objections. No extended text
from either source is reproduced.

## Major scholarly positions

- **Mackie's internal critique:** a theist must either revise a premise or show
  that the bridge principles do not yield the alleged contradiction. Replies
  are unstable when they retain words while changing their operative meanings.
- **Attribute or evil revision:** limiting divine power or goodness, or denying
  evil, can remove the contradiction but may carry high philosophical and
  theological costs.
- **Greater-good and higher-order-good replies:** some goods may be inseparable
  from permitted risks or harms. Critics press necessity, proportion,
  person-centered justice, and accompanying higher-order evils.
- **Free-will consistency defense:** it is possible that significantly free
  agency cannot be causally determined to choose only good. Critics press
  natural evil, heavenly freedom, divine freedom, and actual-world evidence.
- **Evidential continuation:** logical compatibility leaves open whether evil
  lowers the probability of theism or whether any actual theodicy succeeds.

## Confessional analysis

The curriculum affirms classical divine attributes, created goodness,
creaturely responsibility, providence, judgment, resurrection, and new
creation. It receives the consistency defense as successful against the strict
logical charge while refusing to identify a candidate reason as God's revealed
reason for a particular evil. Mackie and Beebe are represented before
evaluation. These judgments are independent and are not official Wheaton
College teaching.

## Discussion and retrieval questions

1. What makes Mackie's argument an internal critique?
2. What is an adequate solution in Mackie's classification, and why might one
   be theologically costly?
3. Distinguish conceptual contrast between good and evil from the actual
   existence of both.
4. Why does an evil-as-means proposal qualify a claim about omnipotence?
5. What higher-order evils pressure a higher-order-good reply?
6. Reconstruct Mackie's question about why free agents could not always choose
   well.
7. What does the free-will consistency model need to show, and what does it not
   need to show?
8. Which objection—natural evil, heaven, divine freedom, or evidential
   distribution—most directly pressures the model? Classify its target.
9. Why is a successful formal defense not an appropriate complete response to
   a newly bereaved person?

## Summary

Mackie's internal critique tests whether theism preserves stable meanings while
answering an alleged contradiction. Open premise revision can be logically
adequate even when it abandons classical doctrine. Counterpart, means-end,
higher-order-good, and free-will replies require explicit qualifications and
cannot simply rename evil. A free-will consistency model can show a possible
world containing both perfect God and moral evil if causally determining a
choice is incompatible with its being significantly free. That defeats the
strict universal inconsistency claim but leaves natural evil, heavenly and
divine freedom, actual justification, evidential force, and pastoral response
open.
